# Understanding ancestral nationalism

## **What is ancestral nationalism?**

Ancestral nationalism is a form of nationalism based on shared ancestry or lineage. Membership in the nation is understood as something a person is born into, rather than something they can fully acquire through citizenship, cultural adoption or political loyalty.

In this view, the nation is closely connected to a people with a shared bloodline, historical origin or inherited identity. Ancestral nationalism often treats the nation as an extended kinship group, where descent is central to belonging.



## **Goal of ancestral nationalism**

The goal of ancestral nationalism is to protect the interests, identity and continuity of the ancestral group of that nation.

Ancestral nationalism can help to preserve the historical population and its inherited identity, culture and traditions. The state would prioritise the interests of the ancestral population. Ancestral nationalism can exclude people who do not shared the required ancestry, even if they are culturally integrated, legally loyal or born in the country.



## Key features of ancestral nationalism

* **Exclusive in principle** — Membership in the nation is limited to people who share the required ancestral ties with the national group. Unlike civic nationalism, where membership is based on citizenship and political loyalty, or cultural nationalism, where outsiders may join through assimilation, ancestral nationalism treats national belonging as inherited. People without the required ancestry may be allowed to live, work or hold legal residence in the country, but they may not be recognised as full members of the nation.
* **Strong emphasis on continuity** — Ancestral nationalism prioritises the preservation of the long-established ancestral population and its inherited identity, culture and traditions. Because membership is based on descent, the nation’s identity is usually seen as more fixed and less open to change. This can create a stronger sense of historical continuity for the ancestral group, but it also makes the national identity less accessible to outsiders.



## **Citizenship**

Citizenship is tied to ancestry, meaning people are considered members of the nation by birth or descent.

Sometimes this could mean anyone who has any amount of shared ancestry, while in other cases it might mean having a minimum amount of shared ancestry to become a citizen. Some forms may allow partial membership or limited rights for people without the required ancestry, while stricter forms may deny full national membership to outsiders entirely.



## **Voting power**

Voting power may be given to all citizens, or only to a subset of citizens who meet a certain threshold of shared ancestry. This threshold might be used to better protect the interests of the long-established ancestral population.



## **Representation**

Representation may be open to all citizens, or only to a subset of citizens who meet a certain threshold of shared ancestry. This threshold might be used to better protect the interests of the long-established ancestral population.
