# Comparing continuity, inclusion, freedom and political equality

The principal difference between civic, cultural and ancestral nationalism concerns the basis of national membership. That difference affects how each model approaches historical continuity, political equality and the inclusion of outsiders.



## **Civic nationalism and civic-state continuity**

Civic nationalism makes citizenship and political membership the main basis of the nation. Its institutions can persist as people from different backgrounds become citizens, provided those citizens continue to support the political order.

However, distinct populations have not all created the same institutions or held the same political values. It is therefore an assumption that people from different backgrounds will sustain the same civic institutions to the same standard or with the same willingness to follow inherited laws and practices. Demographic change can influence institutional preferences even where the formal civic structure remains.



## **Cultural nationalism and civic-state continuity**

Cultural nationalism is generally more selective because newcomers are expected to demonstrate assimilation. Greater cultural and political alignment may help protect civic institutions.

However, cultural knowledge or participation does not guarantee enduring commitment to the existing political order. New members may still seek institutional changes as their preferences or political influence develop.



## **Ancestral nationalism and civic-state continuity**

Ancestral nationalism may preserve civic institutions more reliably by maintaining the population that established, inherited and developed them. Its membership boundary is more selective because it is connected to descent from the historical population.

Nevertheless, ancestral continuity does not guarantee stable political beliefs. The historical population can itself change its institutions, values or priorities.



## **Civic nationalism and cultural continuity**

Civic nationalism can support an official language, shared history, national symbols, public culture and cultural institutions. However, it generally permits citizens from different ancestral and cultural backgrounds to belong on equal terms. Their traditions may therefore become increasingly prominent or eventually predominant.

Where civic membership is prioritised over cultural preservation, the historical culture may receive less protection if new majorities do not place the same value on maintaining it. Even where migrants want to adopt the host culture, questions remain about whether they can fully replicate or assimilate into it.



## **Cultural nationalism and cultural continuity**

Cultural nationalism directly seeks to preserve language, customs, symbols, traditions and social norms. By linking membership to cultural adaptation, it can maintain a stronger connection with the identity that historically shaped the nation. This can support continuity in:

* Historical memory.
* Social rituals.
* Public holidays.
* Shared references.
* Everyday ways of life.

It is therefore presented as more effective than civic nationalism at cultural preservation. However, its effectiveness remains limited. Assimilation is not always complete or one-directional. Newcomers may reshape the host culture while adapting to it. Substantial migration can alter language, religion, family patterns, values and public expectations even where assimilation is formally required.



## **Ancestral nationalism and cultural continuity**

Ancestral nationalism anchors the nation in a continuing historical population. Because that population created and transmitted the culture, preserving it can provide the strongest basis for maintaining inherited traditions, language, symbols and memory.

The model does not depend on outsiders successfully assimilating as a route to full membership. However, culture must still be taught, practised and transmitted. Ancestral continuity alone cannot ensure that future generations retain the same cultural preferences.



## **Civic nationalism and ancestral continuity**

Civic nationalism does not require descent from the historical population. It therefore provides no inherent guarantee that the ancestral population will retain its historical demographic position.

Naturalisation can produce growing communities whose lineage differs from that of the historical population. Over time, the original population may lose demographic dominance, weakening continuity with the ancestry and inherited identity of those who built the nation-state. This is a disadvantage where preserving that population is a central objective.



## **Cultural nationalism and ancestral continuity**

Cultural membership can be acquired by people from different ancestral backgrounds. Even successful assimilation therefore does not preserve the historical population’s lineage.

Cultural requirements may slow demographic change by making membership more demanding, but they do not prevent it. For those who regard ancestry as essential to nationhood, cultural nationalism remains insufficient.



## **Ancestral nationalism and population preservation**

Ancestral nationalism is the most direct of the three models for preserving the historical population because citizenship is linked to descent. It reduces the extent to which naturalisation changes the core national membership.

For those who regard ancestry as essential, this provides the strongest protection for the original people of the nation-state. However, the resident population can still change through migration, emigration and intermarriage. Descent-based citizenship does not eliminate every demographic change within the territory.



## **Civic membership and political equality**

Civic nationalism treats citizens as equal members regardless of ancestry. This can be particularly suitable for mixed societies where no single ancestral or cultural identity dominates. It provides a shared political identity without requiring agreement about a common lineage.

Its openness also means that the historical population does not retain an exclusive right to determine the nation’s future. As new citizens gain influence, political authority increasingly reflects the whole electorate.



## **Cultural membership and political equality**

Cultural nationalism can also provide equal political rights once someone becomes a citizen. The main distinction concerns the conditions for entry. This can combine political equality with a shared public culture, but it makes the assessment of cultural membership especially important.

People may satisfy formal requirements while remaining socially separate. Others may be integrated in practice but still be regarded as outsiders.



## **Ancestral membership and unequal political status**

Ancestral nationalism may distinguish between legal citizenship and political membership. People with limited ancestral ties may receive legal belonging but not the same national voting or representation rights. This can protect ancestral self-determination while producing unequal citizenship.

Even where legal protections exist, unequal political influence can create resentment, instability and legitimacy problems. The boundary proposals and safeguards are discussed in **Descent-based membership: boundaries and contested cases**.



## **Self-determination**

Under civic nationalism, new citizens become equal participants in deciding the country’s future. As the electorate changes, the historical population may find it harder to preserve its preferred way of life or reverse earlier migration decisions.

Cultural nationalism can produce a similar outcome because assimilated newcomers also receive political influence. Even substantial cultural adaptation does not eliminate disagreements about whose preferences should guide the nation.

Ancestral nationalism is designed to retain political authority primarily within the historical population. For supporters, this is a central advantage: the nation’s laws and institutions remain more directly connected to that population’s interests and priorities.



## **Emotional attachment**

A nation defined mainly through political values may struggle to generate the same depth of attachment as one grounded in shared history, culture or ancestry.

Civic identity can appear procedural or abstract.

Cultural nationalism adds common references, traditions and a shared way of life.

Ancestral nationalism can create a particularly strong connection between people, homeland, ancestors and descendants. That connection may strengthen willingness to defend the country, preserve historical sites and maintain collective memory.



## **Solidarity and cultural cohesion**

Where cultural assimilation is achievable, cultural nationalism can reinforce trust, belonging and mutual obligation. It may support cooperation, civic duty, public participation and collective sacrifice. However, the more complex the culture, the harder complete assimilation becomes.

Civic nationalism may be more vulnerable to parallel societies where people share legal membership but remain socially separate.

Ancestral nationalism reduces reliance on assimilation as a path to citizenship, although resident communities can still create social or cultural tensions.



## **Cultural stagnation and personal freedom**

Preserving culture raises questions about who defines it and how much change is acceptable. If standards become rigid, preservation can turn into cultural gatekeeping, nostalgia politics, resistance to beneficial adaptation and disputes over authenticity. Newcomers may feel pressured to suppress parts of their background.

Members of the historical population may also disagree with the dominant cultural expectations. Collective continuity therefore creates tensions with individual freedom, internal dissent and changing identities.



## **Intelligence, inherited characteristics and dysgenic concerns**

Differences in general intelligence and behaviours between populations can be highly relevant to long-term outcomes. Some differences may be reduced through environmental improvements, including nutrition and education.

However, differences can persist even in prosperous and diverse societies where groups live in similar environments. Under civic or cultural nationalism, populations with lower average IQ could migrate and obtain citizenship.

Intermixing with the historical population could produce an intellectual dysgenic trend. Even without intermixing, growth in a lower-average-IQ population could reduce the national average.

This could negatively affect cohesion, crime and economic prosperity. From the perspective of ancestral nationalism, restricting membership can help preserve inherited characteristics of the historical population, including physical traits, intelligence and behaviour.

It is therefore seen as a more effective solution for reducing uncertainty about the long-term effects of population replacement or intermixing.



## **Diversity within states and between nations**

Civic and cultural nationalism can increase diversity within a state by admitting people from different backgrounds. However, if historical identities and cultures are eventually replaced, the diversity between distinct nations could decline.

Where political power follows changing demographics, groups that migrate or reproduce in greater numbers gain influence over institutions and public culture.

Ancestral nationalism is presented as a way to protect global diversity by preserving each historical population in its homeland. Distinct lineages, languages, identities and cultures would then be less likely to disappear through large-scale migration.



## **Intergenerational orientation**

Ancestral nationalism’s focus on descent naturally emphasises future generations. It may give greater priority to:

* Family formation.
* Birth rates.
* Inheritance.
* Land continuity.
* Cultural transmission.
* Long-term national survival.

Its political horizon is therefore connected to both ancestors and descendants rather than only the present population.



## **Other people-continuity models**

The broader terminology includes approaches beyond the three principal models. Their preservation effects differ according to how they define the protected population.



**Nativist nationalism**

In a mild territorial form, nativism may treat people born and raised in the country as native, including children of immigrants. This can coexist with _jus soli_, although birthright citizenship is not inherently nativist.

Stricter versions may require several generations of residence or at least a century of roots. The strictest versions approach ancestral, blood or blood-and-soil nationalism. Nativism can adopt cultural-preservation policies while slowing population change, but its flexibility makes it less precise about exactly what is preserved.



**Racial nationalism**

Racial identity does not ensure common institutions or political values. A broad racial category may include populations with different languages, religions and histories.

Cultural policies could make racial nationalism moderately effective at preserving culture, but it would not be the most reliable method. Its principal effect is preserving a broad racial grouping rather than each distinct ancestral population. A narrower focus on particular historical groups would better preserve their individual diversity.



**Blood nationalism**

Blood nationalism directly preserves a biological descent group. This may support institutional continuity, but shared blood does not guarantee shared politics.

It also does not guarantee cultural continuity because culture must be learned and transmitted. People of the same bloodline may adopt other cultures, especially when raised abroad.

Where biological descent is the principal concern, blood nationalism provides one of the strictest and potentially most reliable membership boundaries. However, it may exclude historically rooted people who do not share the central bloodline.



**Ancestral nationalism**

Ancestral nationalism can include more than one historically rooted population. Its broadest interpretation may recognise groups that have participated in the nation’s formation or development over centuries without sharing the majority’s bloodline. This can reduce permanent exclusion and statelessness compared with strict blood nationalism.



**Blood-and-soil nationalism**

Blood-and-soil nationalism combines descent with rootedness in the homeland. This is presented as potentially providing the highest likelihood of civic-state and cultural continuity because it requires both historical ancestry and formative connection to the national community.

Nevertheless, neither condition guarantees support for existing institutions. Its cultural emphasis comes from preserving a people situated in a particular homeland, with exposure to its traditions and institutions. It can exclude immigrants without the required ancestry and diaspora members who have the ancestry but lack a formative homeland connection.
