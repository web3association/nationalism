# Comparing feasibility, costs, resilience and unintended consequences

Membership principles need institutions capable of applying them. The practical comparison therefore concerns administration, testing, political incentives, demographic control, economic effects and transition risks.



## **Administrative simplicity under civic nationalism**

Civic nationalism is one of the simpler models to establish because membership mainly requires agreement about political values, institutions and responsibilities. It does not require verifying ancestry or determining whether a person has fully assimilated culturally.

Citizenship tests can focus on civic knowledge, language, institutions and legal expectations. This makes the process comparatively straightforward to standardise.



## **Civic citizenship costs**

A civic citizenship process should generally be relatively inexpensive to establish and operate. Knowledge tests commonly use clear criteria that do not change frequently. This allows predictable administration and streamlined assessment. However, simplicity does not mean that the test reliably establishes enduring commitment or integration.



## **Cultural citizenship costs**

Cultural nationalism generally requires more assessment than civic nationalism. The process may consider:

* Language.
* Historical knowledge.
* Customs.
* Social norms.
* Cultural participation.
* Acceptance of the political framework.

This requires more judgement but can still be standardised to a degree. It is generally presented as less administratively demanding than ancestry verification.



## **The difficulty of cultural assessment**

Assimilation is difficult to define, measure and enforce. Language fluency, behaviour, values and identity do not always align. A person may pass formal requirements while remaining socially separate. Another may participate deeply while still being considered insufficiently assimilated. This creates risks of:

* Arbitrary decisions.
* Inconsistent standards.
* Discrimination.
* Psychological pressure.
* Administrative disputes.

The problem does not necessarily disappear after citizenship is granted.



## **Cultural enforcement**

Preservation requires continuing decisions about which practices count as acceptable. Attempts to verify daily behaviour can become intrusive. A system that tries to enforce detailed cultural conformity may require surveillance and excessive control. This limits how reliably cultural nationalism can preserve every aspect of national life. The testing mechanisms are discussed in **Naturalisation and civic or cultural citizenship tests**.



## **Ancestral verification costs**

Ancestral nationalism can require a substantial initial administrative effort. The system needs to define the population, establish evidence standards and resolve uncertain cases. Questions arise around:

* Mixed ancestry.
* Disputed lineage.
* Historical migration.
* Missing records.
* Reference populations.
* Appeals.
* Privacy.
* Changes in evidence.

The more precise the boundary becomes, the fewer unresolved edge cases may remain, but compromises are still required. The technical methods and costs are addressed in **Evidence and technical reference**.



## **Long-term ancestry-based administration**

Once descent rules and verification arrangements are established, citizenship may become relatively simple to administer. The state does not need to repeatedly assess subjective cultural assimilation or ideological commitment. Inheritance rules can be more stable and predictable across generations. This can be presented as a long-term advantage despite the initial complexity.



## **Strategic naturalisation**

If civic citizenship is relatively easy to acquire and provides substantial benefits, people may seek it for instrumental reasons. Possible motivations include access to:

* Welfare.
* Passports.
* Labour markets.
* Property.
* Political influence.
* Security protections.

Naturalisation may therefore occur without deep identification or loyalty.



## **Migration and demographic control**

Civic and cultural nationalism can influence immigration through border policy. However, they cannot easily control emigration, fertility, mortality, intermarriage or identity change.

Once citizenship has been granted, changing the demographic direction becomes more difficult. New citizens gain rights and political influence and may oppose policies that reduce their status or interests. Cultural requirements may slow change but do not eliminate this problem.



## **Ancestral membership and resident population change**

Ancestral nationalism separates national membership from residence. External groups may live in the country on visas without automatically joining the electorate. This retains greater control over citizenship and national political power. However, it does not prevent the resident population from changing through migration, births, deaths, emigration or intermarriage.



## **Reversing migration-related changes**

Because non-members do not automatically receive citizenship, ancestral nationalism can make migration-related changes easier to reverse at the level of membership and voting power.

Where social or cultural tensions arise, the state may respond through visa or residency rules rather than confront the difficulty of revoking citizenship.

This also reduces reliance on large-scale assimilation as a condition of permanent national membership. The proposals and transition concerns are discussed in **Existing citizenship, denaturalisation and security of residence**.



## **Government representation risks**

Under civic nationalism, elected governments can substantially alter immigration and naturalisation policy. A government may justify a major change through selective economic or social arguments, producing lasting demographic consequences.

Stronger direct public influence could reduce this risk, but would increase governance complexity.

Under cultural nationalism, governments also determine what counts as sufficient assimilation. Cultural standards may be manipulated, applied inconsistently or changed for political advantage.

Ancestral nationalism narrows the scope for governments to transform the electorate quickly through naturalisation. This is presented as reducing the risk of short-term political actors reshaping national membership in ways the historical population considers illegitimate.



## **Unequal international outcomes**

Migration is unlikely to be distributed evenly if civic or cultural nationalism is adopted widely. Some states may receive large inflows while others remain relatively homogeneous.

As a result, some countries retain stronger cultural and population continuity while others face repeated demographic transformation and integration pressures. The same formal membership model can therefore produce unequal outcomes internationally.



## **Economic openness and skills**

Civic nationalism can be useful where attracting workers, investment and talent is a major priority. Its more accessible membership may make it easier to retain people who want long-term security.

Cultural nationalism can also admit outsiders, although the adaptation requirements may make entry more demanding.

Ancestral nationalism may face disadvantages in attracting skilled workers, students and investors where citizenship is unavailable. In countries facing ageing populations or strong economic competition, this can become a structural cost.



## **External influence**

People joining a nation may retain family, economic, emotional or political connections abroad. Such ties are often manageable but can become significant during diplomatic conflict or security crises. Cultural assimilation does not necessarily remove them.

Ancestral nationalism seeks to reduce their direct political influence by limiting national membership and voting power. Non-citizen residents may still contribute economically without shaping the long-term direction of the state through national elections.

However, narrower membership does not remove the need for security vetting, disclosure and conflict-of-interest rules.



## **Resilience in crises**

A society that functions during stable periods may become more divided during economic collapse, social unrest, war, diplomatic confrontation and rapid demographic change. Differences in loyalty, trust, mutual support and willingness to defend the country may become more important.

Civic nationalism may face difficulties if its shared identity is too thin.

Cultural nationalism may appear cohesive until a crisis exposes incomplete assimilation.

Ancestral nationalism may benefit from shared identity and attachment, although common descent does not guarantee political agreement or effective institutions.



## **Transition costs**

Moving from civic or cultural nationalism to ancestral nationalism can be especially disruptive. The state may need to:

* Redefine membership.
* Change citizenship law.
* Redesign immigration policy.
* Determine the position of existing citizens.
* Establish verification systems.
* Handle domestic opposition.
* Address international reactions.

Such a transition could produce instability, legal disputes and a legitimacy crisis. Gradual implementation may reduce disruption but does not eliminate the complexity.



## **International legitimacy**

Ancestral nationalism is likely to attract stronger criticism because it explicitly connects membership to descent. It may be regarded as discriminatory or incompatible with liberal equality norms. Consequences may include:

* Reputational costs.
* Diplomatic pressure.
* Strained international relationships.
* Opposition from international institutions.

These are practical considerations alongside the domestic objectives of the system.



## **Stability and contested boundaries**

A boundary that changes frequently can make citizenship and political status insecure. A boundary that never changes may struggle to accommodate historical development, intermarriage and newly recognised evidence.

The distinction between correcting evidence and politically expanding membership is therefore important. These questions are developed in **Descent-based membership: boundaries and contested cases**.



## **Overall practical comparison**

Civic nationalism offers comparatively simple administration and flexibility but weaker guarantees of historical continuity.

Cultural nationalism attempts stronger continuity while preserving a route for outsiders, but faces difficult assessment and enforcement problems.

Ancestral nationalism directly protects descent-based membership but requires difficult boundary decisions, substantial transition work and acceptance of economic, legal and diplomatic costs.
