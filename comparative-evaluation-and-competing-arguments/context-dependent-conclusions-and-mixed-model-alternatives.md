# Context-dependent conclusions and mixed-model alternatives

No single nationalist model is presented as equally suitable for every society. The appropriate arrangement depends on historical circumstances, the population’s priorities and the practical consequences of membership rules. The central distinction is between societies prioritising political openness, those prioritising culture and those prioritising ancestral continuity.



## **When civic nationalism is most suitable**

Civic nationalism is most suitable for newly formed, mixed or institution-based states that prioritise:

* Political inclusion.
* Legal equality.
* Administrative simplicity.
* Institutional flexibility.
* A more adaptable national identity.

It is particularly relevant where no historical ancestral population seeks to remain the defining core of the nation. Possible settings include:

* New nation-states.
* Settler societies.
* Highly mixed populations.
* Political unions.
* Societies built around shared institutions rather than inherited culture.



**Civic nationalism and economic openness**

Civic nationalism can be useful where labour mobility, economic integration, investment and international connections are major priorities. It may suit states intended to function as open economic environments.

However, it can still require strict immigration controls if the population wants to slow demographic or cultural change. Where citizenship is relatively easy to acquire, policymakers may need to consider welfare eligibility, free-riding and incentives for strategic naturalisation. Its principal advantage is flexibility rather than reliable preservation of a historical population or fixed culture.



## **When cultural nationalism is most suitable**

Cultural nationalism is most suitable where the population wants a stable common culture but does not regard ancestry as the essential basis of membership. It allows outsiders to join if they adopt the language, customs, public norms and political framework. It therefore suits societies seeking some openness while placing more weight on cultural continuity than a purely civic approach.



**The conditions for cultural preservation**

Cultural nationalism works best where important aspects of culture can be defined with reasonable clarity. Its effectiveness also depends on immigration remaining limited enough for assimilation to be realistic. At higher levels, newcomers may influence the host culture faster than they can be absorbed into it. Language, religion, family life, values and social expectations can change despite formal assimilation requirements.

For this reason, the model is presented as most effective where only a relatively small amount of immigration is accepted. It is less suitable where a state pursues large-scale migration while expecting little change to its historical culture.



## **When ancestral nationalism is most suitable**

Ancestral nationalism is most suitable for historical nation-states prioritising population continuity, self-determination, inherited identity, historical culture, heritage and long-term preservation.

It is particularly relevant where the population regards descent, rather than civic commitment or cultural adaptation alone, as the defining basis of nationhood.



**Conditions for workable ancestral membership**

The historical population needs to be definable in practical terms. This may involve:

* Historical continuity.
* Official records.
* Legal descent rules.
* Other membership evidence.

Clearer boundaries make consistent administration easier. The model is therefore presented as more viable in older states with strong historical identity than in newly formed or highly mixed societies.



**Migration without full membership**

Ancestral nationalism can permit temporary or long-term residence without granting national membership. This may allow labour migration while retaining stronger control over citizenship and political participation. It is attractive to those who want to preserve self-determination and minimise the difficulty of reversing migration-related changes after citizenship has been granted. Its priority is continuity rather than open membership.



## **Why ancestral nationalism is preferred over stricter variants**

Ancestral nationalism is presented as the clearest term for preserving a historical population while retaining more flexibility than strict blood or blood-and-soil nationalism.

It occupies a middle position:

* More descent-focused than civic or cultural nationalism.
* Less biologically strict than blood nationalism.
* Less necessarily tied to formative homeland residence than blood-and-soil nationalism.

It can also incorporate cultural or territorial rootedness where these are considered important.



**Historical complexity**

Real nations do not always correspond to one biological bloodline. Founding populations may contain several groups. Long-settled minorities may have participated in national life for centuries and have no meaningful alternative homeland. Ancestral nationalism can recognise these complexities while retaining a descent-based understanding of the nation.

Blood nationalism is more precise where the objective is a particular biological lineage. Blood-and-soil nationalism is more precise where both descent and a formative connection to the homeland are essential.



**Exclusion and alternative belonging**

Stricter systems raise difficult questions about:

* Mixed ancestry.
* Minority status.
* Existing citizenship.
* Long-settled residents.
* Statelessness.
* Permanent political exclusion.

Blood nationalism may be easier to envisage where those that will be excluded have another national home they can actually join. It is substantially harder where it leaves people without citizenship elsewhere and they are excluded from the only society with which they have meaningful ties.



## **A pragmatic starting point**

For states previously organised on a civic basis, milder nativist or ancestral approaches may be more practical than strict blood nationalism. They could begin addressing demographic change without requiring membership to depend entirely on common blood.

A proposed ancestral approach would allow existing citizens to remain unless the state can establish that they have stronger ancestral ties to another nation. This places the burden on the state rather than requiring every individual to prove inclusion in a narrowly defined biological group.

Under stricter blood or blood-and-soil approaches, that burden could instead fall on each person. Because civic citizenship is widespread, the ancestral approach is presented as more pragmatic for avoiding statelessness during a transition.



## **The overall case for ancestral nationalism**

Within the preservation priorities adopted in this analysis, ancestral nationalism offers a balance between protecting a historical population and accommodating practical complexity. It is therefore preferred as a model intended to preserve distinct populations while remaining more workable than stricter alternatives. This preference does not imply that national adoption will be easy or that every state will choose it.



## **Local alternatives where national change is impractical**

A population seeking stronger continuity protections could pursue:

* Protected cultural communities.
* Local autonomy.
* Devolved authority.
* Regional self-government.
* Recognised minority institutions.

These may protect identity, traditions and demographic continuity without requiring the entire state to change its membership system. They can also provide a practical starting point where national reform lacks support.



## **Combining models within states**

An ancestral nationalist state could permit more civic participation in:

* Special economic zones.
* Charter cities.
* Special administrative regions.
* Autonomous territories.
* New developments.

A civic state could permit stronger ancestral or cultural protections through local communities, regional autonomy or federal arrangements. This allows different forms of membership and participation to coexist.



## **Preserving old societies and creating new ones**

The proposed broader combination is to protect historical populations and cultures while leaving room for new civic or cultural societies. Older nations may place greater value on inherited continuity. New communities may accept a fluid identity from their beginning. The world could therefore preserve existing diversity while creating additional ways for people to live together.



## **Why a single model may be insufficient**

Communities remain uncertain about outcomes until they observe institutions in practice. They may also disagree about values even after outcomes are known.

A system that produces greater prosperity may not satisfy those who prioritise continuity. A system that preserves identity may not satisfy those who prioritise openness.

Local and regional alternatives can allow comparison and voluntary adoption without requiring universal agreement.



## **Pluralism within each model**

The same principle should apply whichever approach becomes dominant. Communities favouring civic openness should retain meaningful room within societies emphasising ancestry or culture. Communities favouring historical continuity should retain meaningful room within civic societies.

Private association and territorial government remain different, however. Where arrangements affect existing residents, essential services or political rights, they need proportionate and justifiable legal authority, accountability and protection for people who disagree.



## **From comparison to political change**

The conclusions above identify the settings in which each model is considered most suitable. They do not settle how an existing society can move from one arrangement to another. Changing citizenship, political participation or territorial authority creates additional questions of consent, constitutional procedure, existing rights, transition costs, international obligations and institutional legitimacy.
