# Liberalism, nationalism and the critiques

Liberalism is one of the dominant political ideologies of the modern world. Its forms include classical, social, egalitarian and neoliberal traditions. Although they differ, they generally share commitments to individual moral worth, legal equality, basic rights and government by consent.



## **Liberalism’s central commitments**

Liberal societies typically emphasise:

* Freedom of speech.
* Freedom of conscience.
* Private property.
* Legal equality.
* Individual autonomy.
* Government by consent.

Liberalism developed partly in opposition to inherited hierarchy, religious conformity and arbitrary power. It sought an order in which people were not defined entirely by tribe, class, ethnicity or religion, but recognised as citizens with equal standing.

This helped delegitimise caste-like systems and establish a universal language of rights. Liberalism has consequently often presented itself as the moral baseline for modern political life rather than merely one ideology among others.



## **Equality does not mean identical characteristics**

Liberal equality does not necessarily assume that people are identical in talent, character, preference or outcomes. Instead, it holds that such differences should not determine basic rights, legal standing or political membership once someone is recognised as a citizen.

Liberal societies therefore tend to oppose formal legal discrimination and emphasise procedural fairness over inherited identity. The ideal is equal participation despite differences in religion, wealth, ancestry or culture.



## **The relationship with civic nationalism**

Civic nationalism is strongly shaped by liberal assumptions. It defines the nation politically: a community of citizens sharing institutions, laws and a common civic project. This aligns with:

* Equal citizenship.
* Legal neutrality.
* Individual rather than inherited status.
* Inclusion across different backgrounds.

Civic nationalism can therefore be understood as nationalism adapted to a liberal age. It attempts to preserve solidarity without grounding membership in ancestry or bloodline.



## **The criticism of abstraction**

A central criticism is that liberalism begins with an abstract individual and gives insufficient weight to the historical character of communities. Societies are not composed only of interchangeable individuals. They are also shaped by:

* Historical populations.
* Inherited traditions.
* Collective memory.
* Social norms.
* Distinct ways of life.

By treating these as politically secondary, liberalism asks communities to accept that preserving their particular character may not be a central duty of the system. It offers unity through equality without necessarily explaining how historical identities and cultures remain intact.



## **Assumptions about assimilation**

This abstraction can encourage an assumption that people from very different backgrounds can be integrated through rights, access and citizenship. However, integration and assimilation are not automatic and may not always be possible.

People do not necessarily abandon established loyalties or practices when entering a new legal system. Some retain them openly. Others may outwardly comply while remaining socially separate.

A liberal order therefore depends on more than formal inclusion. It also depends on whether residents adopt the norms and obligations that sustain common life.



## **Social cohesion**

Liberal societies require trust, reciprocity, civic restraint, shared expectations and acceptance of common rules. These may be easier to maintain where people recognise one another as members of a shared public culture.

Fragmentation can make politics resemble competition between groups with different interests and loyalties. This may weaken solidarity, increase suspicion and make collective sacrifice harder.

Complete uniformity is not required, but institutions need enough common ground to retain legitimacy.



## **Economic stability and reciprocity**

Political openness can create material risks if it is not accompanied by expectations of contribution and integration. A generous welfare system becomes vulnerable where some people approach it primarily as a source of benefits rather than a reciprocal institution.

Large-scale entry for material advantage without corresponding attachment may create fiscal pressure and resentment. Prosperity also depends on work discipline, trust, institutional confidence and long-term orientation. Legal equality does not automatically produce those qualities.



## **Group differences and economic capability**

Differences in intelligence and behaviour may also affect economic outcomes. Populations have not had equal success in creating prosperous societies. Advocates of liberal ideals commonly assume that all groups are equally capable of producing them.

Ignoring relevant differences could make a society less productive, less stable and less able to support its preferred political and social arrangements.



## **Political power and historical self-determination**

When citizenship is broadly extended, changes in population composition can become changes in electoral power. Those changes can affect laws, institutions, public priorities, cultural policy and national identity. Liberalism commonly regards this as a normal result of equal citizenship.

However, it may leave historical populations without a reliable guarantee that their way of life will continue. A previously cohesive community may become several groups competing to direct the state. Minority protections remain important, but those protections are themselves interpreted and maintained through institutions influenced by political majorities.



## **Hidden favouritism**

Formal equality does not eliminate group preference. Majorities can favour their communities indirectly through:

* Migration rules.
* Welfare design.
* Administrative priorities.
* Education.
* Selective evidence.
* Public-interest arguments.

Governments are unlikely to present these as group favouritism. They may describe them as neutral, efficient or humanitarian. In a fragmented society, policy can therefore become a quiet means of distributing resources and building long-term political advantage. Smaller or shrinking communities may lack the voting power to resist cumulative changes.



## **The absence of a continuity guarantee**

Liberalism does not guarantee that a society’s ancestral, cultural, institutional or political character will remain stable. Once equal citizenship is extended, future outcomes depend on demographic change, coalitions and emerging majorities. Liberalism may regard openness to revision as a strength.

From the perspective of historical continuity, it creates uncertainty. A population may adopt liberal principles without fully considering how those principles could change its future political position. Liberalism can therefore be better at legitimising change than preserving inheritance.



## **Changes in control**

An analogy is a company in which an employee becomes the person in charge. The formal organisation may remain, but its priorities, rules and loyalties may change. Those changes can be beneficial or disruptive.

Similarly, a political system cannot assume that future majorities will retain the assumptions and priorities of earlier ones. A historical population could support a system that eventually enables another group’s dominance and then changes to protect that new position.

The assumption that emerging majorities will protect shrinking minorities as earlier majorities did is not guaranteed.



## **Liberal universalism and global diversity**

Liberalism frequently celebrates diversity. However, if strong boundaries are consistently treated as suspect and societies are encouraged toward the same ideals of openness, mobility and equal citizenship, distinct populations may find continuity harder to preserve.

Diversity within a country may increase while diversity between historical nations declines. The concern is that the result could be a gradual flattening of differences rather than a lasting mosaic of distinct peoples.

Ancestry, genetics, heritage and culture are difficult to protect where continuity is treated as secondary. A more conservative arrangement, including ancestral nationalism, is presented as a possible way to avoid their dilution or loss. The question is not whether openness has value, but whether openness without sufficient limits preserves the distinctiveness that gives diversity meaning.



## **Liberalism and dissenting communities**

Liberalism can become coercive where its preferred social model is imposed on an entire population despite substantial differences in how communities want to live. A majority’s support for civic nationalism does not automatically justify every change affecting people who prioritise ancestral continuity, tradition, cultural preservation and local self-government.

Common rules are necessary, but they should not unnecessarily prevent communities from maintaining institutions and ways of life. Decentralisation, local autonomy and voluntary association may provide alternatives. However, dissenters within those communities must also be protected. Otherwise, local self-government can reproduce the domination it was meant to prevent.



## **Moving beyond liberalism**

Moving beyond liberalism does not mean rejecting everything associated with it. The argument does not require abandoning rule of law, basic rights or equal human dignity. It instead treats liberalism as incomplete. A post-liberal perspective would recognise that:

* People are socially embedded rather than merely autonomous.
* Political communities are historical and cultural realities, not only legal constructs.
* Shared norms, trust and continuity matter.
* Borders and membership have moral significance.
* A society needs authority to decide who belongs and what it preserves.

The proposed task is to identify what each ancestral population wants to maintain. If it wants to preserve its continuity and culture, it should be able to pursue that objective.



## **Self-determination as a response**

Liberalism is strongest at explaining fairness within a settled community, but weaker at explaining how that community remains settled. It struggles to account for deep differences, large-scale integration and the cumulative political consequences of demographic change.

For communities that prioritise continuity, a political framework may need to give greater weight to ancestry, history, culture, borders, civic trust and self-government.

Self-determination allows a people to govern its future without control by outside powers or other groups with different interests. It provides a means of protecting ancestry, genetics, heritage, culture and inherited institutions rather than relying indefinitely on the goodwill of changing majorities.
