# Advantages & disadvantages

## Advantages



**Preservation of heritage and culture**

Cultural nationalism is more effective than civic nationalism at preserving a nation’s historical culture, customs, language, symbols, traditions and social norms. Because national membership is tied not just to legal status or political values but also to cultural integration, the nation-state is better able to maintain continuity with the identity that historically shaped it. This can help preserve historical memory, social rituals, public holidays, shared references and ways of life across generations.



**Stronger social cohesion and solidarity**

It is difficult to define all aspects of culture and difficult for migrants to perfectly assimilate into it. The more complex the culture is to define the more difficult it could be to achieve assimilation. However in cases where it is possible that migrants can assimilate into the hosts culture, it should help to maintain a common culture which can make it easier to build trust, mutual obligation and a sense of belonging. Cultural nationalism can strengthen national solidarity by reinforcing a shared identity that extends beyond legal citizenship alone. This can support cooperation, public participation, civic duty and willingness to make sacrifices for the broader community when needed.



**Moderately cost effective citizenship process**

A cultural nationalist citizenship system is usually more complex and costly than a purely civic one, but still generally simpler than setting up systems that attempt to verify ancestry or descent. The process would likely involve testing or evaluating language proficiency, knowledge of national history and customs, familiarity with social norms and acceptance of the political framework. While this can require more administrative judgement than a civic-only model, it can still be standardised to a reasonable extent and operated in a fairly predictable way.



## Disadvantages



**Ancestral group dilution and replacement**

Even if newcomers assimilate culturally, cultural nationalism does not guarantee continuity of the long-established ancestral population because membership is not limited by lineage. Over time, demographic change can still alter the composition of the population even if many migrants successfully adopt the national culture. For those who see ancestry itself as an essential part of nationhood, cultural nationalism may still be viewed as insufficient for preserving the original historical population.



**Cultural dilution and replacement**

Although cultural nationalism is designed to preserve culture, assimilation is rarely perfect or one-directional. In practice, migrants can influence and reshape the host culture while also adapting to it. Over time, the cumulative effect of large-scale migration may alter language use, social expectations, values, traditions, public norms and cultural symbols. If demographic change is substantial, the historical culture may be weakened, transformed or replaced despite the expectations for migrants to assimilate.



**Cultural stagnation complexities**

A system focused on preserving a core national culture can struggle to determine how much change is acceptable and who gets to decide this. If preservation is interpreted too rigidly, the culture may become overly formalised, politicised or resistant to beneficial adaptation. This can create tensions between continuity and innovation, especially as younger generations naturally reinterpret culture over time. Efforts to preserve culture may therefore drift into cultural gatekeeping, nostalgia politics or disputes over what counts as authentic national identity.



**Cultural assimilation complexities**

Assimilation is often difficult to define, measure and enforce consistently. Language fluency, social behaviour, customs, values and identity are not always easy to assess objectively. Some people may meet formal requirements while remaining socially separate, while others may be deeply integrated in practice but still be seen as outsiders. This creates ambiguity in citizenship decisions and can produce accusations of unfairness, discrimination or arbitrary judgement. It can also place high psychological and social burdens on newcomers who may feel pressure to suppress aspects of their background in order to belong.



**Cultural enforcement complexities**

A cultural nationalist system requires continuous judgement about what counts as adequate assimilation. This creates administrative, political and social burdens that do not disappear after citizenship is granted.



**Difficulty controlling demographic change**

Like civic nationalism, cultural nationalism can influence who is admitted and naturalised, but it cannot easily control broader demographic trends such as due to rates of birth, death, emigration and intermarriage. Differences that can lead to long-term identity. Even with stricter cultural expectations, demographic shifts may still gradually transform the nation. This means the system might reduce the long-term effects of migration-related demographic change but it will not prevent them. Cultural nationalism still faces the difficulty in reversing immigration related demographic change as they would need to revoke citizenships.



**Loss of self-determination risks**

If national membership is open to outsiders who are willing to assimilate, then over time newly incorporated populations will also gain political influence and voting power. This can create concerns among long-established ancestral populations that the character and direction of the nation may increasingly be shaped by people whose family roots in the country are more recent. Even where assimilation is substantial, disagreements may remain over whose preferences should define the future of the culture and whether the historical population retains meaningful control over the nation-states development.



**Unequal international outcomes**

If cultural nationalism were widely adopted, migration would still not be evenly distributed across countries. Some nations might receive large inflows and bear the practical burden of integrating newcomers, while others would experience little immigration and therefore face far fewer pressures on their cultural continuity. As a result, some cultures may remain relatively stable while others experience repeated adaptation and contestation. This can produce uneven outcomes between states even if they all formally adopt the same nationalist model.



**Dysgenic risks**

There can be differences in the levels of general intelligence and behaviours between different populations. In some cases an amount of these differences can be reduced by changing environmental factors such as improving access to nutrition and education. However differences in general intelligence and behaviours can still persist across different groups even in more economically prosperous and diverse countries where distinct groups live in a similar environment. Cultural nationalism can create an environment where lower average IQ populations could migrate into these societies and eventually receive citizenship. If these populations intermix with the long-established ancestral population it could result in an intellectual dysgenic trend. Even without intermixing a growth in the demographics of the migrant population could lower the overall average IQ of the nation state. Lowering the average IQ could create an environment that negatively impacts social cohesion, crime and economic prosperity of those nations.



**Loss of global diversity risks**

Granting citizenship to people from any background gives those individuals voting rights to influence national politics. As demographics shift, a nation’s long-established ancestral population and their culture could come under increasing pressure from these external influences, potentially resulting in the collapse of the ancestral identity and culture over the long term. In a cultural nationalist system, the largest communities will hold the largest amount of electoral power. Groups that reproduce more or that migrate in greater numbers will accordingly gain increasing influence over the country’s direction and governance. Cultural nationalism can create an environment where certain identities and cultures are replaced over the long term due to ongoing demographic change in these countries.



**Government representation risks**

If elected governments control migration and citizenship policy, there is always a risk that they make decisions that parts of the existing population believe are too permissive, too restrictive or culturally destabilising. Under cultural nationalism this problem can be more complex because governments must also define what counts as sufficient assimilation. Political actors may manipulate cultural standards for partisan advantage, applying them inconsistently or changing them over time. This can undermine trust in both the migration system and the legitimacy of national membership rules.



**Resiliency risks in times of crises**

In stable conditions, a culturally integrated society may function relatively smoothly, but in times of crisis cultural differences that were previously manageable can become more politically salient. Economic downturns, security threats or international conflicts may heighten suspicion, group tension and questions of loyalty. If assimilation has been shallow or uneven, the society may discover that its apparent cohesion was more fragile than expected. This can weaken collective resilience and place stress on public order and democratic stability.



**External influence risks**

People who join the nation may still retain family, emotional, economic or political ties to countries or communities outside the state. In most cases this is manageable, but in some situations these ties can create competing loyalties or channels of external influence. This is especially sensitive during diplomatic disputes, security crises or conflicts involving a migrant’s country of origin. Cultural nationalism tries to reduce this risk through assimilation, but it cannot remove it entirely because cultural adaptation does not necessarily erase transnational ties.
