# Approaches for national membership, citizenship and immigration

Countries take many different approaches to deciding who belongs in the national community, who may enter and live in the country, how citizenship can be acquired and how people from different backgrounds should live together. These questions are related, but they are not the same.

A country can have low immigration while making citizenship accessible to the relatively small number of migrants it admits. Another can rely heavily on temporary migrant workers while rarely granting them citizenship. A third can combine high permanent immigration with a demanding naturalisation process. Immigration levels, citizenship rules, national identity and integration policies should therefore be considered as separate dimensions rather than as points on a single political spectrum.

Countries also vary in their ancestral, cultural, religious and racial composition. Some are relatively homogeneous, while others are highly diverse. This may reflect geography, history, migration, changing borders, intermarriage and government policy.



## **National membership & citizenship**

National membership concerns who is recognised as belonging to the political community. It can refer to formal citizenship, but it can also describe wider ideas about national identity and social belonging. Legal citizenship and social acceptance do not always coincide: a person may possess citizenship without being widely regarded as part of the nation, while a long-term resident may participate extensively in society without being a citizen.



**Ancestral membership**

Under an ancestral model, the nation is understood as a historical community connected by ancestry, language, religion, culture or inherited traditions. Membership may be closely associated with descent from an established national population.

This approach can promote historical continuity and a strong sense of shared identity. However, it can also make it difficult or impossible for immigrants and minority citizens to be accepted as full members.

Citizenship is primarily acquired by descent from a citizen parent or through recognised ancestry. Some ancestral models still permit naturalisation, but they may distinguish between legal citizenship and belonging to the historical nation. Ancestry could also be the sole basis for who can become a citizen.



**Cultural membership**

Under this model, people from different backgrounds may become accepted as members of the nation if they adopt its language, public customs, cultural traditions, institutions and central values. Ancestry is not necessarily decisive, but cultural adaptation is expected.

The requirements can vary considerably. A very open version may ask newcomers to learn the national language and respect constitutional principles. A more demanding version may expect them to abandon much of their previous cultural identity.

Access to citizenship may be either restrictive or relatively open, but it will commonly require evidence of cultural assimilation. This could include language proficiency, knowledge of national history and customs, participation in social life and acceptance of the country’s central institutions. The key question is how much adaptation is reasonably required and how much private cultural difference remains protected.



**Civic membership**

A civic model defines the nation mainly through citizenship, shared laws, public institutions and political participation. People can belong regardless of their ancestry, race or religion if they accept the constitutional order and fulfil the responsibilities of citizenship.

Civic models normally make it easier to recognise citizens from different backgrounds as equally national. In practice, however, civic identity is often combined with some expectations concerning language, history or public culture.

Citizenship may be highly conditional or broadly accessible, but membership is based principally on participation in civic life rather than ancestry. Applicants may be expected to support constitutional institutions, obey the law, learn how government works and adopt shared political principles. Once citizenship is acquired, all citizens should normally enjoy equal status regardless of their origin.



**Residence-based membership**

A residence-based model can give migrants options to live and work in a country. Long-term residents could receive political, social or economic rights.

This approach emphasises participation and the fact that residents are affected by the country’s laws. Long-term residents may receive employment protections, public services and voting rights in local or regional elections without obtaining full citizenship. National voting, certain public offices and security-sensitive roles may remain reserved for citizens, however non-citizen residents may participate politically through local institutions, unions, civil society and consultation. The longer a person resides in the country and contributes to its institutions, the stronger the argument may become for granting permanent residence.



## **Immigration**

Immigration policy determines who may enter a country, for what purposes, in what numbers and for how long. Countries can adopt policies ranging from no or near-zero immigration to very high levels of immigration. This also involves an important distinction between permanent settlement and temporary migration for work or study.



**No or near-zero immigration**

A literal policy of zero immigration would prevent foreign nationals from moving to the country for temporary or permanent residence. In practice, this is rare because governments may still admit diplomats, refugees, close family members or essential specialists. Tourism, short business visits and the return of citizens from abroad would not ordinarily count as immigration. A country may nevertheless maintain near-zero immigration to minimise demographic change, preserve cultural continuity, avoid dependence on migrant labour or retain close control over population growth. This can reduce the pace of change and pressure associated with new arrivals, but it mean that labour shortages, skills needs and an ageing population must be addressed through domestic education, higher workforce participation, automation or economic reform.



**Low immigration**

Low immigration permits a limited number of people to enter, usually through selected categories such as family reunion, skilled employment, humanitarian protection or exceptional need. It can allow a country to gain particular skills and meet international or family obligations while keeping population change gradual. Lower numbers may make housing provision and integration easier to manage, although they can also restrict economic growth, reduce the available workforce and leave some labour shortages unfilled.



**Moderate immigration**

Moderate immigration allows continuing movement through a wider range of work, family, education and humanitarian routes. It is often intended to balance the economic and social benefits of migration with the country’s capacity to provide housing, infrastructure and public services. It can support gradual population growth, expand the workforce and provide cultural and commercial connections with other countries. Its effects depend on how migrants are selected, where they settle and whether government planning keeps pace with population change.



**High immigration**

High or large-scale immigration, or sometimes described as mass migration, involves admitting a large number of people relative to the existing population. A country may adopt this approach to address extensive labour shortages, population ageing or regional decline, or to promote economic expansion, education and international exchange. It can increase the supply of workers and skills, create new businesses and strengthen links with the wider world. However, rapid immigration can also increase demand for housing, transport, schools and healthcare, place pressure on social cohesion, labour markets and produce faster cultural or demographic changes than some communities are comfortable with.



## **Adopting different approaches and combinations**

Countries may adopt different combinations of national membership, citizenship and immigration policy. One country might understand itself mainly as an ancestral and cultural community, grant citizenship primarily by descent and maintain little or no discretionary immigration. Another might define membership in civic terms, provide an accessible route to naturalisation and accept moderate or high immigration. Countries can also combine these positions, for example, by maintaining low immigration while offering equal civic membership to those admitted, or by accepting substantial temporary migration while keeping naturalisation restrictive.

There is no single immigration level or model of national identity that every country is morally required to adopt. A population may reasonably value ancestral, cultural and demographic continuity and choose low immigration or demanding naturalisation requirements. Another may value openness, pluralism and the ability of people from different racial, ancestral, religious and cultural backgrounds to become equal members, leading it to favour accessible citizenship and large-scale immigration. These can reflect different collective priorities concerning continuity, diversity, economic development, international mobility and the pace of social change.

Relatively homogeneous and highly diverse societies can both be peaceful, democratic and socially cohesive. An ancestral conception of the nation is not necessarily hostile to outsiders, just as a civic conception does not necessarily require unrestricted immigration. Low or near-zero immigration can be a legitimate collective choice, as can moderate or high immigration, provided the policy is adopted through lawful and democratic institutions.

Major demographic policies should consider democratic consent, public capacity and their effects on both established populations and newcomers. Subject to these limits, societies may reasonably reach different conclusions about who may enter, how citizenship is acquired and how national membership is understood.



## **Far-left and far-right approaches**

The political left generally places greater emphasis on equality, social welfare, labour rights and reducing social or economic hierarchy. The political right generally places greater emphasis on order, continuity, private property, national sovereignty and established institutions. These broad traditions include many moderate and democratic positions.

Far-left and far-right movements represent the tendencies at either end of the spectrum, but neither is entirely uniform. Some far-left traditions are strongly internationalist, while others have supported immigration controls to protect workers or centralised national projects. Some far-right movements define the nation primarily through culture and assimilation, while ancestral nationalist variants place greater emphasis on ancestral continuity.

Immigration restriction is not necessarily far-right, just as support for immigration is not necessarily far-left. Governments across the political spectrum may regulate immigration for economic, humanitarian, security or capacity-related reasons. The following table describes some of the more common tendencies rather than positions held by every far-left or far-right movement.

| **Factor**                             | **Far-left tendencies**                                                                                                                              | **Far-right tendencies**                                                                                                                           |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Basis of national membership**       | Often emphasises residence, participation, class solidarity or universal human equality                                                              | Often emphasises ancestry, inherited culture, religion or demanding cultural assimilation                                                          |
| **Understanding of the nation**        | May regard the nation-state and national borders as secondary to international solidarity                                                            | Usually regards the nation as a distinct historical community whose identity, sovereignty and continuity should be protected                       |
| **Demographic diversity**              | Generally views diversity as acceptable or positive and opposes attempts to preserve racial homogeneity                                              | Often seeks to preserve a historically established national culture. Ancestral nationalist forms may also seek demographic or ancestral continuity |
| **Scale of immigration**               | Often supports more permissive admission, although labour-oriented movements may support controls intended to protect wages and working conditions   | Usually supports substantially lower immigration or highly selective admission                                                                     |
| **Selection of migrants**              | Often gives priority to humanitarian need, family unity and equal treatment rather than wealth or skill alone                                        | Often gives priority to skills, economic benefit, security, cultural compatibility or connections to the national population                       |
| **Temporary labour migration**         | May oppose insecure temporary status because it can enable worker exploitation; often supports equal labour rights and routes to permanent residence | May accept temporary workers when economically useful while limiting their access to permanent membership                                          |
| **Asylum and refugees**                | Emphasises universal obligations, humanitarian protection and the rights of displaced people                                                         | Emphasises national discretion, border control, security, capacity and deterrence of unfounded claims                                              |
| **Naturalisation**                     | Usually supports accessible naturalisation for long-term residents                                                                                   | Usually supports longer residence periods and stronger language, loyalty, character or assimilation requirements                                   |
| **Birthright citizenship**             | Often supports broad birthright citizenship to prevent inherited exclusion                                                                           | Often supports conditional birthright citizenship based on the status or citizenship of a parent                                                   |
| **Dual citizenship**                   | Generally accepts multiple citizenship and overlapping identities                                                                                    | More likely to favour exclusive citizenship or require applicants to renounce previous nationalities                                               |
| **Rights of long-term residents**      | Often seeks to reduce legal and social distinctions between citizens and established residents                                                       | More likely to reserve political and some social rights for citizens                                                                               |
| **Integration**                        | Usually supports multiculturalism and accommodation of different identities                                                                          | Usually supports assimilation into a common national language, culture and set of traditions                                                       |
| **Unauthorised migration**             | Often prioritises regularisation, due process, labour protection and prevention of exploitation                                                      | Often prioritises detention, removal, border enforcement and deterrence                                                                            |
| **Border enforcement**                 | May favour less restrictive borders and stronger safeguards for migrants                                                                             | Usually favours stronger physical and legal border controls                                                                                        |
| **Central concern**                    | Preventing exclusion, exploitation and unequal treatment                                                                                             | Preserving sovereignty, security, cohesion and national continuity                                                                                 |
| **Characteristic risk at the extreme** | May understate practical limits involving housing, public services, labour markets, integration or democratic consent                                | May turn legitimate concerns about borders or continuity into collective blame or unequal treatment of minority citizens                           |

There is a wide range of possible positions between these different ends of the political spectrum. A country can maintain controlled borders while offering protection to refugees, require newcomers to learn a common language while protecting minority cultures, or limit immigration while providing an achievable path to citizenship for long-term residents.

The central policy questions are therefore not simply whether a country should be homogeneous or diverse. They are who may enter, whether migration is temporary or permanent, how national membership is defined, when residents can become citizens and what integration is expected.
