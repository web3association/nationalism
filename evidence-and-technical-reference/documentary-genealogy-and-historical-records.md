# Documentary genealogy and historical records

Official records and historical documents can help establish an individual’s lineage and the length of a family’s connection to a locality, region or nation-state. They can provide evidence of:

* Parent-child relationships.
* Residence.
* Legal status.
* Family continuity.
* Historical rootedness.
* Community membership.
* Social endogamy.
* Stable identity across generations.

However, evidence that a family lived in a region for several generations does not necessarily establish that it shares the same genetic ancestry as the historical population. Documents may demonstrate rootedness in a country or culture without proving genetic purity, exclusive shared ancestry or the absence of admixture.



## **Population coverage**

The usefulness of a record depends partly on how much of the population it covers. Some systems register most people. Others record only people involved in particular activities or legal processes. Records with limited coverage are less suitable as the sole basis for population-wide membership decisions, although they may provide supporting evidence.



## **Civil registration**

Civil registration can provide strong evidence of legal status and parent-child relationships. Examples include:

* Birth certificates and registrations.
* Marriage certificates and registrations.
* Death certificates and registrations.



## **Census and household enumeration**

These records can show where families lived and how they were connected to a locality over time. Examples include:

* Census returns.
* Population registers.
* Residence books.
* Local inhabitant lists.
* Household enumeration forms.
* Enumerator books.
* Household schedules.
* Household rolls.



## **Residency and local registration**

These concern legal residence, local belonging and movement into or out of an area. Examples include:

* Domicile records.
* Settlement records.
* Resident registration records.
* Municipal registration records.
* Address registration records.
* Address cards.
* Inhabitant registration records.
* Local citizenship or belonging records.
* Right-of-residence records.
* Move-in and move-out registrations.



## **Church and religious records**

Religious institutions can provide important evidence, particularly for periods before civil registration. Examples include:

* Baptism and christening records.
* Confirmation records.
* Marriage records.
* Burial records.
* Membership rolls.
* Parish status records.
* Parish household lists.
* Parish family books.
* Church census lists.
* Souls registers or _status animarum_.



## **Population and family registers**

These provide continuous or semi-continuous records of individuals and households within a locality. Examples include:

* Population registers.
* Family registers.
* Family books.
* Household books.
* Household rolls.
* House registers.
* Parish family books.
* Parish household registers.
* Family registration cards.
* Individual registration cards.



## **Citizenship and nationality records**

These establish legal status and may support descent claims. Their coverage is often uneven because they are most likely to exist when citizenship is acquired, confirmed, disputed, changed or formally documented. They may not cover every person in every historical period. Examples include:

* Citizenship certificates and records.
* Naturalisation applications.
* Naturalisation records.
* Nationality confirmation records.
* Passport applications.
* Identity registration records.
* Local citizenship records.
* Municipal citizenship records.
* National identity systems.
* Consular records.



## **Immigration and emigration records**

These help establish movement across borders or administrative boundaries. Their limitation is that they concern people who moved. People who remained in one place may not appear. Examples include:

* Passenger lists.
* Entry records.
* Exit permits.
* Emigration permits.
* Immigration files.
* Border-crossing records.
* Alien registration records.
* Refugee records.
* Displacement records.
* Visa records.
* Passport-control records.
* Refugee registration records.
* Deportation or expulsion records.
* Immigration cards.



## **School and university records**

Educational records can demonstrate residence and local connections. Coverage varies because access to education differed by period, class, gender and region. University records are particularly selective because only a small proportion of historical populations attended. Examples include:

* Enrolment records.
* Admission records.
* Attendance registers.
* School census lists.
* Teacher reports.
* Graduation records.
* Degree records.
* Church school rolls.



## **Military records**

Military records concern people eligible for or involved in service. Historically, this often meant mainly adult men. Coverage depends on conscription arrangements, wartime conditions and whether archives survived. Examples include:

* Conscription lists and registers.
* Draft records.
* Military service records.
* Pension records.
* Officer records.
* Veteran records.
* Wartime military census lists.



## **Notarial, tax and property records**

These can establish a family’s regional connections across generations. They tend to represent property holders, taxpayers, household heads and people involved in formal transactions more strongly than poorer or mobile populations. Examples include:

* Wills and probate files.
* Land deeds.
* Title deeds.
* Property transfers.
* Mortgage records.
* Inheritance records.
* Lease records.
* Dowry records.
* Land registers.
* Tax registers.
* Estate maps.
* Manorial records.
* Probate records.
* Estate inventories.
* Tenancy agreements.
* Cadastral records.
* Rent rolls.



## **Court and administrative records**

These arise when someone participates in a legal or administrative process. Many residents may never appear in them, so coverage can be uneven. Examples include:

* Guardianship records.
* Adoption records.
* Legitimisation records.
* Name-change records.
* Parish dispute records.
* Civil lawsuits.
* Registration appeals.
* Licensing records.
* Local administrative petitions.



## **Recent naturalisation decisions**

Records may be especially useful where a state seeks to reconsider naturalisation from recent decades. Administrative coverage may be complete or nearly complete for that period. The task would involve identifying people for exclusion rather than verifying the ancestry of every person in the country. This can be simpler than a population-wide process.

However, reversing recent naturalisation would not itself create a durable ancestry-based membership system. It would return the population closer to an earlier position while allowing demographic change to continue under the remaining rules. The political and legal transition questions are discussed in **Existing citizenship, denaturalisation and security of residence**.



## **Supporting lineage evidence**

Documents can help show how long someone and their ancestors have lived in a region. They can support genetic evidence even where they cannot establish genetic membership by themselves.

For example, strong historical documentation combined with a genetic match could support including a person’s sample in a reference panel. Conversely, records showing that a family originated elsewhere could challenge an interpretation based on genetic evidence alone.



## **Limits as a primary verification method**

Documents may establish a lineage without proving that the lineage belongs to the same broader genetic ancestral population. For this reason, the source does not regard records alone as a sufficiently reliable primary method for verifying every individual’s inclusion in a genetically defined historical population. Their principal value is establishing relationships, residence, historical rootedness and supporting evidence.
