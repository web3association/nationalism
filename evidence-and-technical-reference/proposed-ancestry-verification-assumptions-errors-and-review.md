# Proposed ancestry verification: assumptions, errors and review

Once membership boundaries have been defined, a verification process would be needed to apply them. The proposed process would determine whether an individual has sufficient ancestral ties for:

* Citizenship.
* Political membership.
* Eligibility for particular decisions.
* Another legal status.

It could be used during a transition from civic to ancestral membership and for later applications.



## **Boundaries come before verification**

Verification applies a rule; it does not decide what the rule should be. The system must first establish:

* Which historical population is being protected.
* What ancestral connection is required.
* Whether citizenship and political rights use different thresholds.
* What happens in uncertain cases.

Those decisions are addressed in **Descent-based membership: boundaries and contested cases**.



## **Evidence sources**

One potential suggested system could uses two broad forms of evidence. Documentary evidence can establish lineage, residence, family continuity and legal status. Genetic evidence can estimate biological ancestry and compare an individual with reference populations. Neither is perfect by itself. The source therefore proposes using them together.



## **Defining the reference population**

Before constructing a genetic panel, the state would need to define the historical population forming the basis of membership. This would involve historical, legal, demographic and genealogical evidence. The definition cannot be produced by genetic analysis alone. If too narrow, it may exclude legitimate historical members. If too broad, it may weaken the intended membership boundary.



## **Constructing the reference panel**

A reference panel is a selected dataset of DNA samples used as the comparison standard. Its quality affects every later result. The proposed panel would draw on individuals whose ancestry is supported by several forms of evidence, including:

* Official records.
* Family trees.
* Long-term regional rootedness.
* Local population history.
* Genetic lineage testing.

The objective is to avoid reliance on random or poorly documented samples.



## **High-resolution reference data**

Whole-genome sequencing may be particularly useful for building the panel because it provides detailed genetic information. Other methods could add supporting evidence:

* Y-chromosome testing for direct paternal lines.
* Mitochondrial testing for direct maternal lines.
* STR testing for close kinship.
* Genome-wide analysis for broader ancestry.

These methods would not independently define the population but could support the combined assessment.



## **Understanding internal population structure**

After collecting suitable samples, modelling could investigate:

* Regional subgroups.
* Historical mixture.
* Founder effects.
* Relationships with neighbouring populations.
* Shared ancestry within the historical population.

Family trees connected to DNA matches could help triangulate ancestors and compare genetic patterns with documented lineage.



## **Existing commercial panels**

The state could create its own reference panel or collaborate with existing ancestry services. Building from scratch introduces costs and requires substantial data collection. Collaboration may reduce some duplication but raises questions about:

* Access to proprietary datasets.
* Privacy.
* Consent.
* Contractual restrictions.
* Permitted secondary uses.



## **External comparison data**

A panel representing the national population may not be sufficient by itself. Comparisons with other populations can be necessary to distinguish overlapping or closely related ancestry. This may require reference data from other regions or countries. The quality of those external panels can affect confidence in the result.



## **Reviewing the reference panel**

A panel should not be treated as permanently complete. New evidence may reveal that it inadequately represents particular regions, families, classes or historical subgroups. Revision may therefore be necessary. However, panel changes can alter previous results. The system would need rules concerning:

* Retesting.
* Credential updates.
* Existing status.
* Grandfathering.
* Appeals.

Without such rules, changes in evidence could make citizenship or political participation unstable.



## **Routine individual testing**

Once the panel exists, individuals could be compared with it. Autosomal SNP genotyping is the most scalable routine method because it is cheaper and easier to apply broadly than whole-genome sequencing. Higher-resolution testing could be reserved for reference-panel construction, research or difficult cases.



## **Clear and uncertain results**

An estimate should not be treated as perfectly exact. Results depend on the panel, analytical methods and similarity between populations. A process will need to distinguish between clear inclusion, clear exclusion and borderline or uncertain cases. Uncertain cases would require further review rather than an immediate definitive outcome.



## **False positives and false negatives**

A false positive occurs where someone is recognised as qualifying when they should not. A false negative occurs where someone with genuine qualifying ties is rejected. Both are risks. Reducing them requires:

* Better reference data.
* Documentary support.
* Retesting.
* Clear standards.
* Independent review.



## **Possible administrative outcomes**

The process could produce:

* Verification for citizenship and political membership.
* Verification for citizenship only.
* Failure to meet a required threshold.
* An unresolved result requiring additional evidence.

A person meeting the citizenship standard would obtain or retain citizenship. A person meeting a higher political threshold could receive national voting rights, office eligibility or authority to participate in specified decisions.



## **Exclusion does not establish another nationality**

Failure to qualify for one historical population does not prove that another country will admit or recognise the person. A genetic connection is not automatically a legal citizenship entitlement.

Where external ancestry is identified, diplomatic coordination may be needed to determine whether another state actually recognises a citizenship claim. If no clear comparison is available, the case should remain unresolved rather than be treated as evidence that the person belongs nowhere.



## **Status during review**

The proposed approach is to retain legal status until a secure alternative exists. Possible arrangements identified include:

* Transitional citizenship.
* Permanent residence.
* Protected non-political citizenship.
* Another legal category.

The central intention is to avoid statelessness while evidence or alternative arrangements are considered. The wider citizenship implications are discussed in **Existing citizenship, denaturalisation and security of residence**.



## **Appeals**

Disputes may concern:

* Exclusion from citizenship.
* Exclusion from political participation.
* Borderline estimates.
* Documentary errors.
* Test quality.
* Reference-panel coverage.
* Interpretation of the boundary.

A dedicated appeal process would therefore be required.



## **Evidence during an appeal**

Individuals could be able to:

* Submit additional records.
* Provide family-tree evidence.
* Request retesting.
* Obtain higher-resolution testing.
* Receive independent review.

The reviewing body could correct administrative mistakes or identify weaknesses in the reference panel. Where new evidence shows systematic underrepresentation, it could recommend panel revision.



## **Protection while an appeal is unresolved**

People should retain existing or temporary protected status during review. This prevents errors, uncertainty or administrative delays from immediately removing rights. The same principle applies where a result changes because of an updated panel.



## **Credentials and updated results**

A verified person might receive a credential recording the result. If the reference panel changes, the credential may no longer reflect the current estimate. There could be a need for arrangements that allow credentials to be updated or revoked when the results change. Some individuals might need to resubmit markers or undergo another assessment. This creates an additional reason for clear rules about the relationship between a technical result and established legal status.



## **Illustrative costs**

A rough population-testing cost might be around $10 to $40 per person. For a population of ten million, that would amount to approximately $100 million to $400 million. The wider cost would also depend on:

* Testing methods.
* Population size.
* Quality of existing records.
* Dispute rates.
* Reference-panel development.
* Administration.

Whole-genome sequencing is more expensive and is therefore proposed mainly for panel construction, research-grade analysis and difficult appeals.



## **Staged implementation**

Testing the entire population immediately would be less realistic than a phased process. The proposed sequence is to:

1. Define the reference population.
2. Build the reference panel.
3. Test a smaller sample.
4. Resolve administrative problems.
5. Establish appeals and oversight.
6. Expand the process.

The intention is to reduce large-scale error and allow the system to improve before broader use.



## **Verification and political legitimacy**

A technically functioning system does not eliminate disputes about the membership rule itself. People may challenge both the quality of the evidence and the legitimacy of using ancestry to determine rights. The constitutional and democratic issues are therefore separate from the laboratory and administrative process. They are addressed in **Constitutional change, consent and democratic legitimacy**.
