# Sensitive data, consent and privacy

Genetic and genealogical information can reveal details not only about the person tested but also about relatives and wider family relationships. A verification system using such data therefore requires clear governance. Privacy protections affect public confidence, the risk of misuse and whether information gathered for one purpose is used for another.



## **What information is collected?**

The system needs to define the data required for its stated purpose. Potential material includes:

* Genetic markers.
* Sequence data.
* Family trees.
* Civil records.
* Residence history.
* Legal-status documents.
* Test results.
* Eligibility credentials.

The rules should make clear what is collected and how it is used.



## **Access**

There should be defined limits on who can access raw data, supporting records and results. The system must distinguish between the information needed for:

* Laboratory analysis.
* Administrative decisions.
* Appeals.
* Credential issuance.
* Other proposed uses.

Access to a credential need not automatically require access to all underlying information.



## **Storage and retention**

Rules are needed for:

* Storage.
* Retention periods.
* Security.
* Deletion.
* Restrictions on further use.

People should understand whether they can request deletion or limitations on processing.



## **Separation from other databases**

The source identifies separation from unrelated medical, policing and commercial databases as the safest model. Combining them could create risks of:

* Surveillance.
* Discrimination.
* Unauthorised profiling.
* Unrelated investigations.
* Commercial exploitation.
* Loss of trust.



## **Secondary uses**

Information gathered for ancestry verification may also be useful for healthcare, family-history research or other purposes. However, those uses are separate from the original membership assessment.

Secondary use should require explicit legal authorisation and strong oversight. Without clear limits, a system created for one purpose could become a broader source of monitoring or discrimination.



## **Public health opportunities and governance**

The source proposes that population testing could support health screening alongside ancestry analysis. Possible benefits include information about disease risk, medication response, carrier status and inherited conditions.

Those opportunities do not remove the need for rules about how medical findings are handled or whether ancestry data are reused. The capabilities are described in **Genetic ancestry inference: capabilities and limitations**.



## **Commercial collaboration**

Using data from existing companies introduces additional questions. A company may have collected samples under terms that restrict sharing or reuse. Collaboration therefore depends on privacy commitments and legal agreements, not only technical access. A state cannot assume that a commercially developed panel is freely available for a different purpose.



## **Digital credentials**

Credentials could reduce the need to repeatedly disclose underlying personal information. For example, someone might prove that an authorised body verified a status without showing every supporting document. However, credentials still require:

* Trusted issuers.
* Correct underlying evidence.
* Secure recovery.
* Update procedures.
* Revocation rules.
* Dispute resolution.

Their privacy benefits depend on implementation rather than following automatically from the technology.



## **Genetic information and family privacy**

Genetic information can disclose relationships or characteristics involving relatives who were not the direct applicant. This makes it unusually sensitive. The effects of disclosure extend beyond an ordinary administrative record concerning only one person. Data governance therefore needs to recognise the wider family implications of testing and storage.



## **Changing results**

Reference-panel updates can alter ancestry estimates. This creates questions about:

* Whether historical data are retained.
* Whether people are retested.
* How credentials are updated.
* Who can see previous results.
* How changed outcomes are challenged.

Stable administrative procedures are important because evidence changes may otherwise produce uncertainty about legal status.



## **Independent review**

Appeals and oversight should cover not only membership outcomes but also errors or misuse within the data system. The broader verification process requires transparency, reviewability and public confidence. People should have a way to challenge incorrect records, disputed results and administrative mistakes.



## **Privacy as part of feasibility**

Privacy is not separate from the practical viability of the proposed system. Poor governance could produce public distrust, resistance and harmful secondary uses. The source therefore treats data collection, storage, access, retention, deletion and secondary use as core design questions alongside test accuracy and administrative cost.
