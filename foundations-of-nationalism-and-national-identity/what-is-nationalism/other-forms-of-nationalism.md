# Other forms of nationalism

Civic nationalism is one of the main forms of nationalism associated with prioritising civic-state continuity.

Cultural nationalism, along with related or overlapping variants such as religious and linguistic nationalism, is most closely associated with prioritising cultural continuity.

Ancestral nationalism is one of several forms of nationalism associated with prioritising the continuity of a distinct people. It defines the nation through the continuity of a distinct historical population, especially through descent from the founding or long-established peoples of that nation. Other variants, such as nativist nationalism, racial nationalism, blood nationalism and blood-and-soil nationalism, also prioritise people continuity, but they do so in different ways and with different levels of membership inclusiveness.



## **Ethno-nationalism and ethnic nationalism**

The word _ethnos_ is derived from Greek and refers to a people understood as a cultural-historical community. In modern discussion, it is often used interchangeably with _ethnic_, though the two are not always identical in meaning.

_Ethnos_ generally refers to a community bound together by a shared identity, culture, language, history, traditions, and often a sense of common ancestry. It expresses the idea of peoplehood: a distinct cultural group that may be understood separately from a political nation-state.

In ancient Greek usage, however, _ethnos_ did not always refer to a strictly defined cultural group. Its meanings were broader and could include a “band”, “tribe”, “caste”, “crowd”, or simply a number of people accustomed to living together.

The term _ethnic_ is fairly broad in its definition and has been defined in a variety of ways by modern dictionaries:

* “related or belonging to a group of people who can be seen as distinct because they have a shared culture, tradition, language, history, etc.” - [Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/ethnic)
* “of or relating to large groups of people classed according to common racial, national, tribal, religious, linguistic, or cultural origin or background” - [Merriam-Webster Dictionary](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/ethnic)
* “means connected with or related to different racial or cultural groups of people” - [Collins Dictionary](https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/ethnic)

Both _ethnos_ and _ethnic_ tend to combine ancestry and cultural aspects into a single concept. Depending on how these terms are interpreted, they can also allow for the possibility that someone outside the group can join the nation through sufficient cultural assimilation.

Because culture and ancestry are distinct aspects of group identity, it can be more useful to treat them separately rather than merge them under the broader labels of _ethnos_ or _ethnic_. For this reason, the terms cultural nationalism and ancestral nationalism could be more suitable here for defining distinct forms of nationalism. Separating these elements makes it clearer whether national membership is being defined by assimilation into a culture or by descent from a historic population.

A cultural nationalist is primarily concerned with preserving the nation’s culture, traditions and way of life. An ancestral nationalist is primarily concerned with preserving the continuity of the nation’s ancestral group. In practice, an ancestral nationalist may also care deeply about cultural preservation, but ancestral nationalism is the more explicit term when the nation is defined principally in terms of descent.



## **Nativist nationalism**

Nativist nationalism prioritises the interests and political influence of people considered “native” to a country over immigrants, outsiders or newer arrivals. It is concerned with protecting the established national community from rapid external change, especially demographic, cultural or political change caused by immigration.

Nativist nationalism can vary significantly in how it defines membership and who counts as native. In a territorial sense, someone born and raised in a country may be described as native to it even if their ancestors came from elsewhere. Under _jus soli_, a person acquires citizenship through birth within the state’s territory. Jus soli is not inherently nativist; it is a territorial citizenship rule commonly associated with civic membership. It may nevertheless operate within a mildly nativist system if native-born citizens are distinguished from foreign-born immigrants or given some form of preference. Whether the locally born children of immigrants count as native depends on how the particular nativist movement defines the term.

A stricter form of nativism may require a person’s family to have lived in the country for several generations before they are considered native. For example, a nationalist movement might treat people as native only if their family has been living there for fifty or one hundred years, or for three or more generations. This type of policy can help with improving the preservation of the ancestral population due to the difficulty of others becoming full members of the nation. However, it may create a pathway by which newer groups eventually become the dominant part of the population over time.

The strictest form of nativist nationalism would overlap more clearly with ancestral nationalism. In this version, native status is tied to descent from the founding or long-established peoples of the nation. Rather than treating birth in the country or several generations of residence as sufficient, it defines native membership through a deeper ancestral connection to the historic national population.

Nativist nationalism is therefore best understood as a more flexible and broad category. It can be used where the aim is to limit or slow demographic change while still allowing some degree of population change over time. However, if the aim is to more specifically preserve historical demographic continuity, the more narrow forms of people-based nationalism are likely going to be more suitable.



## **Racial nationalism**

Racial nationalism defines national belonging through membership in a racial group. Instead of defining the nation by citizenship, culture, language or descent from a specific ancestral population, it defines the nation through a broader racial category.

Compared with the narrower blood nationalism, racial nationalism can be more broadly inclusive within its chosen racial category. For example, a racial nationalist who defines the nation in terms of whiteness might include English, French, German, Dutch or Scandinavian people within the same broad racial grouping. In that sense, racial nationalism can include multiple ethnic or ancestral groups that would remain distinct under narrower forms of blood or ancestral nationalism.

However, this broadness of categorisation means racial nationalism is less precise if the goal is to preserve the continuity of a particular historical people. A racial category can include many distinct peoples with different languages, cultures, histories and ancestral identities.

For that reason, racial nationalism is better understood as a form of broad racial-group continuity rather than an effective solution for the preservation of a distinct national ancestral group. It may preserve a wider biological or racial category, but it does not specifically preserve the continuity of each distinct people within that category. If the focus is on preserving a particular historic nation or founding population, then ancestral nationalism or forms of blood nationalism would be more suitable.



## **Blood nationalism**

Blood nationalism is the idea that membership in a nation is based primarily on shared blood and genealogical or biological descent. A person belongs to the nation because they are understood to share a common biological background with its people. There may be multiple related bloodlines within a nation, but the key idea is that membership is inherited and rooted in biological descent.

Ancestral nationalism overlaps with blood nationalism because both are descent-based. However, ancestral nationalism can be applied more broadly. It may focus on descent from the founding or long-established peoples of the nation, even if those peoples did not all share a single biological bloodline. For example, if several distinct groups participated in the formation of a nation, ancestral nationalism could include their descendants as part of the inherited national community.

Blood nationalism can represent a narrower boundary for membership when it requires a stronger degree of common biological descent among members. It is more exclusive than a broader implementation of ancestral nationalism that might include all groups with long-standing ancestral ties to the nation’s formation.

Ancestral nationalism could be the more pragmatic solution in cases where individuals or minority groups have had ancestral ties to a nation for centuries, even though they do not share the primary bloodline of the majority group. Such groups may be deeply integrated into the historical population and may have no meaningful connection to any other nation across a very long and extended period of time. This might allow for a broader understanding of inherited national belonging over strict blood-based forms of nationalism.

If the main priority is descent from the founding or long-established peoples of the nation, then ancestral nationalism is usually the more suitable term. If the main priority is preserving a distinct biological group defined by common blood, then blood nationalism is more precise.



## **Blood-and-soil nationalism**

Blood-and-soil nationalism combines inherited descent with a strong connection to a particular homeland. The “blood” element refers to shared blood and biological descent, while the “soil” element refers to rootedness in a specific land, territory or ancestral homeland.

Blood nationalism can be geographically portable: a person may be considered part of the nation because of their descent, even if they live abroad or were raised in another country. Blood-and-soil nationalism is stricter because it also stresses the importance of being rooted in the homeland itself.

A blood-and-soil nationalist may argue that full national belonging requires both the correct descent and a formative connection to the ancestral land, its culture and its historical community. This can make it more restrictive toward both immigrants without the relevant ancestry and diaspora members who have the ancestry but lack lived connection to the homeland.

Ancestral nationalism does not necessarily require an organic bond between bloodline and land in the same way that blood-and-soil nationalism does. The primary concern of ancestral nationalism is continuity of the founding or long-established ancestral population for that nation. An ancestral nationalist approach could still place importance on territorial and cultural rootedness.

Blood-and-soil nationalism begins from bloodline and adds land-rootedness. Ancestral nationalism begins from continuity with the historic people of the nation and may incorporate land-rootedness, culture and upbringing as additional factors. This makes ancestral nationalism the broader and more flexible form of nationalism, while blood-and-soil nationalism can be seen as the stricter and more exclusionary approach.



## **Comparison between these forms of nationalism**

Multiple forms of nationalism can be used to help with the preservation of a particular people in their own nation-state. These forms can be compared to gauge which ones are better for the preservation of a particular nation’s civic institutions, culture and historical population.

This comparison has important limitations. Certain nationalist systems may be designed to promote particular forms of civic, cultural or demographic continuity, but no political system can prevent all forms of change. Even a highly homogeneous and self-governing population may substantially alter its institutions, political values, language, religion, cultural traditions or way of life. Culture is inherently one of the most difficult things to preserve as it commonly evolves over time due to changing preferences, environments and technology. Civic-states can be easier to preserve when the institutions are functioning as intended and the political values that people hold are easy to adopt and agree with. The level of preservation for a nation’s distinct population can vary more drastically across the different forms of nationalism.



**Civic nationalism**

* **Civic-state** - The civic-state may be easier to preserve compared to a nation’s culture and its people. However, each distinct population across the world has not created the same political institutions and civic life, nor do they all hold the same political values.
* **Culture** - Civic nationalism makes common citizenship and political membership the principal basis of the nation. It can still support an official language, shared historical narrative and public culture, but it generally permits citizens of different ancestral or cultural backgrounds to belong on equal terms. This means that cultural traditions from other groups could eventually become the predominant national culture.
* **People** - Civic nationalism does not make descent from the historical population a necessary condition of membership. It therefore provides no inherent guarantee that the historical population will retain its historical demographics.



**Cultural nationalism**

* **Civic-state** - Cultural nationalism will often be more selective on who can join the nation due to the expectation that they can demonstrate they have assimilated into the host nation’s culture. This can help with protecting the civic-state due to higher political and cultural alignment.
* **Culture** - Cultural nationalism will usually mean the adoption of policies that help to reinforce and preserve the nation’s own distinct culture. Although this can help to preserve some cultural aspects, it is still limited in its ability to reliably preserve the culture that was defined by the long-established ancestral population.
* **People** - Stricter policies around cultural assimilation could result in slower demographic change, but they would still not reliably preserve the continuity of the long-established ancestral population, as other people could still join the nation-state and become citizens.



**Nativist nationalism**

* **Civic-state** - In its mildest territorial form, nativist nationalism may treat people born and raised in the country as native, including the locally born children of immigrants. This can coexist with jus soli citizenship, although jus soli itself is not a nativist policy.
* **Culture** - The same policies that a cultural nationalist society adopts could also be adopted in any form of nationalism that prioritises the preservation of people. Nativist nationalism can help with preserving both the culture and the people that created that culture in the first place.
* **People** - A stricter interpretation might require someone to have roots in the country extending back several generations or at least 100 years. In its strictest implementation, nativist nationalism begins to replicate ancestral, blood or blood-and-soil nationalism. Nativism is therefore a broad and flexible category, but this can make it less clear about precisely what is being preserved.



**Racial nationalism**

* **Civic-state** - Racial identity alone does not guarantee support for the same institutions or political values. A racial category could contain many populations with substantially different civic traditions.
* **Culture** - People classified within the same race may have different languages, religions, customs and historical identities. Racial nationalism that also has cultural-preserving policies may therefore be moderately effective for preserving cultural continuity; however, this would not be the most reliable form of nationalism to preserve cultural continuity.
* **People** - In its broadest application, racial nationalism protects the continuity of a broad racial group rather than that of a distinct ancestral population. Stricter forms that focus more narrowly on a particular ancestral group would be more effective for protecting the diversity of each distinct ancestral group.



**Blood nationalism**

* **Civic-state** - Blood nationalism can help with civic-state continuity by preserving the descent group historically associated with the state and its institutions. However, common blood does not itself guarantee common political beliefs. Blood nationalism consequently preserves the biological population more directly than it preserves any particular civic-state.
* **Culture** - Culture is not inherited biologically; it must still be taught, practised and passed between generations. People of the same bloodline might assimilate into other cultures, particularly when raised abroad. Bloodline preservation alone cannot reliably guarantee cultural continuity.
* **People** - Where common blood is the primary basis of national membership, blood nationalism provides one of the strictest and potentially most reliable ways to preserve a distinct biological population. It is narrower than ancestral nationalism because it can exclude historically rooted individuals who do not share the core bloodline.



**Ancestral nationalism**

* **Civic-state** - Ancestral nationalism may help to preserve the civic-state more reliably due to preserving the continuity of the historical population that established, inherited and developed those institutions. It is more selective than civic or cultural nationalism because membership is connected to descent from the founding peoples of that nation.
* **Culture** - Preserving the continuity of the populations that created and transmitted the national culture can help with providing a strong basis for cultural preservation. However, ancestral continuity does not automatically preserve the existing culture; cultural transmission, education and participation will still be necessary.
* **People** - The broadest interpretation of ancestral nationalism includes anyone with long-established ancestral ties to the founding or historical development of the nation. This may include people who do not share the majority population’s bloodline but who have lived within the nation for centuries or near to its inception. This broader approach can reduce the risk of statelessness or permanent exclusion more effectively than strict blood nationalism.



**Blood-and-soil nationalism**

* **Civic-state** - Blood-and-soil nationalism may provide the highest likelihood of civic-state continuity because it requires both descent from the nation’s core group and rootedness in the national homeland. Neither shared blood nor territorial upbringing guarantees support for the existing political institutions and values, so civic continuity would still depend on education, institutions and political participation.
* **Culture** - The combination of descent and homeland-rootedness makes blood-and-soil nationalism particularly focused on preserving a culturally situated people. The soil requirement emphasises upbringing within the ancestral land, participation in its historical community and exposure to its culture.
* **People** - Common blood is necessary but not sufficient for full national membership. A person must also be born, raised or meaningfully rooted in the relevant nation-state. This can mean excluding not only immigrants without the required ancestry but also diaspora members who possess the ancestry but lack a formative connection to the homeland.



## **The pragmatic case for ancestral nationalism**

Ancestral nationalism is the clearest terminology for a system seeking to preserve the continuity of a distinct historical people while remaining more flexible than strict blood or blood-and-soil nationalism. It occupies a middle position among people-continuity nationalisms: it is more descent-focused than civic or cultural nationalism, but less biologically strict than blood nationalism. Ancestral nationalism can also adopt a level of strictness for ancestral rootedness in a certain place to better protect cultural continuity.

It can prioritise continuity with a historical population while accounting for the complexity of real nation-states, where founding peoples, long-settled minorities and inherited national identities do not always map neatly onto one or a few biological bloodlines. Blood nationalism would be more precise where the central concern is preserving a distinct biological lineage, while blood-and-soil nationalism is more precise where the aim is to preserve a descent group and their culture due to their roots in a particular ancestral homeland.

Stricter forms of people-continuity nationalism can raise more difficult questions about citizenship, minority status, mixed ancestry, long-settled populations and exclusion. Blood nationalism may be easier to envisage and adopt when excluded individuals have another national home that they are able to join as citizens. It becomes substantially more difficult to justify or implement when it leaves people stateless, politically unrepresented or permanently excluded from the only society to which they have any meaningful ties.

A short term alternative for populations seeking stronger protections for their continuity could consider protected cultural communities, local autonomy, devolved authority or recognised minority self-government within a wider nation-state. These arrangements could allow a distinct group to preserve its identity, institutions, traditions or demographic continuity without requiring the entire state to adopt a stricter ancestral or blood-based model.

For existing nation-states, milder nativist or ancestral-nationalist approaches could be a more pragmatic starting point than stricter variants such as blood nationalism, especially where the state previously operated on a civic and inclusive basis. Nativist or ancestral approaches could help reverse some of the demographic change without defining national membership entirely through descent or common blood.

Ancestral nationalism is the more appropriate term for the following policy: any existing citizen will remain a citizen unless it can be proven that they are more ancestrally tied to another nation. Under this policy, the burden would be placed on the nation itself to prove that any existing citizen is more ancestrally tied to another nation. With blood and blood-and-soil nationalism, the burden could be placed on each individual to prove that they are included in the same biological group. Due to the prevalence of civic nationalism across many countries today, ancestral nationalism would be the more pragmatic solution for preventing people from becoming stateless in the event that they transition over to an ancestral nationalist solution at the nation-state level.

Overall, ancestral nationalism represents a balance between creating a reliable solution for preserving a distinct population whilst also accommodating the practical realities of trying to adopt ancestral nationalism as a political system at scale. For these reasons, it is the preferable solution to recommend as a form of nationalism that is both effective for the preservation of a distinct population and pragmatic in how that could be achieved.
