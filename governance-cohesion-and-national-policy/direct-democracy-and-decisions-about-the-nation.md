# Direct democracy and decisions about the nation

Most political decisions concern how a nation is governed. Others concern who belongs to the nation and who has the power to govern it. This distinction provides a useful starting point for deciding when to use direct democracy.

Representatives can handle most government responsibilities, while citizens have a direct voice in selected foundational decisions - particularly major changes to migration, citizenship and voting arrangements. The purpose is not to replace representative government, but to identify decisions for which explicit public consent is especially important.



## **What direct democracy adds**

Direct democracy allows people to vote on a specific proposal rather than delegate the decision entirely to elected representatives. Its central advantage is that a person can express a preference independently of a party’s wider programme.

Someone might support a party’s education and economic policies while strongly disagreeing with its approach to citizenship. A general election cannot neatly communicate that distinction; a separate public vote can.

Representative democracy asks: **“Who should decide on our behalf?”**

Direct democracy asks: **“What should we decide?”**

Both have a place. The challenge is choosing which decisions benefit from direct public involvement and which are better delegated.



## **Why we cannot vote on everything**

Expecting everyone to investigate and vote on every government decision would demand too much time and attention. Some matters require specialist knowledge, confidential information or an immediate response. Others concern detailed administration rather than a meaningful choice about society’s direction. Direct democracy is most compelling where:

* The proposal and its trade-offs can be explained clearly.
* There is sufficient time for public discussion.
* Decisions are infrequent enough to avoid overwhelming participants.
* Public preferences are central to the outcome.
* The importance of direct consent justifies the effort involved.

Consequences matter too. A decision that is difficult to reverse deserves stronger scrutiny, but irreversibility alone does not make a referendum the best decision-making method.



## **Why membership decisions are different**

Migration, citizenship and voting arrangements address connected but distinct questions:

* **Migration** - Who may enter, live and settle in the country?
* **Citizenship** - Who may become a recognised member of the political community, and under what conditions?
* **Voting arrangements** - Who may participate in collective decisions, and how is political influence organised?

These decisions concern not only government policy but also the composition and future authority of the political community.

The argument for direct involvement is particularly strong here: if citizens are the source of democratic authority, they should have a direct role in major changes to the rules governing membership and participation.

Delegating these questions entirely to representatives creates a potential conflict. Those exercising power also control important rules about who can grant that power in the future.

Direct public approval can provide an additional check. It does not eliminate the need for fair procedures or protections against unjustified disenfranchisement. The underlying membership distinctions are examined in Immigration, citizenship and the rights of residents.



## **Major frameworks rather than every adjustment**

Direct democracy need not mean holding a referendum on every visa category, administrative adjustment or individual application. A public vote could instead address a clearly defined framework, such as:

* The broad direction and scale of long-term settlement.
* Whether particular residence arrangements should provide a route to citizenship.
* Major changes to naturalisation requirements.
* Fundamental changes to national voting eligibility.
* Significant transfers of authority over these matters.

Representatives and administrators could then implement the approved framework within defined limits. The important distinction is between a substantial change in national direction and ordinary decisions made within an established policy.



## **Who should participate?**

The electorate should be identified before the proposal is decided. Different decisions may involve different populations:

* National membership questions concern the national political community.
* Local infrastructure decisions may justify participation by a broader range of residents.
* Internal association decisions may concern the association’s members.
* Territorial autonomy proposals may affect both the group seeking recognition and other residents.

A vote demonstrating what a community wants does not automatically establish its authority over everyone affected. For example, a consultation among members of an ancestral, cultural or religious community can express that community’s preference for autonomy. Whether the requested public powers should be granted is a further question.



## **The difficulty of voting on voting rights**

A proposal to change the electorate creates a particular problem: who should vote on that change?

If the proposal excludes some existing voters, defining the referendum electorate according to the proposed new rules would already assume part of the outcome. The process therefore needs an independently justified basis for participation rather than allowing the proposal’s supporters simply to define the electorate most likely to approve it.

This does not prevent communities from conducting their own consultations. It distinguishes an expression of group preference from a binding decision about the wider political order. The broader issue is discussed in **Constitutional change, consent and democratic legitimacy**.



## **Evidence informs the choice; it cannot make the choice**

There is no single answer to how open a nation should be, what citizenship should require or how much continuity it should prioritise.

One society may place greater importance on openness and population growth. Another may prefer slower change, cultural continuity or more demanding membership conditions.

Economic analysis can help explain likely consequences. It cannot determine how those consequences should be weighed against other priorities. An economic benefit is an argument for a policy, not sufficient justification for overriding public preferences. Evidence nevertheless matters. Voters should distinguish between what they value and what they believe a proposal will achieve.

Preferences help determine the goals. Evidence helps assess whether the proposed policy can deliver them. Public discussion should also make uncertainty visible rather than presenting one forecast as an inevitable outcome.



## **Other compelling uses of direct democracy**



**Local infrastructure and land use**

Communities can benefit from direct choices between clearly specified projects. Proposals should explain costs, practical alternatives and effects on neighbouring areas. A local preference does not make wider consequences disappear.



**Defined portions of public budgets**

Residents could allocate a specified portion of a budget between affordable options. This is more meaningful than asking whether people want additional services without explaining their costs or what other spending would change.



**Major constitutional changes**

Changes to the rules governing public authority deserve particular consideration. However, a referendum should form part of a clear constitutional process rather than substitute for defining what the change would actually authorise.



**Education and public-service priorities**

Direct input may help establish broad priorities. Professional and operational decisions should remain distinguishable from choices about the objectives a service is expected to pursue.



## **Decisions better left to representatives and specialists**

Direct democracy is less compelling for:

* Urgent military responses.
* Detailed diplomatic negotiations.
* Technical monetary decisions.
* Routine administration.
* Specialist operational judgements.

Individual court cases should not become popularity contests. Nor should individual migration or citizenship applications be decided through public campaigns. Citizens might approve broad eligibility rules, while authorised institutions determine whether a particular applicant meets them through consistent procedures and opportunities for review.



## **Designing a meaningful public vote**

A referendum is useful only if people understand what they are deciding. The process should make clear:

* Whether the result is advisory or binding.
* Who is eligible to participate.
* What each available option would change.
* The principal costs, consequences and uncertainties.
* Which institution would implement the result.
* What legal or constitutional steps would still be required.

Questions should avoid combining several separable decisions into a single package where possible. Otherwise, voters may face the same difficulty as in a general election: accepting a policy they oppose to obtain another they support. Adequate notice, accessible information and opportunities to examine competing arguments are also important.



## **Direct democracy and safeguards**

Public approval is important, but it does not resolve every question of justification. A majority preference does not, by itself, establish that a particular restriction on citizenship, residence or political participation is appropriate.

Proposals affecting established status require separate consideration of their effects, alternatives and safeguards. Likewise, a local majority’s preference does not automatically settle a neighbouring community’s claim or authorise coercive measures. The relevant distinction is between:

* Establishing what people want.
* Deciding what authority they may legitimately exercise.
* Implementing an authorised decision fairly.

The community-level framework is developed in **Applying self-determination: powers, procedures and safeguards**.



## **Participation without constant voting**

Direct involvement can include consultation and deliberation before a binding decision. Not every request for public input needs to become a referendum.

Frequent ballots can create fatigue or repeatedly attract an unrepresentative portion of the population. The system should therefore reserve binding votes for decisions important enough to justify sustained attention.

Digital tools may make participation easier, but lower administrative costs do not remove these concerns. The practical opportunities and limitations are discussed in **Digital identity, consultation and democratic participation**.



## **Start with the decisions that define the nation**

Local projects may be the easiest place to introduce direct democracy. Ease of implementation, however, is not the same as democratic importance. If the objective is explicit consent over foundational choices, major migration, citizenship and voting arrangements make a particularly strong case.

They concern who may settle, who becomes a member and who helps determine the country’s future. This is a reasoned priority, not an objective ranking applicable in every circumstance. The proposal must still be clear, the electorate justified, alternatives workable and affected people’s rights considered.

The guiding principle is straightforward:

Use representative government for most governing, but give the public a direct and properly structured voice in major decisions about the membership and democratic authority of their political community.
