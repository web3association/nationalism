# Language, education, heritage and cultural policy

Identity-forming decisions influence how a nation understands itself and what it passes to future generations. They concern:

* The language used in public life.
* What schools teach.
* Which symbols are honoured.
* Which historical events are remembered.
* Which cultural institutions receive support.
* How religion relates to public authority.

These decisions matter because culture, heritage and identity are transmitted through institutions over time.



## **Culture, heritage and national symbols**

Cultural policy can include:

* National events.
* Sports.
* Public holidays.
* Heritage protection.
* Arts and music.
* Official symbols.
* National honours.
* Commemorations.
* State ceremonies.
* Archives.
* Museums.
* Other cultural protections.

These institutions and practices influence what the population regards as part of its shared inheritance.



**Civic nationalism**

Civic nationalists may support a common public culture and national symbols while remaining open to cultural pluralism and freedom of expression. People from different backgrounds can participate in the same political community without sharing every ancestral or cultural characteristic.



**Cultural nationalism**

Cultural nationalists place strong emphasis on preserving historical language, festivals, traditions, arts, symbols and ways of life. Cultural institutions are central to maintaining continuity between generations.



**Ancestral nationalism**

Ancestral nationalists commonly regard culture and heritage as closely connected to the historical population. They may see preserving that population as the most reliable way to protect the culture it created and sustained.



## **Language policy**

Language decisions can concern:

* Official-language status.
* The language used in schools.
* Public signage.
* Court proceedings.
* Translation services.
* Naturalisation requirements.
* Public broadcasting.
* Government communication.



**Civic nationalism**

A common language may be supported as a practical means of integration, equal participation and shared public life.



**Cultural nationalism**

Language is treated as one of the principal carriers of culture, literature, history and national identity. Its preservation is therefore a central political objective rather than merely an administrative convenience.



**Ancestral nationalism**

The historical population may be regarded as the traditional carrier of the national language. Demographic change can therefore create concern about whether that language will retain its position over time.



## **Education and curriculum**

Education is one of the most important institutions through which national identity is formed. Relevant decisions include:

* Curriculum standards.
* Teacher training.
* History education.
* Literature.
* Civic traditions.
* Language instruction.
* National heritage subjects.



**Civic nationalism**

Education should produce informed citizens who understand constitutional values, democratic institutions, rights, responsibilities and national history. Civic education can unify the population without making ancestry the basis of membership.



**Cultural nationalism**

Education should transmit language, traditions, customs, literature, historical memory and an inherited way of life. Cultural nationalists may be concerned about curricula becoming too globalised or detached from national heritage.



**Ancestral nationalism**

Education may emphasise the settlement, lineage, continuity and achievements of the historical population. Ancestral nationalists may object to curricula that present all groups as equally constitutive of the nation. They may also object where historical wrongs are emphasised in ways they believe undermine the legitimacy of the ancestral population.



## **National and local education decisions**

Not every educational decision needs to be made nationally. Local choices can include teaching methods, extracurricular activities, school events and optional community projects. However, national curriculum, history, language and civic education remain identity-forming matters.

**Civic nationalists** may allow local flexibility while maintaining common civic standards.

**Cultural nationalists** may support local cultural education where it reinforces the national heritage.

**Ancestral nationalists** may permit local autonomy in non-core subjects while retaining national authority over ancestry, history, heritage and civic identity education.



## **Religion and state authority**

Religion-state relations may concern:

* Religious education.
* Religious holidays.
* Religious dress.
* Blasphemy laws.
* Religious courts.
* Public funding.
* Secularism.
* The relationship between religious and civil law.



**Civic nationalism**

Civic nationalists may prefer a neutral civic state in which people practise religion privately while public law remains common and secular.



**Cultural nationalism**

Cultural nationalists may support a historical religion where it is closely connected to national holidays, architecture, rituals and moral traditions.



**Ancestral nationalism**

Ancestral nationalists may regard the historical religion as part of the population’s inherited identity even where many members are no longer religious.



**Religious identity and individual dissent**

A community’s historical religious identity is different from compulsory religious conformity. A population may maintain its religious institutions while protecting conscience and equal citizenship. Problems arise where full membership depends on religious adherence or government power is used to enforce observance. These questions are addressed in **Oppression, internal dissent and minority protection**.



## **Media and public broadcasting**

Media policy can include:

* Public broadcasting.
* National cultural media.
* Foreign ownership.
* Misinformation policy.
* Censorship.
* Freedom of speech.
* Internet regulation.
* Media funding.



**Civic nationalism**

Priorities may include media pluralism, informed citizenship, public debate and protection from foreign manipulation.



**Cultural nationalism**

Public media may be used to sustain the national language, stories, culture and historical memory.



**Ancestral nationalism**

Ancestral nationalists may be concerned that media controlled by non-ancestral or foreign-linked groups could reshape national identity against the preferences of the historical population.



## **Family life and cultural transmission**

Family practices can influence how culture is passed between generations. Questions about marriage, child-rearing and family norms are therefore connected to cultural continuity as well as personal life.

**Civic nationalists** may emphasise equal rights, child welfare, gender equality and stable family policy.

**Cultural nationalists** may focus on whether family norms transmit the national way of life.

**Ancestral nationalists** may attach particular importance to family formation, fertility and intermarriage because of their implications for population continuity.

The fiscal and demographic aspects of these policies are considered in **Public resources, economic policy and service capacity**.



## **Local culture, sports and recreation**

Local residents can often manage:

* Sports.
* Recreation.
* Festivals.
* Community gatherings.
* Cultural events.

**Civic nationalists** may favour pluralistic local cultural life.

**Cultural nationalists** may permit local variation while protecting historically significant traditions.

**Ancestral nationalists** may allow flexible local events while giving particular protection to heritage activities associated with the historical population.



## **Heritage and the built environment**

Culture is also affected by land use, planning and infrastructure. Historic buildings, landscapes and culturally important sites can be altered by development decisions. A technically efficient project may therefore still raise cultural questions. The planning and distributional dimensions are discussed in **Public resources, economic policy and service capacity**.



## **Cultural requirements and prohibitions**

Some states may seek to protect culture through rules restricting particular practices. The proposed uses and limitations of such prohibitions, including the difficulty of regulating private behaviour, are discussed in **Naturalisation and civic or cultural citizenship tests**. The broader problem is that culture cannot be reduced to a list of visible practices. Language, institutions, family life, habits and personal preferences all contribute to its continuity.
