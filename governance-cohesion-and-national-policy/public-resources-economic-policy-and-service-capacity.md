# Public resources, economic policy and service capacity

Distributional decisions determine how wealth, land, employment, housing and public services are allocated. They can create disputes because they determine:

* Who contributes.
* Who receives support.
* Which groups benefit.
* Which institutions are funded.
* How the costs of government are shared.

Technical and administrative decisions also matter, although they are often less directly connected to national identity than citizenship, culture or constitutional law.



## **National budgets and public spending**

National budgets may fund:

* Welfare.
* Housing.
* Healthcare.
* Education.
* Infrastructure.
* Defence.
* Culture.
* Public investment.
* Debt and borrowing commitments.



**Civic nationalism**

Civic nationalists generally support spending that benefits citizens as a whole and promotes equal opportunity. This can include redistribution between more and less successful communities.



**Cultural nationalism**

Cultural nationalists may support broad public provision while seeking sufficient funding for heritage, language, arts, cultural initiatives or historical institutions.



**Ancestral nationalism**

Ancestral nationalists may prioritise the historical population. This is particularly relevant where they believe that population disproportionately subsidises other communities or loses access to housing, education or services.



## **Taxation**

Tax policy determines how revenue is raised, who contributes, which activities are taxed and who receives relief. Taxes can include:

* Income tax.
* Land tax.
* Consumption tax.
* Corporate tax.
* Capital gains tax.
* Inheritance tax.
* Tariffs.
* Wealth tax.
* Corrective taxes.
* Other levies.

Although technically complex, taxation also reflects judgements about fairness, ownership, contribution, productivity, redistribution and national priorities.



**Civic nationalism**

A civic approach generally applies common legal rules and funds public goods for the political community. Fairness may involve:

* Ability to pay.
* Equal opportunity.
* Universal services.
* Social mobility.
* Poverty reduction.

It would generally avoid ancestry-based distinctions and fund shared institutions, infrastructure, education, healthcare, security and civic participation.



**Cultural nationalism**

A cultural approach may accept many of the same fairness principles while asking whether taxation supports the national way of life. It may favour relief, grants or spending for:

* Culturally important families and institutions.
* Historic towns.
* Religious or cultural sites.
* Traditional industries.
* National media.
* Language preservation.
* Heritage.
* Local businesses.

Fairness is understood not only through equal individual treatment but also through maintaining inherited cultural life.



**Ancestral nationalism**

An ancestral approach assesses taxation according to its effects on the historical population. Concerns may include whether:

* That population is disproportionately taxed to support newer communities.
* Public benefits flow away from it.
* Foreign owners, migrants or external investors accumulate wealth while it bears the tax burden.

Preferred policies may protect access to housing, land, family formation, ownership and intergenerational wealth. In this view, a fair system protects the material continuity and self-determination of the ancestral population rather than treating every resident or group as equally entitled to accumulated national resources.



## **Economic and industrial policy**

Economic policy shapes growth, innovation, ownership and labour demand. Relevant decisions include:

* Growth strategy.
* Industrial policy.
* Competition law.
* Business regulation.
* Foreign ownership.
* Innovation.
* Labour demand.

**Civic nationalists** prioritise benefits for citizens as a whole and cohesion through opportunity.

**Cultural nationalists** may support culturally important industries and resist forms of globalisation or consumer capitalism that weaken local traditions.

**Ancestral nationalists** may seek to retain wealth, land, jobs and assets under historical-population control. They may accept migrant labour while prioritising the ancestral population for employment, ownership and leadership.



## **Housing, settlement and regional development**

Housing and planning decisions influence:

* Access to homes.
* Demographic concentration.
* Assimilation.
* Segregation.
* Local identity.

Relevant policies include social housing, urban planning, refugee settlement and regional development.

**Civic nationalists** may allocate housing according to need and equal citizenship.

**Cultural nationalists** may be concerned about settlement patterns creating parallel societies and weakening assimilation.

**Ancestral nationalists** may be concerned that the historical population is priced out of important areas or that migration changes the demographic character of towns, cities and regions.



## **Land use and zoning**

Land-use decisions may involve:

* Housing density.
* Commercial areas.
* Religious buildings.
* Heritage.
* Parks.
* Industrial land.
* Agriculture.
* Urban expansion.

Although commonly local, they can have national implications.

**Civic nationalists** may focus on supply, public need and equal treatment.

**Cultural nationalists** may prioritise historic architecture, landscapes and cultural sites.

**Ancestral nationalists** may be concerned about changes to the demographic or cultural character of ancestral towns and regions.



## **Labour markets and employment**

Employment policy concerns:

* Worker protection.
* Workplace safety.
* Labour relations.
* Wages.
* Unions.
* Migrant labour.
* Occupational licensing.
* Anti-discrimination rules.

**Civic nationalists** generally support fair protection for workers and equal opportunity among citizens.

**Cultural nationalists** may support local industries, traditional skills and community stability.

**Ancestral nationalists** may prioritise the historical population for jobs, apprenticeships, ownership and leadership before opportunities are offered externally.



## **Healthcare and public health**

Public health policy may cover:

* Access to treatment.
* Vaccination.
* Elderly care.
* Disability support.
* Pandemic responses.
* Genetic screening.
* Mental health.
* Chronic disease prevention.
* General health rules.

**Civic nationalists** generally favour systems serving citizens equally.

**Cultural nationalists** may focus on health practices affecting family structures, social norms or cultural continuity.

**Ancestral nationalists** may want healthcare and family policy to support the long-term wellbeing of the historical population.

The specific rights of migrant residents are considered in **Basic rights, public services and contribution-based benefits**.



## **Family and demographic policy**

Family policy can influence both everyday welfare and long-term population continuity. Relevant decisions include:

* Marriage.
* Divorce.
* Inheritance.
* Adoption.
* Child benefits.
* Parental leave.
* Childcare.
* Reproductive technology.
* Abortion.
* Family reunification.

**Civic nationalists** may prioritise equal rights, child welfare, gender equality and stable family support.

**Cultural nationalists** may assess whether family norms sustain the nation’s culture.

**Ancestral nationalists** may be particularly concerned with fertility, intermarriage and family formation because of their effects on ancestral continuity.



## **Census categories and demographic information**

The categories used by the state shape its understanding of:

* Ancestry.
* Ethnicity.
* Religion.
* Language.
* Nationality.
* Citizenship.

**Civic nationalists** may prefer categories centred on citizenship, residence and equal legal status.

**Cultural nationalists** may seek information about language, religion and cultural practices.

**Ancestral nationalists** may seek detailed information about ancestry, fertility, migration, intermarriage and citizenship to track continuity of the historical population.



## **Infrastructure standards and engineering**

Many infrastructure decisions can be evaluated through technical expertise and common standards. Examples include:

* Sewage.
* Water.
* Drainage.
* Aviation.
* Power generation.
* Construction.
* Transport safety.
* Agricultural systems.
* Food systems.
* Utilities.

**Civic nationalists** may prioritise safety, efficiency and equal access.

**Cultural nationalists** may be less concerned with technical standards when designs detrimentally affect heritage, architecture or public culture.

**Ancestral nationalists** may accept non-ancestral participation where the work is transparent, technical and aligned with public need.



## **National infrastructure**

Major infrastructure includes:

* Highways.
* Rail networks.
* Airports.
* Ports.
* Bridges.
* Water systems.
* Energy grids.
* Telecommunications.

It can be assessed through engineering expertise, economic evidence and public need. However, infrastructure can become security-sensitive where it affects sovereignty or external control.

**Civic nationalists** prioritise national benefit and equal access.

**Cultural nationalists** may consider effects on historical towns, landscapes and cultural sites.

**Ancestral nationalists** may accept expert technical management while retaining strategic oversight to ensure that any new pieces of infrastructure are aligned with the historical population’s interests.



## **Standards, regulation and consumer protection**

Technical regulation can include:

* Food safety.
* Product standards.
* Aviation rules.
* Consumer protection.
* Environmental standards.
* Workplace safety.
* Business regulation.

**Civic nationalists** generally favour universal standards for citizens and residents.

**Cultural nationalists** may seek protection for traditional industries, food practices or customs where relevant.

**Ancestral nationalists** may be less concerned about ancestry in these roles where rules are transparent, accountable and applied equally.



## **Science, technology and innovation**

Scientific and technological investment often requires specialist knowledge. Areas include:

* Medicine.
* Engineering.
* Artificial intelligence.
* Defence technology.
* Energy.
* Agriculture.
* Biotechnology.
* Computing.

**Civic nationalists** may support research improving welfare and competitiveness.

**Cultural nationalists** may support innovation while resisting changes that undermine culture or cohesion.

**Ancestral nationalists** may support expert-led innovation where it benefits the nation without transferring strategic advantage to external groups.



## **Local budgets and services**

Local budgets may support:

* Council services.
* Roads.
* Waste collection.
* Parks.
* Libraries.
* Community services.
* Maintenance.

**Civic nationalists** may support participation by local citizens or residents.

**Cultural nationalists** may allow local flexibility within national cultural priorities.

**Ancestral nationalists** may permit resident participation where decisions do not threaten ancestral continuity or national identity.



## **Local infrastructure**

Local facilities may include:

* Streets and sidewalks.
* Community halls.
* Bridges.
* Waste facilities.
* Parks and playgrounds.
* Libraries.
* Public toilets.
* Markets.
* Fire stations.
* Clinics.
* Schools.
* Sports fields.
* Recreation facilities.

These are commonly used by all residents and can often be governed through broader local participation.

**Civic nationalists** favour equal access and local democracy.

**Cultural nationalists** may protect historically significant buildings and sites.

**Ancestral nationalists** may permit wider participation in routine infrastructure while retaining oversight of heritage-related assets.



## **Capacity and contribution**

Resources and infrastructure have both financial and practical limits. A person may contribute fairly while still adding demand to an overstretched service. Conversely, restricting access to some services can create broader costs, such as untreated health problems or unreported exploitation.

The detailed proposed distinctions between minimum rights, reciprocal access and capacity-constrained benefits are set out in **Basic rights, public services and contribution-based benefits**.



## **National priorities and technical administration**

Some decisions are primarily questions of political priority: who receives support, which communities are protected and what outcomes matter most. Others are primarily questions of implementation: how to build safely, manage efficiently or apply standards consistently. A workable system needs to recognise both.

Technical competence does not decide every question of fairness, while political preference does not replace engineering, medical or administrative expertise.
