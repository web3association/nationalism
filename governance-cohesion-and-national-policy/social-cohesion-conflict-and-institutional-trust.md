# Social cohesion, conflict and institutional trust

Populations can differ in many ways. Some differences may create a higher likelihood of disputes or conflict. Others may be less disruptive but still affect whether people want to live together.

A historical population may prefer not to accept particular differences even where they are unlikely to produce serious conflict. The value of examining differences is to consider how they might affect daily life and governance as demographics change.



## **Differences and collective preferences**

Some differences can diminish through assimilation. Others can persist. The possibility of assimilation does not mean a population must accept migration. Even where groups are similar, a nation may prefer not to admit additional residents. Its population can decide whom it wants to live with. The relevant questions include:

* Which differences are present?
* How might they affect daily life?
* Could they produce disputes?
* What happens if they become more prominent?
* Would the historical population accept them becoming the dominant pattern?

Assimilation and integration are discussed separately in **Assimilation, integration and multiculturalism**.



## **Crime and public safety**

Differences in crime rates and types of offending can create serious conflict. Relevant issues include gang activity, domestic violence, fraud, public disorder and attitudes toward police. Crime rates may be affected by group differences, but they can also be affected by:

* Age structure.
* Sex ratios.
* Poverty.
* Trauma.
* Urbanisation.
* Policing practices.
* Legal status.
* Social networks.

These factors influence how crime differences should be understood.



## **Political preferences**

Groups may differ in their preferences concerning:

* Liberal or conservative values.
* Secularism or religiosity.
* Individualism or collectivism.
* Short-term or long-term priorities.
* Authoritarianism.
* Democracy.
* Restrictions on speech.

As population shares change, these differences can alter voting power and majority-minority relationships. Differences may also concern political participation, public-service use, infrastructure priorities, patronage and in-group favouritism.



## **Religion**

Religious traditions and movements can have political dimensions. They may seek to influence:

* Law.
* Education.
* Family policy.
* Dress codes.
* Blasphemy rules.
* The relationship between religion and the state.

Differences may also involve sexuality, proselytising, public religious expression, apostasy and radicalisation. Conflict is more likely where religious law is treated as superior to civil law or where dissent, apostasy and criticism are not tolerated.



## **Work ethic and economic outcomes**

Groups may differ in willingness or ambition to work, producing differences in access to jobs and business opportunities. Unequal outcomes can generate wealth gaps. Groups may also differ in how they interpret success and failure, or in their willingness to rely on state welfare. These differences can contribute to disputes about contribution, fairness and public support.



## **Historical grievances**

Past events may affect relationships between groups. These include wars, colonisation, oppression, genocide and expulsions. Communities may retain distrust because of what another group did historically. They may also bring competing accounts of history and victimhood into a shared society.



## **Diaspora politics and imported conflicts**

Migrant communities may retain connections to contemporary foreign conflicts, independence movements or sectarian disputes. Those conflicts can reappear through:

* Protests.
* Lobbying.
* Intimidation.
* Fundraising.
* Foreign influence campaigns.
* Tensions between diaspora groups.

The security and sovereignty dimensions are considered further in **Security, foreign influence and public accountability**.



## **Intelligence**

Differences in intelligence between groups could contribute to:

* Skills gaps.
* Group dominance in particular industries.
* Unequal wealth outcomes.
* Uneven distributions of skills across economic sectors.

Lower intelligence can be associated with higher crime and impulsivity, and greater vulnerability to misinformation, exploitation and poor decision-making. Differences in reproduction, migration and acceptance of cousin marriage could contribute to a decline in the population’s average intelligence over time. A decline could make other issues, including crime, work ethic and politics, increasingly problematic. Lower intelligence does not necessarily create disputes directly. However, it can produce conditions in which problems arise more frequently and lead to conflict.



## **Gender norms and child welfare**

Differences in views about gender and family authority can create conflict where cultural or religious expectations contradict the host society’s legal standards. Relevant issues include:

* Women’s rights.
* Domestic authority.
* Forced marriage.
* Child marriage.
* Honour-based abuse.
* Female genital mutilation.
* Divorce rights.
* Inheritance.
* Girls’ education.
* LGBT rights.
* Parental authority.



## **Clan, caste, tribal and kinship structures**

Some communities organise life strongly around extended families, clans, castes, tribes or lineages. These structures can provide mutual aid. They can also produce:

* Nepotism.
* Endogamy.
* Communal voting.
* Pressure on individuals.
* Arranged-marriage networks.
* Revenge conflicts.
* Distrust of neutral institutions.

The same structure may therefore support internal cooperation while complicating wider institutional relationships.



## **Foreign influence and transnational repression**

External states, parties, religious authorities or organisations may:

* Fund community institutions.
* Influence media.
* Monitor dissidents.
* Pressure diaspora communities.
* Mobilise residents for foreign political objectives.

These activities can create sovereignty and security concerns. They may also affect people within the diaspora who disagree with the external authority.



## **Language and communication**

Differences in language, dialect and communication style affect:

* Schools.
* Courts.
* Public services.
* Employment.
* Everyday interaction.

Communication failures can produce misunderstanding and disputes. A shared language can make cooperation easier, although other differences may remain.



## **Parallel societies and segregation**

Groups may concentrate in particular neighbourhoods or regions and maintain separate social networks. Such arrangements can reinforce distinct identities and practices. They may also create parallel societies and produce disputes with the host population.



## **Differences that may be less conflict-prone**

Other differences may be easier to tolerate but still influence preferences about association and settlement. Their cumulative effect may lead a population to accept or reject migration from particular groups. Any of these differences could contribute to conflict, but they may be less likely to do so than the issues above.



**Identity and physical traits**

Differences in ancestry, racial or ethnic identity and physical appearance can matter to populations seeking continuity as a distinct group. Some people may prefer to live among others who share a similar identity or extended ancestry.



**Heritage**

Traditions, rituals, festivals, performing arts, music and dance may differ. A population with less familiarity or connection to the historical nation’s heritage may place a different value on preserving it. Demographic change can therefore affect the long-term support for inherited cultural practices.



**Social norms**

Differences can involve:

* Greetings.
* Gift-giving.
* Humour.
* Storytelling.
* Gestures.
* Eye contact.
* Facial expressions.
* Touch.
* Noise.
* Personal space.
* Punctuality.
* Queueing.
* Etiquette.
* Dress.
* Customs.
* Honour and shame.
* Privacy.
* Politeness.
* Public behaviour.

These may affect everyday comfort and familiarity even when they do not cause major disputes.



**Beliefs**

Groups may differ in:

* Mythologies.
* Rituals.
* Morality.
* Taboos.
* Afterlife beliefs.



**Religious practices**

Beyond religion’s political dimensions, communities may differ in worship, dress and religious holidays.



**Family expectations**

Differences may concern:

* Marriage.
* Gender roles.
* Whether to have children.
* Family size.
* Care of elderly relatives.
* Cousin marriage.
* Inheritance.
* Nuclear or extended-family structures.
* Burial practices.
* Women’s roles in domestic and public life.



**Dating and relationships**

Groups may have different expectations concerning courtship, sexuality and promiscuity. They may also differ in preferences for relationships within their own group or with particular other groups.



**Authority and law**

Communities may have different attitudes toward hierarchy, government and rule-following. A population with historical distrust of institutions may resist integration programmes or law enforcement.



**Legal and civic expectations**

Differences may concern:

* Rule of law.
* Corruption.
* Bribery.
* Bureaucracy.
* Civil, religious and customary law.
* Citizenship and loyalty.
* Military service.
* Taxation.
* Jury duty.
* Public obligations.



**Working practices**

Differences can include:

* Punctuality.
* Work-life balance.
* Productivity.
* Innovation.
* Risk-taking.
* Work habits.
* Entrepreneurship.



**External loyalties**

People may differ in their loyalties to other states and involvement in foreign politics or conflicts. They may also receive funding from external governments or organisations.



**Educational preferences**

Differences may concern:

* The language of instruction.
* Curriculum.
* Historical narratives.
* Religion in schools.
* Gender norms.
* Segregated or integrated schooling.



**Food**

Groups may differ in cuisine, hospitality, dietary requirements and preparation methods, including halal or kosher practices.



**Leisure and entertainment**

Preferences may differ across media, music, art, recreation, games and sports.



**Health**

Differences may include immunity, vaccination attitudes and the prevalence of endemic diseases.



**Institutional trust**

Political systems can generate trust through different mechanisms. An individual-leaning system relies heavily on predictable rules, equal rights, impartial institutions and enforcement. A collective-leaning system may rely more on identity, shared obligations and a sense of belonging. Neither approach is free of risks. Institutions lose trust when they serve only powerful interests or fail to apply rules fairly. Shared identity loses its unifying force when it excludes dissenters, pressures conformity or produces suspicion of outsiders. The detailed comparison appears in **Individual rights and collective interests**.



**Cohesion and times of crisis**

Social arrangements that function during stable periods may become strained during economic collapse, serious unrest, international conflict and rapid demographic change. Questions of loyalty, mutual support and willingness to defend the country may become more prominent. Cultural or political differences that were manageable in ordinary conditions can become more contentious under pressure. This is one reason the long-term implications of group differences matter alongside their immediate effects.



## **Understanding differences before making decisions**

It is difficult to understand every difference between populations in advance. Some become apparent only through living together. Nevertheless, a population benefits from considering both current differences and how their importance may change with demographic growth. The purpose is not only to identify immediate conflict. It is also to decide which changes to identity, public life and daily experience the existing population is willing to accept.
