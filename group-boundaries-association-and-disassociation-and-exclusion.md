# Group boundaries, association and disassociation and exclusion

A useful way to think about nations and nationalism is through three related concepts: boundaries, association and disassociation and exclusion.

Boundaries establish what a group is, what it exists to preserve and how membership works. Association and disassociation concern how people enter, participate in and leave a group, and whether the group itself can end an association. Exclusion means that membership or participation is not automatically available to everyone.

These concepts help explain why a group might decline an arrangement that offers financial or practical benefits. Groups often care about more than efficiency or economic outcomes. They may also value intimacy, shared history, founding principles, ancestral and cultural continuity and retaining control over their future.



## **Couples**

Imagine a couple living together in a two-bedroom home. A friend offers to rent the spare bedroom. The friend is sociable, would pay a good rent and would gladly share the cooking and cleaning.

The couple declines. They like their friend, but they prefer to live alone together. Privacy, intimacy and having a shared domestic space matter more to them than the additional income or help around the house.



**Boundaries**

The couple’s boundary protects the particular relationship and household they want to maintain. They can speak privately, develop their own routines and spend time together without accommodating another resident.

Their decision does not require them to believe that their friend is unpleasant or unworthy. Even an excellent housemate would change the character of their home.

Boundaries make it possible to distinguish between liking someone, enjoying their company, and wanting to share a household with them.



**Association and disassociation**

Each person must freely choose to participate in the relationship, and either can end it. The couple can also choose to associate with other people as friends or housemates without making those people participants in their romantic relationship.

An existing relationship does not entitle one partner to prevent the other from leaving. Equally, an outsider cannot become a partner merely by wanting to join: a change in the relationship requires the consent of those involved.

Ending an association can still create responsibilities. Separation may involve property, tenancy arrangements, or duties toward children. The ability to disassociate does not mean that every consequence disappears immediately.



**Exclusion**

A monogamous romantic relationship excludes other romantic partners by definition. The household may also exclude additional residents.

This exclusion serves a purpose. Without agreed limits, the couple could face unwanted complexity, conflicting expectations, and a loss of privacy. Other relationship structures are possible, but they also require boundaries and consent.

The friend can genuinely become part of the household if the couple agrees, but cannot claim membership simply because their presence would be beneficial.



## **Private Groups**

Imagine a group of friends who met at university and have continued holding fortnightly games nights for many years. They share memories, familiar routines, and jokes arising from their time together.

They have recently befriended someone whom everyone likes. They invite this person to some parties and outings, but keep certain games nights for the original group.



**Boundaries**

The boundary protects a particular shared experience. The group values being able to revisit its history without explaining every reference or changing the character of every gathering.

Different boundaries can apply to different occasions. A large party might welcome new people, while an annual reunion remains limited to those who shared the original experience.

This allows the friends to maintain an established connection without refusing all new relationships.



**Association and disassociation**

Individuals can form friendships with newcomers, invite them to events, and gradually include them in the wider group. Members can also withdraw, stop attending, or end particular friendships.

The group can stop inviting someone whose conduct repeatedly undermines trust or enjoyment. Unlike a formal organisation, it may have no official membership process, so association and disassociation often happen gradually rather than through an explicit decision.

Neither an individual’s wish to join nor a group’s wish to retain someone can create a friendship without mutual willingness.



**Exclusion**

Being welcome at an event does not automatically make someone a member of every friendship circle represented there.

A newcomer can genuinely become part of the present-day friend group. However, they cannot retroactively become someone who attended university with its original members. There remains a historical distinction between sharing the group’s earlier experiences and joining its life later.

Exclusion can therefore preserve particular occasions or relationships without making the group permanently closed. Shared history cannot be reassigned, but new shared history can develop.



## **Organisations**

Imagine an organic farm founded around a commitment to organic production. A group of skilled farmers offers to join and substantially increase output, but only if the business adopts synthetic fertilisers and production methods inconsistent with its organic standards.

The farm rejects the proposal. Higher output and profits are attractive, but they do not outweigh the purpose for which the organisation was established.



**Boundaries**

Organisational boundaries protect a mission, standards, and ways of working. For this farm, organic production is not simply an incidental business practice: it is part of its identity and its promise to customers.

The founders might value environmental stewardship, particular production methods, or meeting organic certification requirements. Whatever their reasons, a proposal that abandons those commitments changes the organisation rather than merely improving its efficiency.

Clear boundaries help employees and customers understand what the organisation stands for.



**Association and disassociation**

People can apply to work for the farm, and the organisation can decide whether their skills and approach fit its needs. Applicants can accept or decline an offer.

Employees can resign, and employers can end employment subject to contracts, employment law, and fair procedures. An organisation can also decline partnerships or terminate them under agreed terms.

This capacity to associate and disassociate helps protect the organisation’s functioning. However, it is not unlimited: protecting a mission does not remove obligations concerning discrimination, notice, wages, or other legal protections.



**Exclusion**

Organisations ordinarily exclude people from internal roles unless they pass an appropriate hiring or approval process. An interested person cannot simply declare themselves an employee, manager, or director.

This exclusion protects competence, accountability, confidential information and organisational culture. Without it, the organisation would struggle to coordinate its work or uphold its standards.

Unlike a shared historical experience, organisational membership can usually be acquired in full. A newcomer who is hired becomes a genuine employee, even though they were not present at the organisation’s founding.



## **Nation-States**

Imagine a prosperous country of ten million people with an ageing population and shortages in several industries. A million people abroad would like to migrate there, potentially filling vacancies and improving their own circumstances.

After public debate, the country chooses a lower level of immigration. It accepts some additional economic costs because it places greater weight on housing capacity, integration, institutional stability and cultural continuity. This is a boundary decision, but it is not the same kind of decision a couple or a firm makes. A state already governs everyone inside its territory, and its choices bind people who did not agree to the boundary.

Private exclusion does not require a public justification to the excluded person. A couple need not explain to a friend why they will not share a home. A friend group need not defend a closed games night. An employer need not hire every capable applicant. Those relationships are limited, voluntary and easy to decline. A state is different. It is a territorial jurisdiction with coercive authority. Most of its members were born into it rather than chosen for it. Residents who are not citizens still live under its law. Internal minorities did not each consent to the admission rule a majority prefers. Refusal of entry can be legitimate, but it is a public act and has to be argued as one: capacity, continuity, consent of the existing citizenry, and obligations the state already has. It is not self-justifying in the same way a household boundary is.



**Boundaries**

A nation-state’s boundaries determine jurisdiction, admission, residence and citizenship. They also help sustain the institutions through which people govern themselves.

A population may value a common language, inherited traditions, public trust and continuity of ancestry and culture between generations. The scale and pace of immigration can legitimately be debated against those priorities, alongside economic needs and humanitarian responsibilities. A nation-state also contains people with different preferences. Its policies have to account for that internal diversity. One political majority is not the only authentic expression of the nation, and it is not a functional requirement that the state adopt either a fully ancestral or a fully civic definition of membership.

What carries over from the smaller cases is not an unlimited group right. It is a narrower point: a polity that cannot control entry, or that treats every economic gain as decisive, cannot reliably preserve the conditions under which its institutions and way of life continue.



**Association and disassociation**

In the same way that couples, friendship groups and organisations benefit from being able to associate and disassociate from people, nation-states can also benefit from this. It cannot easily be known ahead of time whether a new group will be able to properly assimilate into the culture of a given nation-state. Something that might have worked in the short-term might become increasingly problematic over time as demographics change.

If nations are not able to disassociate from groups they would prefer not to live with the opportunity for ongoing tensions and conflict could increase over time. Those differences could manifest into political advocacy for changes that embrace one groups preferences over others. In some cases a compromised solution can be achieved to accommodate for both groups but this does not always create mutually acceptable outcomes. Indigenous populations or groups that have a stronger ancestral tie to the territory and nation-state could take precedence in any decisions around who can stay and whom should leave when decisions are made around disassociation.

Citizenship is the foundation of a person’s political rights and secure place of residence, not merely membership in a voluntary club. Allowing governments to discard citizens whenever political preferences change would make everyone’s status less secure. So decisions around disassociation cannot be made lightly or without a meaningful justification. A distinct ancestral group may want to reclaim their sovereignty and self-determination so that other groups do not have an influence over how they govern themselves. This could be a reasonable justification for that group to disassociate with other groups that they previously associated with in the same political system.



**Exclusion**

Nation-states ordinarily exclude non-citizens from automatic entry, settlement, citizenship and some political rights. Admission requires permission or an applicable legal entitlement.

Such exclusion can help a country manage population change, maintain institutional capacity, and support ancestral or cultural continuity. These are legitimate subjects for democratic policy, although border control remains subject to applicable legal obligations, including protections for refugees.

It is also important to distinguish ancestral membership from civic membership. No one can acquire someone else’s ancestry or retroactively share their family history. A newcomer can nevertheless learn a language, participate in traditions and form lasting attachments. This does not however mean that other groups need to be given a path to become equal members that can apply for citizenship. These decisions around who is excluded and who can associate will need to be handled by each nation.



## The value of boundaries, association and disassociation and exclusion

All of these examples illustrate the value of setting boundaries and making membership conditional rather than automatically available to everyone. Groups can also change over time: they may want to associate with new people or disassociate from existing members. Not everyone will be happy with the outcomes, but this does not diminish the importance of being able to make those changes when they are proportionate and justified.

Some of the most successful and productive relationships, friendship groups, professional sports teams and international corporations maintain clear boundaries and exclude most other people from membership by default. This does not necessarily reflect distrust or dislike of outsiders. Instead, these groups want to preserve what they have built, look after their members and remain functional or productive. Individuals can leave these groups, and the groups can also end particular associations when membership is no longer working as expected.

The importance of these boundaries becomes clearer when we consider what would happen without them. A romantic relationship or friendship group that anyone could join, a sports team that accepted every applicant, or an organisation that allowed anyone to work and receive wages would struggle to maintain its intended character and purpose. Differences in communication, expectations and performance would become harder to manage. Highly functioning teams generally have clear requirements governing who can join and how members are expected to behave and perform. The highest-performing teams are often particularly selective, seeking the most skilled talent available.

Initial selection is not always sufficient, however. Circumstances change, expectations may not be met, and problems can emerge that were not apparent when an association began. Groups therefore benefit not only from controlling admission but also from being able to reconsider existing arrangements. Without the ability to associate and disassociate, they may struggle to correct problems that undermine their purpose or functioning.

These examples help explain why boundaries also matter for nation-states, although the responsibilities involved are greater. Unlike couples, private groups or organisations, states exercise authority over where and how people can live. That difference affects how exclusion and disassociation can be justified and carried out. It does not, however, negate the importance of boundaries, control over national membership or the ability to change political arrangements when they are no longer working.

The criticism of progressive liberalism here is that its emphasis on equality can lead it to overlook differences between people and the effects those differences may have on a group. Differences in identity, culture and capabilities can affect national cohesion and productivity. An ideology that gives insufficient attention to those differences does not make their effects disappear; those effects may instead become more visible over time. Boundaries and the ability to associate and disassociate are therefore important tools for addressing problems that such an approach may fail to anticipate or acknowledge.

The broader lesson is not that nation-states are identical to smaller groups. It is that the interests illustrated by those smaller groups like preserving their character, maintaining shared expectations and responding when arrangements stop working also matter at the national level. The greater responsibilities of states mean that these tools require greater justification, not that these underlying group dynamics and interests cease to matter.



## **Hidden individual impact in larger groups**

One problem that is more acute in large groups is scale. A small number of migrants working in another country may barely register. If most people do not encounter the change, it is easy to treat each case as too small to matter.

The difficulty is that admission is often path-dependent. If migrants have a route to permanent residence and citizenship, today’s flow becomes part of the future electorate and the future culture. Differences that were easy to miss at the start can matter more once the numbers are larger, and the resulting settlement is hard to reverse without denaturalisation. That is a reason to decide the boundary before the demographic change accumulates, to avoid the problems of unwinding membership later.

This is why the composition of immigration is an important and ongoing subject of public argument. If differences in institutions, expectations or fertility are poorly understood, a civic order can admit people faster than it can integrate them. Once those differences are on the table, the ordinary tools of admission numbers, selection rules and the terms of settlement may not be sufficient to prevent substantial amounts of national change. A state ultimately needs some way to end particular associations.



## **Preference-based solutions**

Private groups often need not justify their boundaries to the people they exclude. A couple, a friend group, or a firm may refuse an association because of what it is trying to preserve, including an ancestral identity or a way of life. Outsiders cannot compel entry by pointing to mutual gain.

A nation-state can act on similar preferences. An electorate that does not want large-scale migration from particular places or cultures can say so and can legislate admission rules accordingly. Preventing it from doing that, in the name of people who are not yet members, subordinates the existing citizenry’s control of its own membership to an open-ended duty to associate. That is recipe for political unrest and disaster.

People from the same ancestral group can also have different preferences. Some people might want to live amongst other groups that have different ancestries and cultural backgrounds. Others might prefer to live amongst their own distinct group to more reliably preserve the continuity of their way of life. Neither of these preferences need to be suppressed by the state and a majority preference for one of these preferred ways of living does not need to prevent the other group from expressing and living their life in a way that better matches their own preference. A majority advocating for one of these preferences does not entitle the state to stop dissenters from associating privately, clustering voluntarily or arguing for different local rules. Nor does it entitle the majority to remove the minority. Some of the available tools to better handle these differences include immigration policy, regional or local variation and voluntary associations.

The preferences of each group can result in boundaries and policies being defined around who is excluded. But it should also be noted that any group can make decisions that end up being a mistake. An initial decision to associate with certain individuals and groups might not go as planned. Nation-states and groups within nation-states need a way to both associate and disassociate with others as otherwise these groups can be forced to live in a way that can erode their ancestral or cultural continuity. A balance is needed between protecting both individual rights and collective rights so that historical groups can be protected but individuals are also not unnecessarily excluded or made stateless.
