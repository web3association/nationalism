# Basic rights, public services and contribution-based benefits

Countries may benefit from migration because of labour shortages, skills gaps or demographic pressures. They may also admit migrants because of historical relationships or collective preferences.

If migrants are invited to live and work in a country, the rights they receive affect whether they can lead productive and fulfilling lives. This page primarily concerns lawfully admitted temporary and long-term migrants, particularly labour migrants.



## **A minimum rights floor**

Some protections should apply to everyone within the state’s jurisdiction, regardless of citizenship or fiscal contribution. These include:

* Protection from violence.
* Access to police and courts.
* Emergency medical care.
* Due process.
* Protection from forced labour.
* Basic workplace protections.
* Protection from arbitrary detention.
* The ability to report crime or exploitation without losing basic legal protection.

These protections benefit migrants and the host society by supporting public order, public health and rule of law.



## **Equal treatment**

Equal treatment means applying the same rules, prices, procedures and conditions when migrants use services or infrastructure. It concerns formal equality. It may leave migrants responsible for managing differences themselves. For example, someone who struggles with the national language may need to improve their understanding or arrange translation independently.



## **Equal access and effective benefit**

Equal access and effective benefit go beyond being subject to identical rules. They concern whether a person can actually use a service successfully and obtain a comparable practical benefit. Different people may require additional assistance, conditions or pricing to achieve this. The state may need to provide accommodations that make the service meaningfully usable. For example:

* Giving a citizen and migrant the same MRI process, report and price is equal treatment.
* Providing translation, understandable information and follow-up support may help achieve equal access and effective benefit.

A state may consider formal equal treatment sufficient. Other systems may provide additional accommodations where capacity and public support permit, or where legal obligations require them.



## **Reciprocal equal treatment**

Treating migrants exactly like citizens is not necessarily fair where services are heavily subsidised by existing taxpayers. New arrivals may receive benefits before making substantial contributions through taxes, fees, insurance or longer-term economic participation. This can create fiscal imbalance or free-riding concerns.

Low prices and broad eligibility may also increase demand for subsidised services. Reciprocal equal treatment seeks to distribute the burden of contribution more fairly among users. Migrants could contribute through taxation, insurance, fees or direct payments. Some services may still be collectively funded because they protect wider public interests. A migrant’s economic contribution may also include filling a position no one else can fill. That value could justify reducing the direct fiscal contribution required for particular services.



## **Capacity-constrained reciprocal equal treatment**

Comparable contributions do not eliminate capacity problems. Differences in prices and subsidies between countries can affect service demand, especially where movement is easy and people can compare systems. Additional demand may reduce availability for citizens and established residents. Services can also become constrained through underinvestment, workforce shortages, policy changes and demographic trends unrelated to migration.

A formally reciprocal arrangement may therefore remain unsustainable in practice. Capacity-constrained reciprocal equal treatment retains reciprocal principles while limiting access according to what the system can provide without crowding out existing residents. Possible adjustments include eligibility, availability, pricing and contribution requirements.

Such changes could discourage non-essential use during sustained capacity shortages. They would need to be assessed for administrative feasibility and possible harms, including whether they discourage necessary care or other essential use.



## **Employment opportunities**

Some jobs involve sensitive information, strategic systems or authority over important national decisions. These commonly require citizenship, residence qualifications, security clearance or other vetting. Equal access to every security-sensitive role may therefore be inappropriate.

A more realistic baseline is fair access, subject to immigration status, to non-security-sensitive work that allows migrants to support themselves. This helps make the country an attractive place to live and work.



## **Essential public services**

Migrants may need services involving:

* Identity and immigration.
* Healthcare.
* Education.
* Justice and public safety.
* Employment.
* Business and taxation.
* Transport.
* Consumer protection.
* Family support.
* Disability and ageing.
* Emergencies.

Some should be available without a prior financial contribution because denying them could produce greater public harm. Examples include health screening that prevents disease outbreaks. Similar arguments can apply to policing, emergency services, labour-rights enforcement and court access. Underuse of these services may create costs for the wider population.



## **Essential infrastructure**

People living in a country may need access to:

* Water and sewage.
* Electricity.
* Transport networks.
* Waste collection and disposal.
* Communication networks.
* Public spaces.
* Government buildings.

These provide the practical foundations for lawful and productive daily life.



## **Rental housing**

Migrants need fair access to rental housing. Eligibility may still depend on immigration status, documentation and applicable housing law.



## **Goods and services**

In ordinary consumer markets, migrants should generally be able to buy the same goods and services as others, subject to the same general legal restrictions. These transactions are normally reciprocal because customers pay. Although goods can become scarce, it would usually be unfair and impractical to prevent migrants from buying a product simply because stock is low.



## **Housing ownership**

Access to housing does not necessarily require ownership. A nation may restrict home ownership to prioritise citizens, particularly where supply is limited. If housing supply is less constrained, migrants could receive equal rights to purchase a primary residence or multiple properties. Ownership may support social stability and long-term fiscal contribution by anchoring people to the local economy.



## **Welfare**

States may restrict migrants’ access to welfare. Social housing or income support can create concerns about free-riding where recipients receive more than they contribute. One proposed reciprocal arrangement would allow claims after sufficient tax contributions, with support limited according to historical contribution. This is intended to prevent non-reciprocal use. Other countries may provide emergency welfare protections to reduce hardship and exploitation.



## **Non-essential public services**

A state does not need to extend every service to all migrants. Services such as relocation assistance, emigration support or international rescue may be reserved for citizens.



## **Non-essential infrastructure**

Some facilities are tied to citizen-specific political rights, nationality processes or security. Examples include:

* Voting stations.
* Election offices.
* Political facilities.
* Nationality documentation offices.
* Military areas and buildings.
* Certain ceremonial, cultural or religious venues.

Some infrastructure is privately owned and may not be required to provide universal access. A state may choose to extend access beyond what migrants need for ordinary life, but this is a separate policy decision.



## **A practical migrant-rights framework**

A workable framework should distinguish:

1. Basic human protections.
2. Work-related rights.
3. Contribution-based benefits.
4. Status-based entitlements.
5. Citizen-only political rights.

Migrants should be protected from exploitation and able to live lawfully and productively. Capacity-constrained reciprocal equal treatment is proposed here as a basis for access to essential services and infrastructure. Fair opportunities in non-security-sensitive employment provide another important foundation.

Beyond these rights, the population and government can decide which additional services, subsidies or accommodations to provide. The appropriate arrangement depends on capacity, cost, contribution and public support.
