# Descent-based membership: boundaries and contested cases

Ancestral membership boundaries determine who belongs to the nation and who can receive citizenship, vote or become a national representative. The boundary is therefore central to ancestral nationalism. It must define the historical population, decide how descent is recognised and address mixed ancestry, incomplete evidence and existing citizenship.



## **Design principles**

The proposed boundary should be pragmatic, administratively workable and not excessively strict. If too rigid, it may create statelessness and unnecessary exclusion. If too loose, it may fail to protect the continuity and self-determination of the historical population. The following principles are proposed:

* **Avoid statelessness** - No one should be left without a country of legal belonging. During a transition, people should retain legal status until another citizenship or secure status is available.
* **Avoid unrealistic purity standards** - Rules should account for mixed ancestry, uncertain records and historical population mixture rather than demand total purity.
* **Protect ancestral continuity** - The boundary should preserve the identity and continuity of the long-established ancestral group.
* **Protect self-determination** - Distinct ancestral groups should have meaningful opportunities to govern themselves where clear collective support exists.
* **Distinguish citizenship from political membership** - Citizenship, voting and representation may use different thresholds. Legal citizenship can be broader to reduce statelessness, while political membership can be stricter.
* **Use proportionate restrictions** - Restrictions should be no stricter than necessary for the objective. Different requirements may apply to citizenship, voting, public office or migration decisions.
* **Balance national and local authority** - Decisions better handled locally should not be controlled unnecessarily by national government. Local autonomy becomes especially important where some citizens have limited national influence.
* **Establish clear rules** - The system should define ancestral ties, thresholds, evidence standards, mixed-ancestry cases and dispute procedures.
* **Recognise evidence limitations** - Records and genetic tests should be treated as evidence with limitations rather than perfect proof. Decisions should be reviewable and open to challenge.
* **Provide appeals** - People should be able to submit additional evidence, request retesting and receive independent review.
* **Protect privacy** - Where genetic data are used, clear rules should govern collection, storage, access, retention, deletion and secondary use.
* **Maintain intergenerational stability** - Rules should not change constantly. People need to understand and rely on their status.
* **Handle transitions cautiously** - Existing citizens and long-term residents should not abruptly lose status. Transitions require clear timelines, appeals and safeguards against statelessness.



## **Future civic societies and stricter boundaries**

New societies may eventually be created on explicitly civic or cultural foundations. They may accept fluid identities, mixed ancestry and voluntary membership from the beginning. If widely available, they could provide alternative places of national belonging for people excluded by ancestral systems.

In that context, stricter ancestral requirements in older states might eventually become more workable. In the short term, however, ancestral systems would need greater pragmatism and inclusiveness. Without accessible alternatives, strict boundaries could conflict with avoiding statelessness, legal stability and fair transition.



## **Problems with ancestral purity**

Ancestral purity requires complete or near-complete descent from the historical population. At national scale, this is generally not pragmatic. Historical populations commonly include mixture caused by migration, intermarriage, border changes, adoption, disputed parentage and incomplete records.

Even someone with strong historical ties could fail because of one ancestor from outside the group. For example, a person with seven great-grandparents from the national population and one from elsewhere may have approximately 87.5% ancestry from that population. A total-purity requirement would exclude them despite their substantial connection.

Their descendants could never return to complete purity because the external ancestor remains part of the lineage. This can:

* Exclude people with strong ties.
* Create statelessness.
* Make citizenship depend on small differences.
* Produce arbitrary or punitive outcomes.

Rejecting total purity at the national level does not require an extremely loose boundary. It supports thresholds recognising sufficient connection without demanding complete ancestry from one group. Smaller voluntary communities may choose stricter standards, but national rules need greater pragmatism.



## **Problems with very low thresholds**

A very low threshold creates the opposite problem. It may include people whose connection to the historical population is minimal. If that connection is too weak, the rule may no longer serve the purpose of protecting ancestral continuity. This is particularly important for political membership.

Citizenship provides legal belonging and a national home. Voting and representation determine control over:

* Migration.
* Citizenship law.
* Education.
* Constitutional identity.
* Public resources.
* Long-term demographic policy.

A very low threshold may weaken the connection between ancestral membership and group self-government. It may allow people with minimal ties to influence the future of the historical population. This does not mean people with mixed or weaker ancestry are necessarily disloyal.

Individuals may have strong cultural, family or personal commitments regardless of their ancestry percentage. The issue is the proposed institution’s ability to preserve collective self-determination, not a judgement about every individual’s character.



## **Different thresholds for different purposes**

One proposed response is to use:

* A broader threshold for citizenship.
* A stricter threshold for national voting or representation.
* More local and regional autonomy.

Another is to reserve stricter requirements for particular decisions, such as naturalisation or migration policy, rather than all political participation. The central design problem is balancing excessive exclusion against a boundary too weak to protect the intended group.



## **Absolute ancestral thresholds**

An absolute threshold requires a fixed percentage of ancestry from the national group. A 50% requirement, for example, means at least half the person’s ancestry must be connected to the historical population. This is comparatively straightforward because it asks whether the person meets one defined national standard.

It can rely primarily on the country’s own records, reference panels and verification systems. Its weakness is that it may exclude mixed-ancestry people whose strongest connection is nevertheless to that nation. Someone with 40% ancestry from one national group and smaller shares elsewhere could fail a 50% threshold despite having no stronger connection to another group. If no other country recognises them, statelessness may result.



## **Proportional ancestral thresholds**

A proportional threshold asks whether the national group is the person’s largest single ancestral connection. Someone with 40% ancestry from Group A, 30% from Group B and 30% from Group C would be most strongly connected to Group A under this approach. This is more flexible for mixed ancestry and may help identify a national home.

However, it requires reliable evidence about many populations, not just the state’s own historical group. Accurate comparison may depend on records and reference panels from across the world. It also permits someone to qualify even when the majority of their combined ancestry comes from other groups.

For these reasons, an absolute approach is proposed as the more pragmatic starting point. It is simpler, more transparent and less dependent on external evidence, provided safeguards prevent statelessness.



## **Citizenship and political membership as separate categories**

Citizenship provides legal membership, protection, residence and a recognised national home. Voting and representation provide authority over the state’s future.

The proposed distinction allows a person to have sufficient ancestry for legal belonging without necessarily meeting the threshold for full national political participation. This could make citizenship broad enough to avoid statelessness while providing stronger protection for ancestral self-determination.

However, it creates serious design challenges. Citizens with limited national influence remain governed by national institutions. If political participation is restricted, national authority should not extend unnecessarily into every part of their lives. Local autonomy, legal protection, due process and appeals become more important.



## **Possible arrangements**

Three approaches are proposed:

1. A single boundary granting citizenship and full political rights together.
2. A broad citizenship boundary with a stricter national voting and representation boundary.
3. Broad ordinary voting rights with stricter ancestry requirements for particular decisions affecting national membership or continuity.

The first is simpler and more equal between citizens. The others are more targeted but create greater complexity and require stronger safeguards.



## **Suggested citizenship boundary**

The simplest proposed citizenship rule would recognise anyone with a verified ancestral tie above zero. This is particularly relevant during a transition from civic to ancestral membership. The principal advantage is reducing statelessness.

If ancestral systems were adopted widely, every person would need at least one recognised national home. Broad citizenship eligibility reduces the risk that mixed-ancestry people are rejected everywhere.

It also avoids abruptly removing status from people with genuine, though limited, connections. A broad legal boundary does not require equally broad political membership. Voting and representation can remain separate if stronger protection for ancestral self-determination is desired.



## **Parental citizenship and historical rootedness**

A secondary requirement could involve a citizen parent or sufficient historical rootedness. This could prevent very distant or weak ancestry from automatically producing citizenship claims, particularly where similar ancestral populations live in several states. For example, someone with British ancestry whose family has lived in Australia for generations raises questions about which country should count as their principal ancestral home. Clear rules would be needed for overlapping claims.



## **Mixed ancestry and stronger connections elsewhere**

Someone may have weak ancestry in their current country but stronger connections to another. The proposed approach would allow them to retain citizenship unless another state clearly recognises the stronger claim. This protects against exclusion from every national home while allowing citizenship rules to become more precise.



## **Missing or uncertain evidence**

Some existing citizens may lack provable ties to the historical population and also lack clear evidence connecting them elsewhere. Possible reasons include:

* Missing records.
* Incomplete reference panels.
* Adoption.
* Historical displacement.
* Insufficient data.

Citizenship should not be revoked unless an alternative citizenship or secure status is available. A temporary or transitional citizenship category could be used during review, but the person should not become stateless.



## **Serious crime and weak ancestral ties**

Some may argue that serious crime justifies denaturalisation where a person has only weak ancestral ties. Under the proposed safeguards, this should not be arbitrary and should require due process. It should be considered only where another country clearly recognises the person as a citizen or rightful national member or when another country is willing to accept them as a citizen. Otherwise, it would conflict with the principle against statelessness.



## **Suggested political-membership boundary**

Political membership concerns voting, office-holding and decisions shaping national government. One option is to give every citizen full political rights. This avoids classes of citizenship, is easier to administer and reduces disputes about exclusion.

However, if citizenship is available through minimal ancestry, it may offer weaker protection for the historical population. A stricter option is to require at least 50% shared ancestry with the national group. The reasoning is that someone at least half connected to the population has a substantial ancestral relationship and cannot simultaneously be majority-connected to another single group.

A threshold that is above 50% would exclude people with exactly half their ancestry in the nation, despite their substantial connection. Therefore, a proposed 50% threshold is presented as a more pragmatic strict requirement.



## **The argument for a stricter political boundary**

Political power affects:

* Migration.
* Naturalisation.
* Citizenship.
* Constitutional identity.
* Education.
* Resource distribution.
* Foreign policy.

If the purpose of the system is ancestral continuity and self-determination, the boundary for these powers may need to be stronger than the boundary for legal belonging.



## **The disadvantages of unequal political status**

A stricter political threshold means some citizens have fewer national political rights. This can create resentment, instability and legitimacy problems. The stricter the boundary, the more important it becomes to limit national power and provide local or regional influence. People without full national participation could still influence:

* Local services.
* Community institutions.
* Public infrastructure.
* Local rules.
* Other decisions affecting daily life.

Another option is to apply the stricter boundary only to migration, naturalisation or constitutional questions rather than ordinary elections.



## **Consent to political-membership rules**

A referendum among the historical ancestral population could establish whether it supports stricter membership rules. However, deciding who may vote in that referendum is itself part of the boundary problem. Any such process would need clear eligibility, evidence standards, appeals and protection against arbitrary exclusion. The system would also need to define whether the threshold applies to:

* All national elections.
* Particular policy areas.
* Eligibility for office.
* Some combination of these.



## **Updating ancestral boundaries**

Boundaries may come under pressure to change as populations intermarry, migrate, assimilate or become more deeply rooted. New evidence may also show that an earlier definition was incomplete. Including additional groups can change what the nation is understood to be.

The state would then protect a broader population with potentially different identities and preferences. This could weaken the original population’s self-determination and create internal disagreement between those favouring inclusion and those who regard expansion as abandoning the system’s purpose.



## **Administrative correction and political expansion**

Correcting inaccurate evidence is different from expanding membership. An administrative correction applies the existing boundary more accurately. Political expansion changes the boundary itself. This distinction matters because once a group receives recognition and political rights, withdrawing them later may generate serious conflict. Expansion can therefore create a ratchet effect: membership broadens but becomes difficult to narrow.



## **Alternatives to repeated boundary expansion**

Instead of repeatedly redefining the national group, demographic and social change could be addressed through:

* Local and regional autonomy.
* Protected communities.
* Federal arrangements.
* Differentiated residence statuses.
* New autonomous regions.
* In larger cases, new states.

These could allow communities to follow their preferences without continually revising the ancestral boundary. The proposed principle is that changes affecting identity, self-determination and political power should require strong justification and broad consent.



## **Relationship to verification**

Boundary rules determine what the system is trying to establish. Verification procedures determine how those rules are applied to individuals.
