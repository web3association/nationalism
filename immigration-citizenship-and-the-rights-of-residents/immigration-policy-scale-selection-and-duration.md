# Immigration policy: scale, selection and duration

Immigration policy determines who may enter a country, for what purpose, in what numbers and for how long. These decisions are related to national identity and citizenship, but they are not identical.

A country can admit few immigrants while offering citizenship to those it accepts. Another can depend heavily on temporary migrant workers while rarely granting citizenship. A third can combine substantial permanent immigration with demanding naturalisation requirements. Immigration levels, duration of residence, citizenship eligibility and integration expectations should therefore be considered separately.



## **Purposes of immigration**

Countries may accept migrants for several reasons:

* Employment and labour shortages.
* Specialist skills.
* Education.
* Family reunion.
* Humanitarian protection.
* Historical relationships.
* A collective preference to admit people from particular groups.

Some migration is temporary, such as a period of employment or study. Other arrangements allow permanent settlement. The common legal pathways include residence permits, work visas or permits, and freedom-of-movement arrangements between states.



## **No or near-zero immigration**

A literal zero-immigration policy would prevent foreign nationals from moving into the country for temporary or permanent residence. In practice, this is rare because governments may still admit diplomats, refugees, close family members or essential specialists.

Tourism, short business visits and the return of citizens living abroad would not ordinarily count as immigration. A country may nevertheless maintain near-zero immigration to:

* Minimise demographic change.
* Preserve cultural continuity.
* Avoid dependence on migrant labour.
* Retain close control over population growth.

This can reduce the pace of change and pressures associated with new arrivals. However, labour shortages, skills needs and population ageing would then need to be addressed through domestic education, higher workforce participation, automation or economic reform.



## **Low immigration**

Low immigration permits a limited number of people to enter, generally through selected routes such as:

* Family reunion.
* Skilled employment.
* Humanitarian protection.
* Exceptional need.

It allows a country to acquire particular skills and meet family or international obligations while keeping population change gradual. Lower numbers may make housing provision and integration easier to manage. They can also restrict economic growth, reduce the available workforce and leave some labour shortages unfilled.



## **Moderate immigration**

Moderate immigration allows continuing movement through a wider range of employment, family, education and humanitarian routes. It is often intended to balance migration’s economic and social benefits against the capacity to provide housing, infrastructure and public services. It can:

* Support gradual population growth.
* Expand the workforce.
* Provide cultural connections.
* Strengthen commercial links with other countries.

Its effects depend on migrant selection, settlement patterns and whether government planning keeps pace with population change.



## **High immigration**

High or large-scale immigration, sometimes described as mass migration, involves admitting substantial numbers relative to the existing population. A country may adopt it to address:

* Extensive labour shortages.
* Population ageing.
* Regional decline.
* Economic expansion.
* Education and international exchange.

It can increase the supply of workers and skills, create businesses and strengthen international connections. However, rapid immigration can also increase demand for housing, transport, schools and healthcare. It may place pressure on labour markets and social cohesion, and produce cultural or demographic change faster than some communities prefer.



## **Temporary migration and permanent settlement**

Temporary migration allows people to enter for a defined purpose and period without necessarily becoming permanent members of the national community. Permanent settlement creates a different relationship. Residents may establish families, develop lasting social ties and seek citizenship.

A country may accept substantial temporary migration while keeping permanent settlement or naturalisation restrictive. Another may admit fewer people but provide those admitted with a clearer route to permanent residence and citizenship. The distinction matters because immigration numbers alone do not explain the long-term relationship between migrants and the state.



## **Different nationalist approaches**

**Civic nationalists** define membership primarily through citizenship, law and institutions. They may accept people from many backgrounds if they follow the civic order and become loyal citizens. Civic nationalism can still support strict borders, controlled immigration and demanding naturalisation requirements.

**Cultural nationalists** focus on whether newcomers can assimilate into the national language, culture, norms and way of life. They may prefer immigration from culturally similar countries or require stronger cultural and linguistic adaptation before citizenship.

**Ancestral nationalists** are concerned that migration and naturalisation could change the ancestral composition of the citizenry and electorate. They may oppose granting citizenship and voting power to non-ancestral communities where this is considered a threat to historical self-determination.



## **Combining policy choices**

Countries can combine identity, citizenship and immigration policies in different ways. One may define itself as an ancestral and cultural community, grant citizenship mainly by descent and admit little discretionary migration. Another may define membership civically, offer accessible naturalisation and accept moderate or high immigration.

Other combinations include low immigration with equal civic membership for those admitted, or substantial temporary migration with restrictive naturalisation. There is no single immigration level or national-identity model that every country is morally required to adopt.

A population may value ancestral, cultural and demographic continuity and prefer low immigration or demanding naturalisation. Another may value openness, pluralism and equal membership across different backgrounds, leading it to favour accessible citizenship and larger inflows. Relatively homogeneous and highly diverse societies can both be peaceful, democratic and cohesive.

An ancestral conception of the nation is not necessarily hostile to outsiders, and a civic conception does not require unrestricted immigration. Low, moderate or high immigration can be a legitimate collective choice when adopted through lawful and democratic institutions. Major demographic policies should consider democratic consent, public capacity and their effects on established populations and newcomers.



## **Left and right approaches**

The political left generally emphasises equality, social welfare, labour rights and reducing hierarchy. The political right generally emphasises order, continuity, private property, sovereignty and established institutions. Both traditions contain moderate and democratic positions.

Far-left and far-right movements are not uniform. Some far-left traditions are internationalist; others have supported immigration controls to protect workers or centralised national projects.

Some far-right movements emphasise cultural assimilation, while ancestral variants place greater weight on descent and historical population continuity.

Immigration restriction is not necessarily far-right, and support for immigration is not necessarily far-left. The following comparison describes common tendencies rather than positions held by every movement.

| **Factor**                      | **Far-left tendencies**                                                                                                          | **Far-right tendencies**                                                                                       |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Basis of membership**         | Residence, participation, class solidarity or universal equality.                                                                | Ancestry, inherited culture, religion or demanding assimilation.                                               |
| **Understanding of the nation** | Borders and nation-states may be secondary to international solidarity.                                                          | The nation is a historical community whose identity, sovereignty and continuity should be protected.           |
| **Demographic diversity**       | Generally accepts or values diversity and opposes preservation of racial homogeneity.                                            | Often seeks cultural continuity; ancestral variants may also seek demographic continuity.                      |
| **Scale of immigration**        | Often more permissive, although labour-oriented movements may favour controls protecting wages and conditions.                   | Usually substantially lower or more selective immigration.                                                     |
| **Selection**                   | Humanitarian need, family unity and equal treatment rather than wealth or skills alone.                                          | Skills, economic benefit, security, cultural compatibility or connections to the national population.          |
| **Temporary labour**            | May oppose insecure status because it enables exploitation; often supports equal labour rights and permanent residence pathways. | May accept temporary workers while restricting permanent membership.                                           |
| **Asylum and refugees**         | Universal obligations, humanitarian protection and displaced people’s rights.                                                    | National discretion, border control, security, capacity and deterrence of unfounded claims.                    |
| **Naturalisation**              | Accessible citizenship for long-term residents.                                                                                  | Longer residence and stronger language, loyalty, character or assimilation requirements.                       |
| **Birthright citizenship**      | Broad eligibility to prevent inherited exclusion.                                                                                | Conditional eligibility based on parental citizenship or status.                                               |
| **Dual citizenship**            | Generally accepts overlapping citizenships and identities.                                                                       | More likely to prefer exclusive citizenship or renunciation of previous nationality.                           |
| **Long-term residents**         | Reduces distinctions between citizens and established residents.                                                                 | Reserves political and some social rights for citizens.                                                        |
| **Integration**                 | Multiculturalism and accommodation of different identities.                                                                      | Assimilation into a common language, culture and traditions.                                                   |
| **Unauthorised migration**      | Regularisation, due process, labour protection and preventing exploitation.                                                      | Detention, removal, enforcement and deterrence.                                                                |
| **Border enforcement**          | Less restrictive borders and stronger migrant safeguards.                                                                        | Stronger physical and legal border controls.                                                                   |
| **Central concern**             | Exclusion, exploitation and unequal treatment.                                                                                   | Sovereignty, security, cohesion and national continuity.                                                       |
| **Risk at the extreme**         | Understates housing, service, labour-market, integration or consent constraints.                                                 | Converts concerns about continuity or borders into collective blame or unequal treatment of minority citizens. |

A country can combine controlled borders with refugee protection, a common language with minority cultural protections, or limited immigration with an achievable citizenship pathway. The main questions are therefore who may enter, whether residence is temporary or permanent, what integration is expected and when national membership becomes available.
