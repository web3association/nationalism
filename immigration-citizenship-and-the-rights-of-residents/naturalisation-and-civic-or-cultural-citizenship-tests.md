# Naturalisation and civic or cultural citizenship tests

Civic and cultural nationalism can both provide routes through which people from outside the historical population become citizens. These routes may require several years of residence followed by tests or assessments. The principal difference is whether the assessment concerns civic knowledge and commitment alone or also requires cultural assimilation.



## **Civic naturalisation**

A civic citizenship process may assess whether an applicant understands the country’s institutions and how to live within its legal and political order. Common areas include:

* Language proficiency.
* Civic knowledge.
* National history.
* Constitutional principles.
* Residence and social participation.



## **Language proficiency**

Applicants may be tested on speaking, reading or writing the national language. Language assessment is one of the more effective parts of a citizenship test because it establishes whether someone can communicate. However, this is a low threshold for determining whether a person is integrated into national life.



## **Civic knowledge**

Tests may cover:

* Institutions.
* Laws.
* National symbols.
* Workplace expectations.
* Schools.
* Healthcare.
* Political systems.

They can demonstrate knowledge but do not reliably establish that the applicant agrees with or is committed to the civic way of life.



## **National history**

Applicants may be asked about the national flag, the national anthem, major public holidays, important historical events or why particular events are commemorated. Knowledge of these subjects provides evidence of familiarity with the country, not necessarily loyalty to its inherited institutions or culture.



## **Constitutional commitment**

Applicants may be required to accept the constitution, democracy, rule of law and other political principles. A central difficulty is determining whether an oath reflects genuine agreement or merely the applicant’s willingness to complete the citizenship process.



## **Residence and participation**

Evidence may include employment, schooling and participation in local life. These can demonstrate that someone is present and involved, but reliable assessment beyond one-off evidence is difficult.



## **The limits of civic testing**

Civic tests can establish basic knowledge, language ability and some participation. They cannot reliably show that someone will remain aligned with the country’s institutions, laws and public life. Nor do they guarantee that the applicant will not later seek changes reflecting different preferences or the interests of their own group.



## **Civic institutions and population differences**

A civic nationalist system assumes that individuals from different backgrounds can help preserve its institutions and laws. However, the same institutions and legal practices do not exist everywhere, and populations have not all created or sustained them to the same standard.

These differences may arise from intellectual differences between groups or persistent cultural differences. As a civic society’s demographics change, it is difficult to guarantee that its institutions and laws will remain the same. Genetic differences affecting intelligence, behaviours or cultural differences retained after migration, could influence future outcomes.

A citizenship oath does not prove that someone fully agrees with the existing civic order or will refrain from changing it. Civic testing therefore does not reliably preserve institutions over the long term, nor does it preserve the historical culture or ancestral population.



## **Cultural naturalisation**

Cultural citizenship generally extends civic requirements. Applicants may need civic knowledge, language competence and an understanding of the society, together with sufficient knowledge of or participation in its culture. However, culture is broad, constantly evolving and difficult to define exhaustively. The distinction between knowledge of a culture and assimilation into it is therefore important.



## **Testing cultural knowledge**

Applicants could be questioned about cultural practices and expectations. Practical questions would need to concern widely adopted practices because not every citizen knows or participates in every aspect of national culture. Knowing a cultural fact does not demonstrate that the person has assimilated. They may simply have memorised the information required to pass.



## **Testing cultural participation**

An assessment could consider whether someone participates in festivals, traditions, community events, recreation or sports. However, verifying participation across a range of events is difficult. Attendance undertaken to pass a test does not demonstrate a lasting desire to participate or genuine assimilation.



## **Testing cultural contribution**

Applicants could be assessed on contributions through art, music, volunteering, film, literature and cooking. Verifying ongoing contribution is difficult without directly monitoring the person. Doing something required by a citizenship process does not show that it is a continuing personal practice.



## **Knowledge, participation and genuine assimilation**

None of these approaches reliably establishes that a person has adopted the culture in everyday life. Participation and contribution assessments would be difficult to verify without substantial monitoring. Knowledge tests are easier to administer but demonstrate memory rather than cultural alignment. Where citizenship provides a strong incentive to appear assimilated, some applicants may respond to that incentive without genuinely adopting the expected culture.



## **Cultural change and testing standards**

Culture can change through technology, economic conditions and social preferences even within a homogeneous population. This makes it difficult to determine which version of the culture applicants should be expected to adopt. The relationship between culture, ancestry and genetic influences is discussed in **Cultural continuity, transmission and change**.



## **Prohibited cultural practices**

A nation-state may collectively decide to prohibit practices considered incompatible with its culture. Examples in the proposed approach include:

* Other languages.
* Religious practices.
* Particular forms of marriage.
* Cultural events originating elsewhere.

The intended benefit is to make it harder for migrants to continue practices that conflict with the national culture. However, preventing public expression does not mean those affected agree with the restriction. If they later gain sufficient voting power, they may seek to reverse it.



## **Limits on cultural prohibition**

Some cultural practices are publicly visible, but many are personal and difficult to prohibit. These include:

* Values.
* Social norms.
* Communication styles.
* Etiquette.
* Family structures.
* Dating norms.
* Cuisine preferences.

Trying to prevent every such difference would require excessive surveillance of everyday life. Prohibitions may therefore slow some visible forms of cultural change without preventing all differences from becoming more prominent. They may also be removed if demographic or cultural preferences change.



## **Deciding which practices are prohibited**

A prohibited-practices list could be created through representatives or a democratic vote of the long-established ancestral population. It could develop gradually as particular differences are identified as problematic. Opinion polling could indicate whether a formal vote is needed to remove a prohibition when preferences change. Alternatively, a representative could be responsible for gathering public sentiment and updating the list.



## **The practical limits of cultural citizenship tests**

It is impossible to test every aspect of culture reliably. Language and basic historical knowledge can be assessed relatively easily. Social norms, attitudes, preferences and daily behaviour are much harder to verify.

Applicants can state that they share particular values without demonstrating that those values guide their lives. Determining this would require intrusive monitoring. Once applicants become citizens, they also gain influence over the tests and cultural rules themselves. They may campaign to remove prohibitions or reshape the culture through their behaviour and political preferences. No cultural citizenship test can therefore reliably guarantee meaningful assimilation across the full range of national culture.



## **Pragmatic cultural tests**

The most workable criteria are those that are straightforward to verify. A practical assessment might include:

* **Basic cultural knowledge** - Examples include knowledge of greetings, gift-giving norms, visiting etiquette, table manners, formal dress expectations, wedding and funeral etiquette and appropriate ways to address older people or professionals.
* **Knowledge of prohibited practices** - Applicants could be tested on practices prohibited in the country. For example, religions outside the country’s historical traditions may have certain practices restricted or may be prohibited from using public infrastructure or holding public events. Passing such a test demonstrates that an applicant remembers the rules. It does not prove agreement with them or a desire to adopt the national culture fully.



## **Other approaches to cultural preservation**

One alternative is to reduce immigration substantially. With fewer arrivals, newcomers are more likely to be surrounded by the host culture and have time to understand and adopt it. Higher immigration makes it easier to maintain separate cultural networks and influence the host culture instead of assimilating.

Another proposed approach is to stop granting citizenship to migrants. This would slow demographic change in the political community and make it easier to reverse settlement arrangements or remove groups exhibiting cultural differences regarded as undesirable.

These approaches concern migration and membership policy rather than the reliability of citizenship tests themselves.
