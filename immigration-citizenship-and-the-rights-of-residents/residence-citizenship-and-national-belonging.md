# Residence, citizenship and national belonging

Residence, citizenship and national belonging describe related but different relationships with a country. A person may live and work in a state without being a citizen. They may possess citizenship without being widely accepted as part of the historical nation. They may also participate extensively in national life while remaining a long-term non-citizen resident. These distinctions matter when deciding what rights and expectations should accompany different forms of membership.



## **Citizenship**

Citizenship is the legal status of belonging to a country. It defines a person’s formal relationship with the state. It commonly includes:

* **Rights** - Residence, work, voting and legal protection.
* **Responsibilities** - Obeying laws, paying taxes and sometimes military or jury service.
* **Identity and belonging** - Official recognition as a national member, commonly accompanied by a passport.
* **Access to services** - Healthcare, education, transport or welfare, although these are not necessarily exclusive to citizens.



## **Ancestral membership**

Under an ancestral model, the nation is a historical community associated with ancestry, language, religion, culture or inherited traditions. Membership is closely connected to descent from an established national population.

This can promote historical continuity and shared identity, but can make it difficult or impossible for immigrants and minority citizens to be accepted as full members. Citizenship is primarily acquired through descent from a citizen parent or recognised ancestry. Some ancestral models allow naturalisation while distinguishing legal citizenship from membership of the historical nation. Other models make ancestry the sole basis of citizenship.



## **Cultural membership**

A cultural model allows people from different backgrounds to become members if they adopt the national language, customs, traditions, institutions and central values. Ancestry is not necessarily decisive, but adaptation is expected.

Requirements can range from language competence and respect for constitutional principles to substantial abandonment of a previous cultural identity. Citizenship may be restrictive or relatively accessible, but commonly requires evidence of assimilation.



## **Civic membership**

A civic model defines the nation through citizenship, laws, institutions and participation. People can belong regardless of ancestry, race or religion if they accept the constitutional order and fulfil civic responsibilities.

In practice, civic identity is often accompanied by expectations about language, history and public culture. Once citizenship is acquired, people normally receive equal status regardless of origin.



## **Residence-based membership**

Residence-based arrangements allow migrants to live and work in the country without necessarily becoming citizens. Long-term residents may receive social, economic or political rights because they participate in the country and are affected by its laws. These can include:

* Employment protection.
* Public services.
* Local or regional voting.
* Participation through unions and civil society.
* Consultation through local institutions.

National voting, some public offices and security-sensitive employment may remain reserved for citizens. The longer someone resides and contributes, the stronger the argument may become for permanent residence.



## **Potential problems with granting citizenship**



**Permanence and reversibility**

Citizenship has historically been understood as something with a lasting status. However this can be problematic if group differences become more disputed over time and the historical population wants to reconsider its earlier membership decisions. Although citizenships can be revoked, it is not something that has been historically treated as easily reversible.



**Costs and capacity**

Public services cost money, and infrastructure supports a limited population. Growth in the citizen population can increase service costs and require additional infrastructure.



**Free-riding**

Citizens can be a net cost to the state if they receive more in welfare and services than they contribute in taxes. Migrants who become citizens may therefore contribute to an increasing fiscal burden.



**Ancestral replacement**

Citizenship permits permanent residence. Immigration may be easier to control than emigration, reproduction or death. If substantial migrant populations become citizens, their demographic growth can eventually contribute to replacing the historical population as the dominant group. The underlying demographic processes are discussed in **Demographic change: migration, births, deaths and intermarriage**.



**Persistent group differences**

New citizens may come from backgrounds with differences in culture, preferences or behaviour. As their demographic share grows, those differences could become increasingly prominent or dominant. The historical population would need to accept that the incoming groups’ way of life could eventually become the predominant national pattern.



## **Is citizenship necessary for a fulfilling life?**

The position presented here is that migrants do not require citizenship to live productive and fulfilling lives in another country. Equal treatment when using essential services and infrastructure, together with opportunities in non-security-sensitive employment, can often be sufficient. A state can provide many practical rights without granting permanent political membership.



## **Long-term and perpetual visas**

One alternative is to offer long-term visas, such as visas lasting ten years. Another is a perpetual visa allowing migrants to remain for as long as they wish, subject to the conditions of that arrangement. Either could allow people to live and work throughout the country and access essential services. Access to publicly funded services does not need to depend solely on citizenship. For example, migrants who have made sufficient tax contributions could receive free healthcare or education. Eligibility in one year could depend on contributions made during the previous year.



## **Why a visa-based approach may be preferred**

A perpetual or long-term visa retains a distinction between residence and citizenship. If group differences become problematic, or the ancestral population no longer wants particular migrants to remain, their visas could be revoked. If service costs are excessive or free-riding emerges, the conditions governing access could be changed. If demographic change becomes a concern, the state could stop issuing visas and revoke some existing ones. This is presented as a way to address the problems associated with permanent citizenship while retaining opportunities for migrants to live and work in the country.



## **Stability for migrants and their families**

Some migrants will be reluctant to settle where they can be required to leave. This is especially important where they have children and departure would disrupt schooling, family life or employment. The proposed response is to provide a sufficient grace period following revocation. That could be a fixed number of years or a variable period reflecting historical contributions. The wider questions concerning changes to legal status and departure arrangements are considered in **Existing citizenship, denaturalisation and security of residence**.



## **Membership and informed policy choices**

A country needs to determine:

* How long migrants may remain.
* Whether their residence is renewable or permanent.
* Whether citizenship is available.
* What services and protections accompany each status.
* Whether political participation is attached to residence or citizenship.
* How much security each arrangement provides.

These decisions should not be treated as automatic consequences of admitting someone for work, study or another temporary purpose.
