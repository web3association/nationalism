# Voting, representation and eligibility for public office

Political participation determines who can influence national leadership, laws and long-term priorities. Three related questions need to be considered:

* Who can vote?
* How are people’s interests represented?
* Who can hold public office?

These questions are connected to citizenship but can also be considered separately.



## **Voting rights**

Voting rights allow people to participate in elections or decide public questions. They provide a means of choosing leaders and influencing laws and policies. Granting migrants voting rights changes the composition of the electorate and the distribution of political influence.



## **Potential problems with extending national voting rights**



**Different political preferences**

Migrants may have different preferences concerning political ideology, public policy or the role of government. Groups may also differ in work ethic or general intelligence. These differences can influence what they vote for and how much they rely on the state. They may produce disputes with the historical population that granted political membership.



**Free-riding**

New voters may support parties offering greater welfare or public services at the expense of other taxpayers. This can include costs borne by the long-established ancestral population.



**External influence**

Migrants may retain connections to their home countries or face external pressure intended to influence politics. For example, a foreign country or community may encourage support for admitting more migrants from that country.



**Loss of self-determination**

As the electorate changes, the historical population’s relative influence may decline. If migrant-origin populations become a voting majority, it can become harder for the historical population to reverse demographic change or protect its preferred way of life. The underlying population mechanisms are discussed in **Demographic change: migration, births, deaths and intermarriage**.



## **Are national voting rights necessary?**

The position presented here is that migrants do not require national voting rights to live fulfilling lives. They need access to essential services, infrastructure and employment opportunities.

A state seeking migrants can offer equal treatment and an attractive lifestyle without giving them authority to select national representatives. If someone moves because they value the country’s opportunities and existing way of life, voting rights are not necessary to enjoy those benefits.

If they dislike that way of life, voting power could enable them to reshape it in ways the historical population rejects. If the country later changes in a way the migrant dislikes, they can return to their country of origin or move elsewhere.



## **Local participation**

Restricting national voting does not require excluding migrants from every collective decision. They could participate in decisions involving:

* Local infrastructure.
* Land use.
* Extracurricular school activities.
* Hospital priorities.
* Transport changes.
* The operation of essential services.

They could also provide feedback directly influencing service design. This would give them a voice over daily life without granting the same influence over national sovereignty or membership policy. The distinction between national, local, technical and identity-forming decisions is developed further in **Governance, cohesion and national policy**.



## **Representation**

Representation concerns whether people’s views, needs and interests are considered in government. Electing representatives is one route. Another question is whether migrants themselves should be eligible to become those representatives.



## **Potential problems with migrant office-holding**



**Favouritism**

An elected representative may favour their own community beyond what is considered fair. This could influence welfare, social housing, immigration or other resource-allocation decisions.



**External ties and control**

Even second, third or fourth generation immigrants can retain connections with external countries through family, friendships, preferences or sympathies. These ties may affect decisions about diplomacy, foreign aid or military policy. The result may not align with the priorities of the historical population.



**Changes in political representation**

As demographics and voting power change, more representatives may be elected who reflect the interests of growing communities. The historical population may consequently have less influence over national government.



**Is direct office-holding necessary?**

The position presented here is that migrants do not need to become national representatives for their needs to be considered. A government seeking skilled workers has an incentive to create attractive conditions. Equal treatment, access to services and opportunities in non-security-sensitive employment can provide a sufficiently fulfilling environment. Where migrants receive treatment similar to citizens in ordinary life, the argument that they require their own national representatives becomes less compelling.



## **Alternative forms of representation**

Governments could establish:

* Feedback systems through which migrants suggest service improvements.
* Prioritisation systems identifying their most serious problems.
* Consultation on essential infrastructure.
* Opportunities for self-organisation.
* Community representatives dealing with local services.
* Representatives organising cultural or religious events.

These arrangements can communicate migrant interests without giving them the same authority over national political direction.



## **Security-sensitive office and employment**

Some public roles involve strategic decisions, sensitive information or national security. Citizenship requirements, security vetting and conflict-of-interest checks may be appropriate. These are different from ordinary employment opportunities, where equal treatment can help migrants participate productively.



## **Political participation under the three models**

**Civic nationalism** normally treats eligible citizens as political equals regardless of ancestry.

**Cultural nationalism** generally provides equal political rights once a person has satisfied the requirements for cultural membership and citizenship.

**Ancestral nationalism** may reserve national voting or office-holding for those meeting an ancestral boundary. It may also apply one boundary to citizenship and another to political participation.

Those proposed distinctions are examined in **Descent-based membership: boundaries and contested cases**.
