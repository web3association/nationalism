# Ancestry, genetics, heritage and cultural identity

Ancestry, genetics, heritage and culture can all be important parts of a group’s identity. Some nations may want to protect and preserve these differences. Nationalism can provide a political solution intended to achieve that outcome.



## **Ancestry**

Ancestry refers to someone’s family lineage and geographic origins. Shared ancestry and history as a distinct group can contribute to a sense of belonging within an ancestral community.

A nation might want to protect its ancestral group because of its long-established history together. People may find it easier to share commonalities with others who have a similar ancestry and background.

The long-established ancestral group is most closely tied to the historical culture and achievements of its nation-state. Protecting that group could be seen as one of the more reliable ways to preserve its heritage, culture and way of life.



## **Genetics**

Genetics refers to inherited biological variation encoded in DNA. It influences traits such as eye colour, blood type, disease risk and many other physical characteristics.

Differences exist within groups and across groups. Genetic differences can also mean differences in average group behaviours and general levels of intelligence.

A nation might want to protect its genetic differences because of the distinct physical traits it wants to preserve. It may want the distinct visual appearance of its group to continue as part of national identity.

Another reason a nation may want to protect genetic differences is if its group has higher average intelligence or more desirable behaviours on average than other groups.

Protecting this genetic pool could be an effective way to prevent dysgenic outcomes through intermixing with populations with lower average IQ scores or populations exhibiting less desirable group behaviours.



## **Heritage**

Heritage is the legacy passed down from previous generations. It includes family inheritance and shared historical traditions. Heritage can include language, collective memories, religion, customs and stories, food and art, historical infrastructure, artefacts and memorials.

Groups may have different heritages because of their distinct histories, institutions and traditions. A nation might want to protect its heritage because its population has close ties to what previous generations passed down. Individuals may feel pride in their nation’s history, infrastructure or achievements. They may also feel connected to the group’s past and traditions.



## **Culture**

Culture refers to a group’s shared practices, values, norms, symbols, language and ways of life. Culture also forms part of heritage. Generations pass traditions, rituals, festivals, performing arts, music, dance and other practices to their children and wider communities.

A nation might want to protect its culture because it affects everyday life. Some individuals may strongly prefer their own culture to others they have experienced. Culture is not fixed, however. It changes through invention, reform, contact with other societies, technological change and generational changes. A nation may preserve core cultural features while adapting in other areas.



## **Ethnicity**

Ethnicity commonly combines cultural and ancestral aspects of identity.

An ethnic group may be understood through shared culture, traditions, language, history, religion, geographic origin or ancestry. The breadth of the term means it does not always clearly distinguish inherited descent from cultural practices that someone could adopt.

For this analysis, ancestry and culture are treated separately when that distinction is important. The terminology behind this separation is discussed in **Related forms of nationalism and terminology**.



## **Group identity, dilution and replacement concerns**

Each group’s ancestry, genetics, culture and heritage are not the same. If migrants are invited into another group’s country and eventually receive citizenship and political membership, there is a risk that this could dilute or eventually replace certain group differences.

Even over a long period, it can be difficult to expect another group to assimilate perfectly into a country’s culture. Persistent differences may remain and change the host nation. Many people will not want to abandon the heritage and cultural identity most closely linked to their ancestry.

New and expanding cultures may also contain aspects that are not better than the existing culture. Some incoming practices may be demonstrably undesirable in numerous ways.

First-cousin marriage is one example. Inbreeding can increase the absolute risk of certain recessive disorders, which could include a reduction in general intelligence. This can become a burden for society where children become dependent on their parents and the state for care. The host population needs to be able to identify and express its preferences about which group differences it wants to protect and preserve.



## **How the three models approach group identity**

**Civic nationalism** defines membership primarily through citizenship, law, shared institutions and political values. It generally accepts people regardless of ancestry if they accept the civic order.

It may allow greater demographic and cultural change than descent-based nationalism, but it can still use strict borders and citizenship rules to help preserve a common language, public culture, national history and institutions.

**Cultural nationalism** defines the nation primarily through language, customs, values, historical memory and a way of life. People from different ancestral backgrounds may become full members if they assimilate.

Its openness depends on how narrowly culture is defined and whether cultural membership is treated as learnable or inherited. The ancestral group and its genetics could therefore be diluted or replaced over time through demographic change.

**Ancestral nationalism** seeks to protect the long-established ancestral population and can therefore also help protect its genetics.

Because that population predominantly defines the national culture, protecting it can often be the most reliable way to preserve its heritage and culture.

Ancestral nationalism should be the most reliable approach for preserving continuity of group identity, although even this model still does not prevent slow and gradual change through intermixing.
