# Assimilation, integration and multiculturalism

Countries take different approaches to how people from different backgrounds should live together. Some have been experimenting with becoming more multicultural. Others have preferred to remain more monocultural and homogeneous.

These approaches involve different expectations about whether newcomers should adopt the existing culture or retain a distinct cultural identity.



## **Monocultural and multicultural societies**

A monocultural society is one in which everyone adopts the same culture. A multicultural society contains multiple distinct cultural groups, each with its own culture.

There is nothing morally or ethically wrong with either approach. Both are viable ways to structure a society, and different groups may prefer one or the other.

People with different heritages and cultures may live together in peace and harmony. However, a monocultural society with a well-established dominant heritage and culture is also welcome to continue as it is. A monocultural nation-state does not need to invite anyone else into its society if it does not want to.



## **Assimilation**

Assimilation occurs when migrants adopt the dominant culture, language, norms, values and behaviours of the host society to such an extent that their original cultural distinctiveness largely disappears.

It is a one-way process: the immigrant group changes to fit the existing society. The responsibility to change lies almost entirely with the immigrant.

Full assimilation is possible only on a cultural basis. Ancestry and genetics are fixed differences between groups and cannot be changed.

Cultural differences can potentially be adopted by immigrant groups, so assimilation may resolve many group differences in certain cases.



## **The difficulty of full assimilation**

In practice, assimilation can be extremely difficult because of the number of differences migrants would need to abandon to adopt the host culture fully.

Expecting someone to give up their own identity entirely may be considered extreme and is unlikely to happen in many cases. It would be difficult to expect anyone to assimilate fully even after many years in another country.

People may abandon some practices without stripping themselves of their historical identity. For many, doing so would not be desirable.



## **Assimilation and immigration levels**

Assimilation becomes more difficult as immigration increases. When many migrants arrive during a similar period, they can more easily find others who share their background. This makes it easier to maintain their own culture and norms, or adopt those of other immigrant communities, without experiencing the host culture as extensively.

If assimilation is to occur without changing the host culture, it will likely require relatively small annual inflows. This allows newcomers time and exposure to adopt the existing culture.

Even then, intermixing could change the country’s genetic composition, including physical traits. The host population would need to accept that certain differences could become more dominant.

If it wants to remain homogeneous to protect its ancestral group’s way of life, it may prefer to prevent immigration from groups that do not share its ancestral background.



## **Assumptions about the ability to assimilate**

A society may want to preserve its traditions while inviting migrants to join it. One potential problem is assuming that all groups can assimilate into another group’s culture.

If a culture produces greater prosperity, there would be an argument for other nations to duplicate its most important features. Although this may be logical, it is not something regularly observed.

One potential explanation is intellectual capacity. Some groups are more intelligent than others, and genetics influences this.

Culture is downstream from the people who form the nation. In some instances, groups may be unable to replicate another group’s culture because of differences in general intelligence.

A second explanation is the practical reality of assimilation. Adopting another way of life can require abandoning practices and norms learned while growing up.

People may be willing or able to abandon some aspects without fully giving up their historical identity.



## **Integration**

Integration occurs when migrants participate fully in the host country’s social, economic and political life without being forced to abandon their cultural identity. The aim is mutual respect rather than complete cultural uniformity.

Integration is a two-way process of adaptation. The host society must be open to cultural differences, while immigrants must find ways to contribute and connect with the host population.

Integration is possible and much easier to achieve than assimilation. However, it also means the host culture is likely to change as it adapts to immigrants who are not expected to abandon their cultural identities. Understanding the differences between groups therefore becomes particularly important.



## **Uncertainty about integration**

It is difficult to understand the full range of differences between two groups before they live together in the same country.

Differences that were initially unproblematic may become increasingly disliked or problematic over time. Integration is therefore more risky and potentially problematic because it is difficult to predict how cultures will coexist or which differences will produce disputes.

Changing demographics may eventually make the incoming groups’ cultural differences dominant. Because demographics are difficult to influence and control, integration can introduce permanent cultural changes that later become the predominant way of life.

The host population needs to understand that possibility before inviting large numbers of immigrants where integration is the only expectation.



## **Choosing between assimilation and integration**

If a host population wants to protect its culture from being changed by immigration, it realistically wants assimilation rather than integration.

If it accepts some cultural change but does not want incoming cultures to become dominant, it needs to consider which immigrants receive citizenship and voting power.

The host population may need to revoke visas and denaturalise non-ancestral citizens if demographic change becomes problematic for maintaining its culture.

Integration is easier to achieve, but it increases the importance of understanding group differences and retaining the ability to reverse changes that are no longer accepted.

Sometimes an informed decision will only become possible after living with other groups and experiencing those differences in everyday life.



## **Advantages of monocultural societies**

Monocultural societies can increase the likelihood of maintaining cohesion.

Fewer differences in ways of life can mean fewer opportunities for conflict. There is less concern about other cultural differences becoming prominent and replacing existing norms.

A monocultural society does not need to worry as much about its culture being replaced. This could still occur slowly through demographic change, but such a society can be more proactive in preventing it.

A shrinking population should not mean that its culture diminishes or that its people will be replaced. Greater commonality can also speed up decision-making because there are fewer matters over which people disagree. Shared heritage and culture may produce greater alignment on certain topics.

Monocultural societies can maintain traditions with less disruption. Cultural evolution will generally be slower, more natural and influenced by the long-established ancestral group.



## **Disadvantages of monocultural societies**

The main risks concern changes in the working-age population. If there are insufficient young and economically active people, supporting a growing elderly population may become more difficult.

This does not need to be solved by giving citizenship to other groups. Labour gaps could instead be filled through temporary migrant visas.

Emerging AI and automation capabilities provide another possibility. Demographic problems may have several long-term solutions that do not require diluting the national culture.

Monocultural societies may also lack skills that could improve prosperity. Bringing in external talent can be faster than educating people domestically or developing alternatives. Temporary visas could help fill these gaps when necessary.



## **Advantages of multicultural societies**

Multicultural societies may fill skill gaps more quickly if they are open to external talent that brings its own traditions and preferences. This openness can improve adaptability to sudden economic or demographic changes.

Such societies may also offer more languages, arts, cuisines and perspectives, which some communities prefer.



## **Disadvantages of multicultural societies**

Rapid demographic change can reduce cohesion and trust as people become less familiar with one another and with the preferences, morals and values of different groups.

This can make public goods harder to fund where some groups exploit welfare more than others because of cultural differences.

Groups may segregate into distinct community enclaves that reinforce their own cultures and create parallel societies.

Language differences can make communication and cohesion more difficult.

Laws and policies may become more complex because they need to account for different languages, values and preferences, as well as anti-discrimination enforcement.

Housing and public-service capacity can also become concerns, especially where demographics change quickly.

Religious differences and differences in values and belief systems may produce ongoing disruption and conflict.

Multicultural societies also introduce the possibility that the native and ancestral population is replaced by populations introduced into the society. This could mean replacing its historical genetics, ancestry, heritage and culture.

These characteristics are not equal and can differ substantially. The long-established ancestral population therefore needs to consider whether it accepts the possibility of its genetics, ancestry, heritage and culture being replaced over time.



## **Differences, preferences and the decision to accept migration**

Although assimilation may reduce some differences, others can persist. Even where assimilation is possible, the ancestral population does not have to accept migration.

Similarly, the absence of substantial differences does not mean a group cannot reject another group’s settlement in its country. A nation’s population can decide whom it wants to live with.

Considering group differences is useful because it helps a population assess how those differences may affect its future. The relevant questions include:

* What happens if demographics change?
* Which differences could become more prominent?
* Would the ancestral population accept those differences becoming a primary part of everyday life?
* Does it prefer assimilation, integration or no additional settlement?

The existence of potential economic benefits does not remove these questions about identity, culture and collective preferences.
