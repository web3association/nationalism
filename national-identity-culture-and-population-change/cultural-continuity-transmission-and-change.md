# Cultural continuity, transmission and change

Culture is a shared and learned system of meanings, values, norms, symbols and practices. It helps people understand the world and organise their lives. It is transmitted between generations, but it also evolves over time.



## **Culture and heritage**

Heritage is the cultural, historical and material legacy passed down from previous generations. Traditions, values, artefacts and historical infrastructure can all form part of that inheritance.

Culture includes inherited practices, but it also includes contemporary ways of living that may develop or change within a generation.

A nation may therefore care about both preserving what it has inherited and maintaining the everyday cultural environment in which its people live.



## **Why culture changes**

Preserving culture is inherently difficult because the culture of a nation-state can constantly evolve. Changes in technology, economic conditions and social preferences can alter the environment in which people live and lead to new cultural practices. Some existing cultural aspects may become less prominent or be replaced by other forms of culture.

Even a fully homogeneous country, inhabited only by its long-established ancestral population, would still experience cultural change.

There is therefore some inevitability to cultural change. Nevertheless, this does not remove the reasoning for adopting political systems that better protect a culture the population cares about.

Cultural nationalism can help slow the rate of change and make a nation more deliberate about who it invites to live there and whether newcomers are likely to assimilate.



## **Culture must be transmitted**

Culture is not inherited biologically. It must be taught, practised and passed between generations.

People from the same bloodline may assimilate into other cultures, particularly when raised abroad. Preserving a bloodline alone cannot reliably guarantee cultural continuity.

Preserving the populations that created and transmitted a national culture can provide a strong basis for cultural preservation. However, ancestral continuity does not automatically preserve the existing culture. Education, cultural transmission and participation remain necessary.



## **Ancestral and genetic influences on culture**

When considering whether groups can assimilate into another country’s culture, an important question is how much ancestry and genetics influence the culture itself.

One factor is differences in general intelligence between populations.

If one country has a higher average IQ, it may be easier for that population to create a prosperous society. Higher average intelligence may make it easier to create a high-trust society in which citizens cooperate in positive-sum games. A number of social outcomes could be downstream from a higher average IQ.

It is difficult to say that every distinct ancestral group has the intellectual capacity to adopt the cultures of other groups because of genetic differences in general intelligence.

Even if intelligence were not a factor, assimilation would still depend on whether those groups want to adopt another culture fully and abandon their own.

Ancestral ties to culture and heritage may also influence people’s interest in preserving another group’s culture at the expense of giving up their own.

Even where people can adopt another group’s culture, it remains a question whether they will want to keep it unchanged if their own ancestral group becomes more dominant and gains greater voting power.



## **Continuity and cultural adaptation**

A population may want to preserve its culture without preventing every form of change. The difficulty is deciding how much change is acceptable and who has the authority to decide.

If preservation is interpreted too rigidly, culture may become overly formalised, politicised or resistant to beneficial adaptation. Younger generations may reinterpret inherited practices, creating tensions between continuity and innovation.

Efforts to preserve culture can therefore develop into cultural gatekeeping, nostalgia politics or disputes about what counts as authentic national identity.



## **Shared culture and social cohesion**

Where people share a common culture, there may be fewer differences over ways of life and fewer opportunities for conflict over those differences.

Shared culture can make it easier to build trust, mutual obligation and a sense of belonging. It can also support public participation, civic duty and willingness to make sacrifices for the community.

However, culture can be difficult to define in full. The more complex it is, the harder it may be for newcomers to assimilate perfectly.

Where assimilation is possible, it can help maintain a common culture. Where it is incomplete, the host culture may be influenced by persistent differences.



## **Preserving culture through institutions**

Culture and heritage can be transmitted through institutions over time. Schools, language policy, cultural institutions, archives, museums, public broadcasting, national symbols and commemorations can all influence what is passed to future generations.

A population may use these institutions to preserve its language, historical memory, traditions, public culture and inherited way of life. The policy choices involved are discussed further in **Language, education, heritage and cultural policy**.
