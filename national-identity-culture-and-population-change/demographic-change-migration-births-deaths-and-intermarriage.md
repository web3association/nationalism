# Demographic change: migration, births, deaths and intermarriage

Each nation-state may contain one or multiple ancestral groups. It may also include people from the same ancestral group who came from different countries and have different cultures.

Changes in the population’s ancestral and cultural composition can affect how society is governed.

Understanding demographic change is important when considering how a nation’s population, politics and culture may evolve following decisions about migration, citizenship and voting power.

Four main factors influence demographic change: immigration, emigration, reproduction and death.



## **Immigration**

Immigration occurs when people move into a country. A nation decides whom it wants to invite, and that decision directly affects its demographic composition. This is the easiest form of demographic change to control. A nation can decide to stop allowing further immigrants at any point and can change which countries it accepts immigration from.



## **Emigration**

Emigration occurs when people move out of a country. People can emigrate where other countries offer visas for which they qualify. A nation cannot easily force another country not to accept its people. Emigration is difficult to control because preventing people from moving elsewhere would be excessively authoritarian.

Problems caused by immigration could influence people’s desire to emigrate. If incoming migrants commit crimes or exhibit behaviours the ancestral population dislikes, some members of that population may decide to leave.

Welfare usage can also be a factor. If incoming migrants rely more heavily on state welfare, higher taxes may be needed to pay for those costs. Some members of the ancestral population may then move to countries with lower tax rates.

Even small amounts of immigration from certain groups could therefore contribute to the emigration of people who no longer want to remain.



## **Reproduction**

Two key variables affecting population growth are:

* The number of children each family has.
* The average age of mothers when they conceive.

A higher number of children and a lower average maternal age can produce faster population growth. Even small differences in reproduction rates between distinct groups will eventually change their relative demographic shares.

All groups may be having more or fewer children than required to reach replacement level. Exceeding replacement level means the overall population grows. However, differences in reproduction rates between groups determine how the composition of that population changes.



## **Death**

When someone dies, they are no longer part of the population’s demographics. Average age at death and causes of death may differ between groups.

Genetic factors can play a role through predispositions to diseases and different health risks.

Cultural decisions can also influence lifestyle, including diet or the adoption of cousin marriage, which can result in inbred offspring.

Behaviour, occupation and sleeping patterns are further examples of environmental and personal factors that may affect when someone dies.

Other factors include involvement in dangerous crime or military operations during war. Internal conflict can cause deaths among particular groups and may also contribute to their emigration.

A nation could provide additional support and public services to groups experiencing preventable deaths. It could also try to discourage behaviours that harm health.

If sanitation, vaccination, healthcare access, medical screening and other health policies are already reasonable, however, the state has limited ability to influence mortality easily. Individuals make daily decisions that can affect whether they die sooner or later.



## **Intermarriage**

Another reproductive factor is whether people have children with members of their own ancestral group or other groups. Choices about whom to reproduce with change the ancestral demographics of the nation.

A nation could try to protect a particular ancestral group through targeted incentives supporting its families. Beyond such incentives, however, it has little control over reproduction because those decisions are personal. Each couple decides whether and when to have children, how many to have and with whom.



## **The difficulty of controlling demographic change**

Immigration, emigration, reproduction and death all influence demographic change. Immigration is the only one of these factors that is easy to control. A nation can stop further immigration at any point.

Emigration cannot easily be controlled because individuals have freedom to move if other countries will accept them.

Reproduction can be influenced to some extent through incentives, but there is limited reason to place confidence in those incentives as a reliable means of substantially changing demographics.

Mortality is also difficult to influence because genetics and personal choices affect when people die.

These realities around demographics increase the importance of understanding group differences before making migration decisions.

Demographics may change rapidly because of immigration policy, reproduction and emigration together.

A nation accepting a moderate or large number of migrants needs to understand and accept the possibility that their group differences could become dominant if those migrants receive a path to permanent settlement.

Over the long term, migration decisions could contribute to ancestral replacement, changing religious preferences or different social norms and behaviours becoming the dominant reality.



## **Citizenship and voting power**

The difficulty of controlling demographic change makes decisions about citizenship and voting power particularly important.

If migrants receive these rights, reversing earlier decisions through the same political system becomes more difficult.

It is not compelling to assume that migrants who receive citizenship would support policies that could result in their deportation. They have an incentive to protect their interests, including remaining in the country even if the ancestral population no longer wants them there.

Demographic change can also produce feedback loops:

* Migrant communities may develop networks that encourage further migration.
* Native emigration may accelerate local demographic change.
* Political changes may alter future immigration rules.
* Fertility rates may converge or diverge across generations according to integration, education, religion and economic conditions.

Ancestral populations do not need to give migrants citizenship. If they do, future conflict may arise over whether and how citizenship could be revoked if the ancestral population changes its preferences.

Problems associated with group differences may not be apparent when small numbers of migrants first arrive. As demographics change, those differences may become more visible and prominent.

This can create conditions for disputes and conflict if the ancestral population does not accept those differences at scale.

The ancestral population may then need to reclaim its self-determination to better guarantee its continuity as a distinct group.

A nation that wants more control over its future therefore benefits from understanding group differences before deciding whom to admit and who can become a citizen.
