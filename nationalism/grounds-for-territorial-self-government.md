# Grounds for territorial self-government

Groups have stronger claims to political self-government when they constitute an enduring historical or territorial community and when they have a substantial interest in preserving their shared identity, culture and institutions.



## **Why territorial self-governance is important**

Imagine if groups could not enforce any meaningful form of territorial self-government. It would mean that anyone would be able to live alongside them without any means to stop them from doing so. The people that come and live in the same territory as these groups may only provide positive contributions and be friendly to those around them. They might also not be involved in politics at all. Even if both of these things were true these other groups could still have a profound impact on the day to day life of those living in these areas. If these changes were not supported by the historical community it would mean they are being forced to accept changes that were never democratically supported.

A group’s identity may become increasingly diluted as people from other ancestral groups become part of the region’s daily life, bringing differences in language, social norms and cultural traditions. Territorial self-governance would allow communities to decide who may settle alongside them, restricting where people can live much as nation-state borders already do. This would mean fewer choices for individuals, but greater assurance that communities can protect their way of life. People could still lead prosperous lives where they currently reside or, if asked to leave another group’s territory, where they are ancestrally from. Without meaningful self-governance, communities may face disputes, conflict and changes that undermine their everyday lives and cultural continuity.

Ultimately, which is more problematic: denying communities any meaningful autonomy to determine their future and forcing them to accept a way of life they have not chosen, or is it limiting an individuals ability to live anywhere they wish in the world? Framed this way, the clear answer is to enable territorial self-governance. The freedom for anyone to choose where they live is desirable, but it does not amount to an unlimited entitlement to settle anywhere. Communities should have meaningful power to shape their collective future, but this collective right for self-determination does not negate the need to also respect the needs of others who need a place where they can reside and call home.



## **Proportionality and levels of autonomy**

Different communities may need different levels of autonomy to protect the things they care about. Preserving a language might require educational rights and support for cultural institutions, while protecting an established way of life might justify local or regional self-government. In some circumstances, a stronger claim to political independence may arise.

These arrangements can range from cultural protections and community institutions to regional autonomy, federal arrangements or a separate nation-state. The central question is what degree of authority is necessary to address the community’s concerns, while respecting the rights of others affected. A legitimate interest in continuity does not automatically establish a need for independent statehood when other solutions might be sufficient.



## **Distinct ancestral groups**

One potentially significant basis for collective self-government is an enduring historical community with ancestral connections to a particular homeland. Such communities may want to preserve their continuity as a distinct group with its own shared identity, culture, language, historical memory and institutions. These ancestral groups may be indigenous to these territories that they have historically controlled.

However, a reason to preserve a community is not automatically a claim to govern a particular territory. A territorial claim becomes stronger when it is supported by longstanding residence, an enduring relationship with the land, historical political institutions and the freely expressed preferences of the people currently living there. Its practical legitimacy also depends on whether self-government can be exercised without displacing others in a way that would make them stateless.

A distinct ancestral community may therefore have substantial grounds for seeking self-government, but the appropriate form and extent of that authority require consideration of both its historical connections and the people who would still live under its jurisdiction.



## **Historical communities formed from multiple ancestral populations**

Historical belonging need not be fixed permanently at the moment a state was founded. Later arrivals and their descendants could become integral to the national community through sustained participation and shared experience.

In this instance multiple groups might be happy to continue living together due to their deep rooted connections to a shared homeland, language, culture and institutions that have formed since the inception of the nation-state or over many generations.

Similar to nations formed by a distinct ancestral group, these communities still might not want other individuals to join their community and live amongst them. That might be due to a preference to preserve their identity and culture that has been formed over an extended historical period.



## **Religious communities**

Religious communities may seek greater autonomy to maintain their institutions, religious practices and organise aspects of community life around their shared beliefs. Where such communities have an enduring connection to a territory, this can contribute to a case for local or regional self-government.

It is important to distinguish a historically religious community seeking self-government from a government imposing religious observance. A community can have a strong religious identity while protecting freedom of conscience and equal citizenship. Religious identity alone does not necessarily imply religious domination.

The concern becomes greater when political authority makes adherence to a religion a condition of full membership or uses state power to enforce religious conformity. People born into the community have not necessarily consented to its beliefs. They may wish to change religion, reject religious practice or interpret their traditions differently. Their freedom to do so should not depend on leaving their homes or surrendering their rights.

This problem is not unique to religious communities. A culturally defined political community could also become coercive if it polices marriage, belief, language or personal identification in the name of preserving continuity. Across these different arrangements, collective self-determination must leave room for individual dissent.

Voluntary religious institutions and regional autonomy may provide appropriate ways to sustain a shared religious life. A separate nation-state requires a broader justification concerning territory, historical institutions, democratic support and the rights of everyone affected. In either case, the central safeguard is that minorities, converts, non-believers and dissenting members remain protected. Otherwise, self-determination for the community can become domination over people within it.



## Less suitable grounds for territorial self-government

Aesthetics, height, athleticism, intelligence, personality, interests and hobbies are some example characteristics or grouping preferences that could theoretically be used to form distinct groupings that might want to govern their own regional territory or to start a completely separate nation-state. These are generally weak grounds for dividing a political community or creating a separate state. They do not ordinarily establish a shared homeland, historical claim or a need for collective self-government.

It is not reasonable to try and use any basis of group formation to make a claim for territorial self-governance. These groups may want to disassociate from other members within their nation but this doesn’t then immediately justify any political authority over a territory. Trying to use these characteristics or preferences to determine citizenship or political rights would result in unnecessary amounts of exclusion for certain individuals within a historical community.

These characteristics may provide reasons for voluntary association, but ordinarily do not explain why a group would need political authority over a territory or over other residents. Someone's preference for a particular company is not equivalent to a justifiable claim for collective self-government. These groups could still come together in voluntary residential communities if they preferred but this would not be the same as a politically recognised group that can exert authoritative control over a regional area.
