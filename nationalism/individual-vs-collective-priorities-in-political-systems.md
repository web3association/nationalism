# Individual vs collective priorities in political systems

Political systems rarely satisfy all preferences and values equally; there are often trade-offs that need to be made. Whether a political system leans more towards prioritising the individual or the collective is one of those foundational decisions. The following comparisons focus on national political systems and the contrast between individual-leaning and collective-leaning solutions. These same comparisons could also be made at the regional and local level within nations. Different approaches and solutions could be adopted for different population groupings.

Individual-leaning and collective-leaning approaches should not be treated as total opposites. Most political systems combine both. A state may protect individual rights while also preserving a national language, funding cultural institutions, recognising indigenous land rights or promoting social solidarity. The important question is not whether a system is purely individual or purely collective, but which side is prioritised when there is conflict between individual freedom and collective continuity.



## **Ideological comparisons**

Some broad comparisons between different ideas and approaches can help with understanding the problems and priorities that each political system could focus on. Each approach has been labelled with whether it leans more towards individual or collective based solutions. These ideas only broadly correlate with the individual versus collective comparison. They are not exact synonyms or strict opposites. Instead they are groupings of ideas that can be somewhat aligned with individual-leaning or collective-leaning political systems.



**Normative scope**

Normative scope is concerned with how far moral principles can reach and who they should be applied to. The core difference is not about whether rights or duties exist, but instead about where they come from and how far they extend. Two approaches include:

* **Universalism (Often individual-leaning)** - Believes that some principles, values or standards apply to everyone. A core premise is that what is right is not just whatever a particular group happens to believe. It advocates for common humanity, general principles, equal moral standing and standards that can transcend culture or tribe. Universalism has the risk of over-abstraction and imposed uniformity.
* **Particularism (Often collective-leaning)** - Believes that moral and political judgment cannot be fully separated from history, culture, tradition, community, social role and relationships. A core premise is that what is right often depends on the context and the specific community involved. It advocates for situated identity, inherited practices, special obligations and historical and cultural specificity. Particularism has the risk of excusing injustices and being exclusionary.



**Social ontology**

Social ontology is concerned with what people are like at the social level and how individuals and society relate at a fundamental level. The core difference is about the basic relationship that exists between individuals, communities and social wholes. Two approaches include:

* **Individualism (Individual-leaning) - B**elieves individuals are the basic unit of moral and political concern.
* **Communitarianism (Collective-leaning)** - Believes individuals are shaped by communities, traditions and social roles and that these cannot be treated as secondary.



**Political membership**

Political membership is concerned with who belongs to the political community and who will receive citizenship, voting rights and representation rights. The core difference is about what constitutes the nation, shared descent and culture or shared citizenship and political membership. The choice will have an influence on whether civic individual rights or ancestral group rights are being prioritised and who will have power in the political system. Two approaches include:

* **Civic nationalism (Individual-leaning)** - Defines the nation mainly in terms of shared citizenship, political institutions, laws and civic commitment. Anyone can belong regardless of ancestry, provided they subscribe to the state’s values. It often leans towards equal individual citizenship, universal civic rights and non-ancestral based belonging. Often it means that any citizen can vote or become an elected representative in government. The government primarily represents the interests of all citizens. Civic nationalism risks being too abstract, underestimating the importance of culture and historical identity and preserving it and failing to generate deep solidarity.
* **Ancestral nationalism (Collective-leaning)** - Defines the nation mainly in terms of shared ancestry and heritage. The state would have a responsibility to protect that specific group. Ancestral nationalism leans towards a stronger importance of collective identity, preference for the historic national group and is concerned with preserving the nation’s historical identity and culture. Sometimes it means that only members of the nation’s ancestral groups can vote or become elected representatives in government. The government would primarily represent the interests of that ancestral group however similar rights could still be given to other residents. Ancestral nationalism has risks around being exclusionary to minorities and immigrants.



**The significance of political membership**

Political power is what determines which representatives are elected, how the government is operated and whose interests it prioritises. The individuals that have political power can influence the direction of the country and the policies and laws that are adopted.

A nation could adopt more individual or collective leaning policies and experiment with the different outcomes that it produces. An ancestral nationalist society could still adopt laws that lean more heavily towards individualism and universalism. Moving between policies that are more universalist or particularist will generally be easier and quicker than changing political membership.

Rates of emigration, reproduction and death are difficult to influence and control. This reality means demographics can be hard to influence and control. This is one reason why political membership matters so much, it shapes who holds political power and how a nation changes over time as demographics evolve.



## **Other broad comparisons**

The following are some other broad comparisons that can help with thinking about individual and collective leaning political systems.



**Part–whole problem**

The part–whole problem is concerned with how wholes relate to their parts, specifically, whether a whole is nothing more than the sum of its parts or whether it has properties, powers or meanings that cannot be reduced to them. Two approaches include:

* **Atomism (Individual-leaning) -** Social atomism views society as a collection of self-sufficient individuals (atoms). The “whole” is simply the sum of its parts.
* **Holism (Collective-leaning) -** Social holism views society as an organic whole. It suggests that groups have a “life” of their own that is greater than the individuals within them, and if the group structure fails, the individuals suffer a loss of meaning or identity.



**Justice**

Justice is concerned with the principle of assigning rights, duties, procedures and outcomes in a way that can fairly regulate how claims are recognised and balanced between individuals and the collective. Both of the following approaches are often used within political systems, and neither is inherently individualist or collectivist:

* **Procedural justice (Often associated with individual-equality frameworks) -** Focuses on the process. As long as the rules are applied equally to every individual (blind to race or creed), the outcome is considered fair.
* **Substantive justice (Often associated with group-protective or outcome-focused frameworks) -** Focuses on the outcome. It argues that if a specific group is disappearing or being marginalised, the law should intervene with specific protections or rights to ensure that group’s continued existence. Broadly it is focused on whether outcomes and social conditions are fair in practice.



**Legal rights**

Legal rights are concerned with any entitlements and protections that allocate claims, powers and duties either to individuals or collectives, or to both, within a legal system. The following are idealised approaches, in practice legal systems often combine predominantly individual rights with some collective rights or protections:

* **Predominantly individual-rights model (Individual-leaning)** - This model treats rights as belonging exclusively to individuals, not to any group, ancestry, culture or collective entity. Laws are universal and impersonal as everyone would be equal before the law. Citizenship is often civic and based on shared political principles and residence. The risks are that it may weaken social cohesion, inherited majorities, traditional identities or minority cultural continuity if no group protections exist.
* **Predominantly collective-rights model (Collective-leaning)** - This model treats rights as belonging or at least in part to groups whether that be ancestral, cultural, linguistic or religious. The state recognises and protects the collective right of groups to preserve their identity, self-determination, cultural practices, language, land or institutions. Individual rights still exist but might be limited or shaped by group membership. Citizenship or political status may be partly or entirely based on ancestry. The risks are that it may entrench hierarchy, exclusion and unequal treatment.



## **Comparing individual vs collective political systems**

A number of topic areas can be mentioned to help with comparing individual-leaning and collective-leaning political systems.



**What gives a political system legitimacy? (Legitimacy)**

* **Individual** - Legitimacy is grounded in the individual. It usually comes in the form of rights and consent. The system is considered legitimate as it protects personal freedom, autonomy and equal legal status.
* **Collective** - Legitimacy is grounded in the collective. It usually comes in the form of a shared identity such as ancestry or culture. The system is considered legitimate because it helps to maintain social cohesion, solidarity and culture and it also protects a group identity and continuity.



**How are long-established ancestral groups and minorities protected? (Continuity & protection)**

* **Individual** - Individuals are commonly protected with equal rights as individual citizens. Emphasis is placed onto ensuring there is freedom of speech, religion, association and culture with anti-discrimination rules. Constitutional limits on majority power might exist and groups may also protect themselves through voluntary association. An individual approach can often overlook the need for special recognition and protections to preserve long-established ancestral groups or minority groups and their language, culture and political voice. Group protection is only as strong as the majority’s support and enforcement of those policies that protect smaller groups. If the group majority changes over time due to demographic change there is a risk that newer groups don’t have the same attitudes towards protecting other groups than the historical majority might have had. The risks are around the continuity of distinct groups such as long-established ancestral populations or minority groups as all people should be treated the same.
* **Collective** - Groups are commonly protected with a recognised status and group rights or cultural rights. Sometimes this can mean reserved representation or power-sharing. Policies might be adopted to preserve the long-established ancestral populations’ own language, culture and traditions but also to protect minority groups as well. A collective approach can be better at protecting distinct communities but it can also result in pressuring conformity into the dominant vision from distinct groups and their idea of what the group and its culture represents. Collective guarantees may be considered to prevent minorities from being absorbed or dominated by the majority. In an ancestral nationalist system, only some ancestral groups might be able to participate in politics, this protects their continuity and way of life over the long term. The downside of this is it can feel exclusionary to other groups that are residents in the country.



**How is social trust generated or eroded under each approach? (Trust)**

* **Individual** - Trust is generated through rules, rights and neutrality. People trust the system when laws apply equally to everyone, contracts are enforced and rights are protected. Institutions are neutral and predictable. Trust is eroded when inequality becomes too large, institutions only serve the powerful, extreme individualism weakens solidarity and social fragmentation leads to isolation. A main risk is that people could feel disconnected from one another. Low-trust societies or diverse ones may require stronger universal rules and individual rights to prevent free-riding or conflict. Individual systems can scale trust through strong institutions rather than blood or culture.
* **Collective** - Trust is generated through shared identity, belonging, common purpose and solidarity. People trust the system when they feel part of the same community, there is a strong sense of mutual obligation, institutions visibly promote the common good and public life encourages cooperation and belonging. Trust can be eroded when the collective excludes the dissenters or minorities, people feel pressured to conform and divisions over who belongs can weaken shared identity. A main risk is that people may feel united but only if they fit in the dominant idea and identity of the community. High-trust societies that are often more homogenous or communitarian can sometimes more easily sustain collective systems with lower formal enforcement. Collective systems can build intense in-group trust but they have the risk of out-group suspicion.



**Do people have a right of exit and a right to a voice so that they can share internal dissent and suggest reform? (Exit and voice rights)**

The tension between exit and voice concerns how a political system protects individuals within recognised communities. An exit-based model holds that group autonomy is acceptable so long as members remain free to leave. A voice-based model argues that this is insufficient, because many people cannot leave without losing family, identity, economic security or social existence itself. Justice requires not only the right of departure but also meaningful internal rights of dissent, participation and reform.

* **Individual** - Rights of exit and voice are usually central in individual-leaning systems because the individual is politically primary. Purely individualist systems may tolerate fragmentation which can cause friction between groups.
* **Collective** - In a more collective system, exit and voice are often more conditional, because the group’s continuity and authority matter more, though members could still retain meaningful rights of exit and voice. Purely collective systems risk suppressing dissent in the name of group harmony. Opt-out options could include federalism or emigration.



**How does the system impact cultural integrity and collective survival? (Culture & collective continuity)**

* **Individual** - The focus is on the individual and equal rights under the law. Protecting a distinct culture can become more difficult as culture would be influenced by all citizens in the country. Traditions can become more voluntary and less binding. A group may preserve itself, but not by trapping or silencing its members. So cultural survival must be compatible with individual freedom. As the demographics change the preferences, attitudes and norms of that population may change over time. Collective survival for certain groups could become increasingly dependent on the will and preferences of the dominant population groups. Groups and their distinct identity may struggle to reproduce themselves across generations.
* **Collective** - The focus is on the collective and protecting a certain group. Protecting a distinct culture is a lot easier as the group can more easily maintain its norms, boundaries and inherited practices more effectively. Traditions are less exposed to constant internal fragmentation. A collective system should often be more reliable for protecting a distinct group’s survival over the long term as reproduction, stability and political continuity can be more easily prioritised.



**Can a political system ever be truly neutral between identities, cultures and moral worldviews? (Neutrality)**

* **Individual** - Universalist or civic models often claim neutrality, but in practice states usually reflect some dominant language, history, public culture or moral assumptions. The groups with the largest share of the population will have a meaningful influence on the identity, culture and morals of the country. Institutions might be neutral; however, demographic change will have a role in changing those institutions over time and what those systems prioritise. Certain morals and values might be adopted as a shared form of identity though this thin form of identity might not be enough to maintain social cohesion over the long term or during a crisis.
* **Collective** - Collective systems are less neutral by default as they usually define politics around some shared identity, common good or moral purpose. They often affirm a national identity, culture and collective duties and values.



**How resilient is the system in crisis such as war, rapid demographic change or economic collapse? (Resilience)**

* **Individual -** An individual-leaning solution may draw resilience through flexibility, rights, institutional checks, pluralism and adaptability. Resilience could be achieved due to strong institutions, legal predictability and capacity for criticism and correction. Individual systems can be adaptable but divided and can struggle when a crisis requires rapid unity, shared sacrifice, strong collective discipline and acceptance of severe restrictions.
* **Collective -** A collective-leaning solution may draw its resilience through solidarity, discipline, shared identity and willingness to sacrifice for the whole. Resilience could be achieved due to social cohesion, collective discipline, readiness for mobilisation, strong legitimacy during emergencies and a willingness to bear burdens for the group. This approach can struggle when shared identity breaks down, leaders lose legitimacy, minorities are blamed or excluded, dissent is suppressed until failure or when rigid unity prevents adaptation.



**How will it handle group differences over the long term? (Group differences)**

* **Individual -** Tends to handle differences by treating people primarily as equal individuals, regardless of group membership. Politically these differences have a reduced importance. Group differences are tolerated but they might become more private, fluid and less politically functional.
* **Collective -** Tends to handle differences by recognising groups as real political communities that may need protection, authority or distinct status. Politically these differences become more enduring features of political life. Group differences are more commonly preserved, structured and politically recognised in durable ways.



**How does it handle demographic change? What happens if certain groups start to disappear? (Demographics)**

* **Individual** - Usually treat demographic change as acceptable so long as individual rights, equal citizenship and fair institutions are preserved. An individual system will often ask whether people are being protected fairly? An individual system generally does not treat the preservation of every group as a primary political duty even if this leads to assimilation, transformation or disappearance of groups.
* **Collective** - More likely to treat demographic change as a serious political issue because it may threaten identity, continuity and the survival of the group. A collective system will often ask whether the community can remain itself over time? Birth rates, migration, assimilation, integration and population balance can become central political concerns and protecting the continuity of groups would be prioritised even if this places limits or pressures on some individual freedoms and choices.



**How could the system evolve and adapt as identities change, intermix, or become less salient? (Adaptability)**

* **Individual** - Should usually adapt more easily when identities mix, weaken, or become fluid, because they are already treated as individuals instead of fixed groups. Individual-leaning systems will often be highly adaptable to globalisation, migration, urban mixing and cultural pluralisation however it can come at the cost of social cohesion, common purpose and maintaining high levels of trust. Individual systems are better at handling fluidity of identity but risk weaker shared bonds and common identity.
* **Collective -** Will often adapt less easily if they depend on stable group boundaries, but they may adapt well if they can redefine the collective at a higher or more flexible level. Collective-leaning systems will help with preserving identity, meaning and continuity of a distinct group but problems could emerge around handling identity transformation which can happen due to things like ethnic intermixing. Collective systems are better at preserving continuity but are at risk of being too rigid.



**When should you use individual or collective leaning political systems? (Recommended usage)**

A political system can adopt ideas that lean towards either individual or collective based systems.

Individual leaning systems are more suitable if:

* Society is deeply diverse and no shared culture can legitimately dominate. Some main dangers are oppression by the majority, state, family, religion or community.
* Protecting personal freedom, dissent and mobility is the priority.
* Identities can be more fluid, mixed or contested.
* It is preferred to have institutions that can handle pluralism without requiring deep agreement on a common good.
* When the aim is peaceful coexistence among people with different moral worldviews.
* You gain freedom and flexibility, but you accept it may result in weakened solidarity, shared purpose and cultural continuity.
* Individual principles to protect rights, dissent, exit and equality.

Collective leaning systems are more suitable if:

* The survival of a distinct people, culture, language or nation is genuinely at risk or this is the preferred priority of the political system.
* The society has a strong, legitimate shared identity that people broadly accept.
* The main problem is not personal oppression but disintegration, domination by outside forces, or loss of collective continuity.
* Long term coordination, solidarity and common purpose are more essential as outcomes.
* You gain continuity and cohesion, but you accept potential risks around exclusion, conformity and reduced freedom for internal dissenters.



## Combination approaches

Political membership is one of the most important parts of a political system. Membership determines who will have an influence on how the nation is governed and how it changes over time. A collective leaning solution for political membership like ancestral nationalism does not mean that individual leaning ideas can’t also be adopted. In practice, ideas are often adopted from either side of the spectrum.

Individual systems might be preferred when you want to maximise personal freedoms, flexibility and equality. Collective systems might be preferred when you want to preserve the identity and culture from distinct groups and better guarantee their continuity over the long term.
