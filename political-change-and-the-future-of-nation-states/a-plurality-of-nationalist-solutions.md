# A plurality of nationalist solutions

When considering the future of nation-states, three broad forms of nationalism are worth considering: ancestral nationalism, cultural nationalism and civic nationalism. Each offers a different answer to the question of what binds a nation together. Ancestral nationalism emphasises historical descent and continuity of a distinct people and their homeland. Cultural nationalism focuses on shared language, customs, values and traditions. Civic nationalism defines membership through citizenship, institutions and participation in a shared political framework.

The suitability of each approach depends heavily on the goals of a particular society. If the priority is openness, mobility and integration around shared institutions, civic nationalism may be appealing. If the priority is preserving a shared way of life, language and cultural identity, cultural nationalism may be more compelling. If the priority is protecting the continuity of a historically rooted ancestral population and its inherited heritage and culture, then ancestral nationalism should be the more reliable solution.

The future of nation-states may not be one where one nationalist model replaces all others. Instead, global society may benefit from a wider range of political arrangements, allowing different peoples and communities to govern themselves in ways that reflect their own preferences and priorities. These forms of nationalism can also be combined through different policies that might combine civic citizenship, cultural integration and recognition of historical or ancestral continuity.

It is difficult to change political systems, especially when changing from more inclusive civic nationalist systems over to more exclusive ancestral ones. It could be difficult or eventually democratically impossible to change a civic nationalist system using a general election when that same system might have given voting power to people who would be incentivised to vote against any policy changes that would negatively impact them. Revoking citizenships and voting power can easily lead to disputes and conflict. This is why it’s so important to consider the alternative approaches outside of the general election process that can lead to political system change.



## **The problem with adopting a single solution**

Different communities may prefer different combinations of civic, cultural and ancestral nationalism. These preferences reflect different priorities, and people may remain uncertain about which arrangements would best serve them until they can observe how those arrangements work in practice. A single national model may therefore struggle to accommodate the full range of ways people want to live.

Even if one approach produces better economic outcomes or greater stability, some people may still prefer another because they value different aspects of community life. Where meaningful alternatives are possible, requiring everyone to follow the same model can unnecessarily limit their agency. Local and regional autonomy could allow different approaches to coexist within a shared national framework.

This would create opportunities to compare systems, learn from experience and voluntarily adopt successful ideas. Some arrangements might become more popular over time, but those trends need not be permanent: communities should retain ways to reconsider their choices and pursue alternatives.

The aim would be a more voluntary and adaptable system, supported by clear laws governing association, governance and community boundaries. However, private membership arrangements differ from territorial government. The more an arrangement affects existing residents, public services or political rights, the stronger the requirements for democratic accountability and protections against unreasonable demands for exclusion.



## Legal system change

Legal reform may be needed to give local and regional communities greater autonomy over how they live, preserve their identity and experiment with alternative political arrangements. Relevant areas include local-government powers, electoral rules, nationality, freedom of association, anti-discrimination and data protection. Some changes could be made through ordinary legislation, while others may require constitutional amendments.

Changing the legal system would not necessarily require removing all forms of protection against discrimination. It could instead establish carefully defined legal structures for ancestral communities, cultural associations, local referendums and devolved government, while protecting individual rights. Laws would need to distinguish between voluntary private associations and public authorities that provide essential services or exercise governmental powers.

Legislative and constitutional reform can give experiments democratic legitimacy and formal public authority, allowing communities to demonstrate the benefits of different approaches. It can clarify participants’ rights and responsibilities, establish processes for forming and disbanding communities, and define enforceable boundaries between local, regional and national powers. A national framework could coordinate experiments under consistent rules, provide a peaceful route to political change and give courts and tribunals a clear basis for resolving disputes.

These reforms would also face legal, political and practical constraints. Governments must account for international obligations concerning discrimination, nationality and statelessness; any permitted renegotiation or withdrawal could carry legal and diplomatic costs. Different regional rules could complicate administration, while reorganising institutions, contracts and public services could create transition costs. Reforms without broad support may also be reversed by later governments. Democratic consent would therefore matter at both national and local levels, particularly where membership or access would depend on ancestry or culture. National majority opposition does not alone settle a community’s claim to self-government, but local majority support likewise does not immediately override the rights of other residents.



## Local and regional communities

Legislative change is the most compelling starting point for enabling a plurality of nationalist solutions, but some experiments with local communities and new forms of governance can begin within existing law. Voluntary communities, charter cities, special economic zones, autonomous regions, seasteading projects and negotiated political settlements represent different possibilities, although they involve very different levels of autonomy and some require specific legislative authorisation.

Changing an entire national system involves legal barriers, institutional resistance, financial costs and the need for broad public support. Local and regional experiments may therefore be more realistic in the short term. Within their legal powers, communities could make distinct choices about education, planning, cultural preservation, economic development, language policy, family policy and local institutions without requiring immediate transformation of the national order.

This approach allows people with shared cultural or political preferences to associate voluntarily without requiring everyone in the state to follow the same model. Local institutions may respond more quickly to community preferences and encourage participation by bringing decisions closer to those affected. Devolution may also reduce national conflict and help protect cultural and linguistic traditions without requiring sovereign independence. However, disagreements can persist within communities, so local autonomy also requires fair decision-making, minority protections and effective ways to resolve internal disputes.

Practical constraints matter too. Smaller communities may lack the tax revenue or administrative capacity needed to exercise additional powers, while wealthier regions may benefit more from autonomy than poorer ones. Local decisions about pollution, transport, housing and taxation can also affect neighbouring areas, making coordination and shared funding arrangements necessary. Even with these limitations, experiments in decentralised administration, cooperative economics and democratic participation could help communities compare approaches, adapt unsuccessful policies and provide evidence for wider adoption, with failures generally more contained than in nationwide experiments.

For communities seeking to move from a primarily civic model towards one that gives greater recognition to ancestral continuity, local and regional autonomy could offer an incremental transition path. Communities rooted in long-established ancestral populations could develop cultural and civic institutions reflecting their identity and preferences while respecting the equal rights of other residents. Over time, these experiments could inform broader political change, provided they remain legally accountable, financially sustainable and responsive to everyone affected.



## **Digital identity and democratic participation**

Local and regional communities have an opportunity test new digital identity and democratic participation systems as a foundation for wider reform. Credential-based systems, including self-sovereign identity (SSI), could give individuals greater control over their identity credentials and reduce reliance on centralised stores of personal information. For example, residents could prove their eligibility to participate in a local consultation without revealing unrelated personal data. Trusted organisations would still need to verify the underlying information and issue credentials.

These systems could make it easier and less costly to gather community preferences on infrastructure, education, policing, taxation, housing and cultural policy. Remote participation could help residents unable to attend physical meetings, while reusable credentials could reduce administrative work and, where accepted, work across services or jurisdictions. A town or region could begin with limited trials, allowing others to adopt successful approaches later. However, digital identity alone does not ensure secure or legitimate voting: binding decisions would still require appropriate voting safeguards and legal recognition.

Practical safeguards would also be essential. Accessible, non-digital alternatives would help prevent the exclusion of people without suitable devices, internet access or technical knowledge. Participants would need secure ways to recover credentials after losing a device or private key, alongside clear rules for software updates, credential revocation and dispute resolution. Privacy benefits would depend on the system’s design and governance, rather than following automatically from the technology.

Lower administrative costs could make consultations and referendums more practical, but frequent votes may cause fatigue or attract unrepresentative participation. Rules on notice, deliberation, voting frequency and participation thresholds would remain important, as would protections for minority rights. Capturing majority preferences is not a substitute for inclusive democratic decision-making.

Any proposals to certify ancestry would also raise additional concerns about definitions, historical cutoffs, mixed ancestry, evidential standards and incomplete records. Collecting sensitive genealogical or genetic information could also create risks of surveillance, profiling and unauthorised reuse.



## **Other opportunities with devolution**



**Economic ideas**

Greater regional autonomy could create opportunities to test economic policies such as land value taxation or demurrage-based currencies, which discourage holding money by applying a periodic charge. Carefully designed pilots could explore their effects on investment, spending, housing and public revenue within applicable tax and monetary laws. Clear evaluation criteria and safeguards would help distinguish promising reforms from approaches that create unintended costs before considering wider national adoption.



**Business ventures**

Regions with greater control over economic development could support industries and services suited to their local skills, resources and infrastructure. Simpler licensing, clearer planning rules and coordinated administrative services could make it easier to establish businesses and cooperatives. The aim would be to remove unnecessary complexity while maintaining appropriate protections for workers, consumers and the environment.



**Community building ideas**

Local autonomy could support co-living developments, shared workspaces, community-owned facilities and other projects that strengthen everyday social connections. Communities could invest in cultural venues, festivals, language programmes and shared infrastructure that preserve established traditions or encourage new activities. Involving residents in designing and managing these projects could help ensure they meet local needs and remain accessible to different generations and income groups.



## **Growing adoption of ancestral nationalism**

Local and regional experiments could allow people to observe how communities that emphasise ancestral continuity operate in practice. If these arrangements prove attractive, more members from the long-established ancestral population may choose to participate. Growing support could encourage a wider discussion about recognising ancestral continuity within national institutions or changing the balance between civic, cultural and ancestral approaches.

If national change were pursued, it would require a legitimate constitutional and democratic process. A consultation or referendum among an ancestral population could reveal that group’s preferences. Proposals would also need to address how to reform of withdraw from any international laws and treaties that prevent the planned change from happening. Revoking citizenship on grounds of ancestry would currently raise particularly serious legal and human-rights concerns, these would need to be resolved prior to changing the political system.

These difficulties strengthen the case for beginning with voluntary local arrangements and cultural institutions rather than attempting an immediate national overhaul. An incremental approach could provide evidence about benefits, limitations and public support while allowing policies to be revised.



## **A collective goal for the future**

A compelling future would make room for different combinations of civic institutions, cultural identity and ancestral continuity, rather than assuming that one model suits every society. Different peoples and communities have different priorities. Some place particular value on rootedness and preservation, while others emphasise openness, experimentation and change. These priorities can also coexist within the same society.

One long-term objective could be to protect the historical nations that already exist, along with their distinct cultures, identities and ways of life. These nations represent centuries, and sometimes millennia, of inherited memory and social development. Their loss would reduce the richness of global human diversity.

In the short term, local and regional arrangements may offer the most practical opportunities. Communities could develop institutions centred on ancestral heritage, preserve a shared language or culture, or emphasise open civic participation. Indigenous peoples’ self-government could also be strengthened in accordance with their distinct rights, treaties and circumstances. New experimental communities could exist alongside established nations rather than needing to replace them.

Over time, successful arrangements might attract wider support and influence national policy. Any broader transition would still require legitimate democratic processes and safeguards for people who prefer another way of life. The same commitment to pluralism should apply whichever approach becomes dominant: communities favouring greater openness should retain meaningful space within societies that place greater emphasis on ancestral or cultural continuity, and vice versa. Where legally authorised, differentiated regional policies could accommodate some of these preferences.

The broader goal would be a world in which people can live in the manner they prefer, within the communities they value most. If groups are able to exercise meaningful self-determination, they are better able to protect their collective continuity and way of life while allowing others to do the same.

Self-determination is central to this vision. Communities should have the ability to preserve what is important to them, whether that means maintaining ancestral continuity, protecting cultural traditions, building civic institutions, or creating new experimental societies. Some nation-states may choose to be more exclusionary in order to preserve a particular identity. Others may choose to be more inclusionary in order to encourage movement, diversity and rapid social change.

This allows global society to preserve the rich history of existing identities and cultures while also making room for experimentation and innovation. Protecting the diversity that already exists whilst also increasing the number of ways that people can choose to live together.
