# Difficulties with changing political systems

Political systems can be inherently difficult to change because they determine who holds power, who belongs to the political community and how collective decisions are made. Once a system has existed for a long period of time, it becomes embedded in laws, institutions, education, public expectations and personal identity. This means that even when people believe a different system would be better, the path toward change can be slow, contested and complex.

Changing a political system is not simply a matter of proposing an alternative. It requires persuading people, overcoming institutional resistance, managing disputes and creating a legitimate process through which the affected population can express its preferences. These challenges become even more complicated when the proposed change involves redefining national membership or voting rights.



## **Ongoing differences in preference and opinion**

Even if an ancestral nationalist political system were shown to produce better economic outcomes or maintain stronger social cohesion in a particular context, this would not necessarily create agreement that it was the only desirable system. People judge political arrangements by different standards. Some prioritise cultural continuity and collective identity, while others place greater importance on individual autonomy, openness or equal citizenship regardless of ancestry. Evidence of success in one area would not settle these broader disagreements or eliminate legitimate differences in preference.

This raises an important question: how much uniformity should a political system require from people who disagree about how they wish to live? Some common rules are necessary within any nation-state, but not every aspect of public life needs to follow a single national model. Devolution and local or regional self-government can give communities greater scope to maintain different institutions, traditions and policy priorities while remaining part of the same wider political framework.

Under such an arrangement, communities could voluntarily pursue different approaches to cultural preservation and social organisation. Those who favour liberal or universalist ideals could organise aspects of community life around those commitments, while others could place greater emphasis on ancestral heritage and inherited traditions. However, voluntary cultural association is different from governmental authority over everyone living in a territory. Meaningful choice also requires opportunities to challenge local decisions and change community arrangements, not simply an expectation that anyone who disagrees must move away.

A more decentralised system would not eliminate disputes. Communities would still disagree over resources, jurisdiction, national obligations and the limits of local autonomy. Preferences could also change over time, requiring arrangements to be reviewed or renegotiated. Nevertheless, this approach would recognise that lasting agreement on a single political ideal is neither realistic nor always necessary. The aim would be to accommodate a plurality of preferences without allowing either a national majority or a local majority to exercise unchecked power over those who dissent.



## **Willingness to pursue change**

Another difficulty in changing a political system is persuading people that change is necessary or desirable. Many citizens are raised to believe that the system they live under is not only legitimate, but superior to the alternatives. Schools, media, political parties and public institutions often reinforce support for the existing order. In some cases, this can create a form of ideological attachment where people struggle to imagine that a different system could function better.

There is also a natural fear of change. Even if a new political system is presented as an improvement, people may worry about whether it will actually work in practice. They may question whether the costs, risks and disruption involved in changing the system are worth it. Political systems shape daily life, rights, obligations and national identity, so uncertainty about a new arrangement can make people cautious.

Some people also attach their personal identity to a particular ideology or political tradition. For them, accepting a new system may feel like abandoning core commitments or admitting that a worldview they supported for years was flawed. This emotional and ideological resistance can be just as significant as the practical concerns of migrating over to a new system.

Because of these factors, convincing a population to support a new political system usually takes time. Even if evidence suggests that an alternative system could be beneficial, not everyone will accept that conclusion. This makes incremental adoption more realistic in many cases. Smaller experiments, local examples, pilot projects or gradual reforms may be needed to build confidence and demonstrate that another political system such as ancestral nationalism can work as intended.



## **Disputes and conflict**

Political change often creates winners and losers. A new system may give more influence to some groups while reducing the power or privileges of others. This can lead to disputes, resistance and conflict within the population. The fact that change produces conflict does not necessarily mean the change is unjustified, but it does mean the process must be handled carefully.

The most serious conflicts arise when a proposed system would alter who has voting power, citizenship or membership in the political community. People who previously had full participation in the nation-state may fear losing influence or status under a new arrangement. This can create strong opposition and may slow or destabilise the transition.

For that reason, legitimacy becomes essential. A political system cannot simply be replaced through force, manipulation or unclear procedures without risking severe division. If a distinct national community is being asked to express its preferences about the future, the process must be transparent and peaceful. Proposals that involve changing citizenship status, political membership or voting rights would also face major legal, ethical and humanitarian concerns, particularly if it involved denaturalisation or exclusion of current citizens. The more fundamental the proposed change, the greater the need for caution and safeguards.



## **Realities of a civic nationalist system**

Civic nationalist systems define national membership primarily through citizenship, shared institutions and commitment to common civic principles rather than ancestry and origin. Compared with other forms of nationalism, civic nationalism is generally more inclusive. It allows people from different backgrounds to become full members of the political community if they meet the legal requirements for citizenship.

However, this inclusiveness can also make it more difficult to change the system into a more exclusionary one. Once citizenship has been granted broadly, those citizens usually receive voting rights and legal protections. They then become part of the electorate that decides what future changes should occur. This creates a kind of path dependency where earlier decisions about citizenship shape the political possibilities with later generations.

The problems with these civic systems is that in many cases the long-established ancestral populations had little or no direct say in previous migration or naturalisation policies. From that perspective, they could claim that the current voting outcomes are influenced by citizens who would not have been admitted under a different historical system. A democratic vote should have been held in the past and this would have likely produced different citizenship rules.

This reality creates a major tension. In a civic nationalist system, naturalised citizens are legally and politically part of the nation. They have voting power and are incentivised to resist changes that would reduce their rights or exclude them. Any attempt to redefine national membership on ancestral grounds would therefore face strong opposition from those who would be negatively affected, as well as from others who support civic equality.

The existing political system may also make such change extremely difficult or legally impossible. Liberal democratic systems often contain constitutional protections against discrimination based on race, ancestry or national origin. A proposed alternative that explicitly distinguishes between citizens on ancestral grounds would likely conflict with anti-discrimination law, human rights protections and constitutional principles. Because of this, a separate community-led process, such as ongoing public advocacy or a referendum limited to the long-established ancestral population might need to be considered as a potential solution for expressing this preference to pursue political system change.

In many civic nationalist countries, there are already large communities of citizens who do not share the ancestry of the long-established population. These individuals are not outsiders under the existing legal order, they are considered members of the nation-state. Their presence and political rights make any transition to more ancestry-based systems more complex.

Education, law and public culture also tend to reinforce the existing civic framework. Supporters of civic nationalism may view this as a necessary commitment to equality and social cohesion, while others may see it as ideological conditioning that prevents alternatives from being considered. Either way, the institutions of the existing system make it difficult to challenge that system, even through peaceful and democratic means.



## **Complexities of adopting ancestral nationalism**

A further difficulty of adopting ancestral nationalist ideas is around creating a process that supporters would regard as legitimate for adopting ancestral nationalism. If the proposed system is based on membership in a long-established ancestral population, then the first question to answer is who qualifies?

This is a fundamental reason why trying to adopt a national solution in the short-term could be extremely difficult. A national referendum on whether to adopt ancestral nationalism as the new political system would immediately introduces practical, legal and ethical challenges.

A verification process based on ancestry will be difficult to design. If genetics were used, it would require reference panels, testing standards, privacy protections, appeal procedures and ongoing updates. Genetic categories are also complex and do not always map neatly onto national, cultural or historical identities. Any system that relies heavily on genetic testing would therefore face potential limitations that it needs to overcome as well as serious concerns about misuse and personal privacy.

The scale of testing would also be costly. Verifying large numbers of people could not realistically happen in a few days. It would likely take weeks or months, depending on the size of the population and the strictness of the criteria. People would need a way to prove they had been verified, perhaps through some form of credential or registry, but that could raise further questions about data security.

Transparency would be essential. Any process that determines who can vote on a foundational political question must be open to scrutiny. If people believe the verification process is biased, inaccurate or manipulated, the result will lack legitimacy. The same applies to the referendum itself. Voting procedures would need to be clear, independently monitored and protected against fraud or coercion.

The reality of adopting a fully ancestral solution in a nation-state that is currently civic-nationalist is that it would be extremely complex and difficult to execute in the short-term. These complexities with adopting a single type of nationalist solution can help with justifying a more pragmatic solution - embracing a plurality of nationalist solutions.
