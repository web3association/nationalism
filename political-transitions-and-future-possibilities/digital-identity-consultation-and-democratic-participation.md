# Digital identity, consultation and democratic participation

Local and regional communities provide opportunities to test digital identity and democratic participation systems. These systems could reduce administrative costs, make consultation more accessible and allow people to prove eligibility without repeatedly disclosing personal information. They may also provide foundations for wider institutional reform.



## **Credential-based identity**

Credential systems allow a person to hold evidence of a particular status or qualification. Self-sovereign identity, or SSI, is one approach intended to give individuals greater control over their credentials and reduce reliance on centralised stores of personal information. For example, someone might demonstrate eligibility for a local consultation without revealing unrelated personal data.



## **Verification and credential issuance**

A credential does not establish its own truth. Trusted organisations still need to verify the underlying information, decide whether the person qualifies and then issue the credential. The quality of the system therefore depends on the institutions and evidence behind it, not just the technology used to store or present it.



## **Local consultations**

Credential-based systems could make it easier to gather preferences about infrastructure, education, policing, taxation, housing and cultural policy. Reusable credentials could reduce repeated administrative work. Where accepted, they could also operate across services or jurisdictions.



## **Remote participation**

Digital participation can help people unable to attend physical meetings. It may reduce travel, scheduling and administrative barriers. A town or region could begin with a limited trial, allowing other communities to adopt successful approaches later.



## **Identity is not the same as secure voting**

A reliable identity credential does not automatically create a secure or legitimate election. Binding decisions still need appropriate voting safeguards, clear eligibility rules, legal recognition and protection against misuse. The technical ability to collect votes does not itself resolve questions about who should participate or what the result is authorised to decide.



## **Accessibility**

Digital systems may exclude people without suitable devices, internet access and technical knowledge. Accessible non-digital alternatives are therefore important. Participation should not depend entirely on owning particular technology or being able to use it confidently.



## **Credential recovery**

People may lose devices or private keys. They need secure ways to recover credentials without making the system vulnerable to impersonation. Recovery arrangements form part of the identity system’s governance rather than an optional feature.



## **Updates, revocation and disputes**

A functioning credential system also needs rules covering:

* Software updates.
* Credential changes.
* Revocation.
* Errors.
* Disputes.
* Recovery.

Credentials may become outdated or need correction. People need to understand how decisions are made and how to challenge them.



## **Privacy**

Privacy benefits depend on design and governance. They do not follow automatically from using decentralised or self-sovereign technology. A system needs to determine what information is disclosed, which organisations can access it and how different uses are connected. Reducing centralised storage can have advantages, but those advantages depend on practical implementation.



## **More frequent consultation**

Lower costs could make consultations and referendums more feasible. However, frequent voting can create fatigue. Participation may also become unrepresentative if the same motivated minority repeatedly takes part while others disengage. Rules may therefore be needed concerning notice, deliberation, voting frequency and participation thresholds.



## **Majority preference and political legitimacy**

Capturing majority preferences is not the same as inclusive democratic decision-making. Minority protections remain relevant. A system should also provide space for discussion, challenge and reconsideration rather than treat every numerical result as a complete answer.



## **Ancestry-related credentials**

Proposals for certifying ancestry raise additional questions concerning:

* The definition of the ancestral population.
* Historical cutoffs.
* Mixed ancestry.
* Evidence standards.
* Incomplete records.
* Changes in reference information.

The underlying verification problem remains even if the result is stored in a digital credential.



## **Sensitive information**

Genealogical and genetic data can create risks of surveillance, profiling, unauthorised reuse and disclosure of family information. Any digital arrangement handling such information needs additional safeguards. The detailed evidence and data-governance questions are addressed in **Evidence and technical reference**.



## **A gradual approach**

Local trials could begin with limited consultations or eligibility checks. This would allow communities to examine:

* Administrative savings.
* Accessibility.
* Public confidence.
* Recovery procedures.
* Privacy.
* Dispute handling.

Successful systems could then be considered for broader use. Digital participation is therefore an opportunity for experimentation, not a substitute for legitimate political institutions.
