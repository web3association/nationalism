# Existing citizenship, denaturalisation and security of residence

Changing future admission rules is different from changing the status of people already living in a country. Existing citizens and long-term residents have established legal positions, family relationships and expectations. A transition between political systems therefore needs to consider not only the preferred future membership rule but also how existing arrangements will be handled.



## **Citizenship as a lasting status**

Citizenship has historically been intended as something that is permanent. It provides a person with political rights, legal protection and a secure national home. Allowing governments to discard citizens whenever political preferences change would make everyone’s status less secure.

At the same time, an absolute prohibition on reconsidering citizenship could restrict a nation’s ability to redefine political membership and pursue ancestral self-determination. If a distinct nation is not able to disassociate from others it could mean they are being forced to live or be governed by people that they are not aligned with.

These competing concerns are central to any proposed transition.



## **Why a population might seek change**

A historically homogeneous population may become substantially more diverse after an extended period of immigration and naturalisation. Arrangements initially accepted may later become disputed because of:

* Cultural differences.
* Institutional pressures.
* Changes in political influence.
* Cumulative demographic effects.
* Changing preferences.

A historical population may then seek a different political or demographic direction. Sustained support for such a change should be able to find political expression.



## **Denaturalisation**

Denaturalisation is the revocation of citizenship previously granted through naturalisation. Most states already permit citizenship revocation in limited circumstances.

The broader proposal considered here is that a historical population may need to reconsider earlier citizenship grants to recover self-determination. From this perspective, treating every grant as permanently irreversible could prevent the community from withdrawing membership from people it no longer considers entitled to participate in its institutions.

This would change some peoples established legal status and can lead to risks around conflict, exclusion and insecurity.



## **Existing citizens are not merely applicants**

People already recognised as citizens are members under the current legal order. They possess rights and generally have voting power. They are therefore likely to resist reforms that reduce their status, and other citizens may support them on grounds of civic equality. This is one reason a transition from civic to ancestral membership is more difficult than the simpler decision around deciding who may naturalise in the future.



## **Historical records and recent naturalisation**

Where a state seeks to reconsider naturalisation from recent decades, existing administrative records may provide broad or near-complete coverage. The task would focus on identifying people for exclusion rather than verifying the ancestry of every person in the country.

This may be simpler and faster than population-wide ancestral verification. However, reversing recent naturalisation would not by itself establish an enduring ancestry-based membership system.

It would move the system back toward an earlier population position while leaving future demographic change possible under the remaining civic rules. The documentary methods are examined in **Documentary genealogy and historical records**.



## **Preserving status while cases are reviewed**

A transition may encounter people whose ancestral position is unclear. Reasons may include:

* Missing records.
* Adoption.
* Historical displacement.
* Incomplete reference data.
* Mixed ancestry.
* Uncertain links to another country.

The proposed approach is that people retain legal status while evidence is reviewed. Possible arrangements discussed in the source include transitional citizenship, permanent residence or another protected legal category. Uncertainty should not be treated as proof that a person belongs nowhere.



## **Statelessness**

Citizenship deprivation may leave someone without any nationality if no other state recognises them. To avoid this, citizenship should not be revoked unless another secure nationality has already been obtained or is guaranteed through an enforceable arrangement. A connection by ancestry is not the same as an existing legal right to citizenship.



## **Refusal by a receiving state**

A state is not automatically required to admit someone merely because their ancestors came from there. This is particularly important where the person:

* Was born elsewhere.
* Grew up elsewhere.
* Has never held that country’s citizenship.
* Has little practical connection to it.

A proposed receiving state may refuse admission or nationality. The state seeking to change membership therefore cannot assume that an ancestral destination is available.



## **Alternative receiving arrangements**

When an ancestral state refuses admission, agreements might be negotiated with another state willing to provide secure residence and citizenship. Financial assistance or other compensation could form part of such an agreement.

The proposed safeguards are that citizenship be genuine and durable, and admission secured before existing citizenship is removed. Without those guarantees, the arrangement could leave a person stateless or without a secure place to live.



## **Repatriation and departure**

A historical population may seek the voluntary or compulsory departure of residents it does not regard as members of the ancestral nation. Different measures need to be distinguished:

* Voluntary assisted return.
* Denaturalisation.
* Deportation.
* Compulsory population transfer.

These are not interchangeable arrangements. Any proposed departure requires consideration of the person’s legal destination and whether secure residence or citizenship is actually available.



## **Residence without citizenship**

Long-term or perpetual visas are proposed elsewhere in the analysis as an alternative to naturalisation. They can allow people to live and work in a country without becoming permanent political members.

The potential advantage of this is that visa conditions can be changed, or residence ended, where the historical population’s preferences or circumstances change.

However, people may build families and substantial connections while living under such arrangements. The security offered by the visa therefore matters to whether migrants will choose to settle.



## **Grace periods**

The proposed response to disruption from visa revocation is to provide sufficient time to leave. A grace period could be fixed, variable or related to historical contribution. The purpose would be to reduce sudden disruption to employment, children and family life. The residence arrangements themselves are set out in **Residence, citizenship and national belonging**.



## **Political rights and legal residence**

A transition might distinguish citizenship from political membership. Some people could retain legal belonging while having more limited national voting or office-holding rights. Such an arrangement may reduce statelessness but create unequal political status. The stricter the national participation rules, the more important the source considers:

* Local autonomy.
* Clearly defined rights.
* Appeals.
* Due process.
* Limits on national power.

The proposed boundaries are examined in **Descent-based membership: boundaries and contested cases**.



## **Territorial separation**

Not every membership dispute needs to be addressed through individual citizenship changes. Where populations have deeply incompatible identities or constitutional aspirations, national separation may be considered. This requires decisions about boundaries, consent, citizenship, property and institutions and minorities remaining within each new jurisdiction. Territorial alternatives are discussed in **Special jurisdictions, free association and independent statehood**.



## **Transitional principles**

Reclaiming ancestral self-determination could come with several practical requirements:

* Avoid abrupt changes where possible.
* Define rules and timelines.
* Provide opportunities to challenge decisions.
* Retain status during unresolved proceedings.
* Avoid statelessness.
* Confirm receiving arrangements rather than assume them.
* Address legal and international constraints before implementation.

These requirements do not eliminate disagreement about the transition. They form part of the attempt to make a proposed change more stable and administratively workable.
