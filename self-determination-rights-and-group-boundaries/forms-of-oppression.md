## Outward oppression

Outward oppression occurs when a state or population uses coercive power against another people. It may include military aggression, territorial conquest, forced displacement, political subjugation or the appropriation of land and natural resources.

Outward oppression is often comparatively visible because its perpetrators, victims, territorial objectives, and material consequences can be readily identified. This visibility may make it easier to construct a moral and political case for resistance or external intervention. Although governments may disagree about the facts or appropriate response, foreign domination is generally easier to recognise than coercion operating through the internal institutions of a society.



## Inward oppression

Inward oppression occurs when institutions within a political community impose arrangements that unfairly prevent part of the population from preserving its way of life or exercising meaningful collective self-determination.

For example, a majority may support large-scale immigration and expansive naturalisation, while a substantial minority of the historical population opposes the resulting demographic and cultural transformation. The fact that a minority loses a particular vote would not, by itself, constitute oppression. Instead, inward oppression may arise when the affected population has no meaningful or enduring mechanism, whether at the national, regional or local level through which it can preserve its preferred way of life.

Inward oppression may be more difficult to identify because the policies involved can be presented through universal moral principles, such as equal human worth, or through economic arguments about the benefits of immigration. Those principles and arguments do not necessarily resolve questions concerning national membership, demographic continuity, territorial attachment or the distribution of political authority.

The burden may also be distributed unevenly: some historical populations may retain a substantially homogeneous homeland, while others undergo rapid and far-reaching demographic change without clear or sustained consent.

One possible response is territorial differentiation, including regional autonomy, decentralised control or the creation of separate political jurisdictions. Communities with substantially different preferences could exercise greater control over immigration, settlement, cultural institutions and local government within defined areas. Some territories might prioritise ancestral and cultural continuity, while others might adopt a more pluralistic social model.



## Political preferences and proposed collective measures

The following are some reasonable and justifiable preferences that people might have or collective actions that a nation might take when determining how they want to live:

* **Advocating different political arrangements** - Individuals and communities may legitimately advocate different political and social arrangements according to their understanding of national identity, political membership and collective life. Some may accept temporary or permanent foreign residents while favouring restrictive naturalisation policies. Others may support higher immigration and more accessible citizenship. These positions should be openly discussed and assessed according to their principles, preferences and consequences rather than these topics being excluded from political debate.
* **Transitioning between homogeneous and pluralistic models** - A historically homogeneous population may undergo substantial demographic change following an extended period of immigration and naturalisation. Arrangements initially regarded as functional or mutually acceptable may later become contested because of cultural differences, institutional pressures or the cumulative effects of demographic change. The persistent denial of meaningful control over immigration, settlement, ancestral and cultural continuity and constitutional identity can amount to national dispossession. Where a historical population has no practical political mechanism through which to express or implement its collective preferences, that denial may be understood as a form of inward oppression. Under this principle, a long-established population should be able to pursue a different political and demographic direction where that preference commands clear and sustained support.
* **Revocation of citizenship** - Citizenship is generally intended as something with permanent legal status. Nevertheless, most states permit revocation in limited circumstances. The argument for permitting denaturalisation is that any absolute prohibition could restrict a nation’s capacity to define its political membership and exercise self-determination as a distinct historical community. Treating citizenship as permanent can prevent a political community from withdrawing membership from individuals whom it no longer considers entitled to participate in its collective institutions. If a long-established ancestral population wants to more reliably protect its continuity as a distinct people it benefits from self-determination to achieve that outcome. Revoking historically granted citizenships might be necessary to achieve self-determination for a long-established ancestral population.
* **Repatriation** - An long-established ancestral population may also seek the voluntary or compulsory departure of residents whom it does not regard as members of the ancestral nation. Any such programme would need to distinguish between voluntary assisted return, denaturalisation, deportation and compulsory population transfer. Repatriation would require a lawful destination in which each affected person could obtain secure residence or citizenship as otherwise this policy could create statelessness.
* **National separation** - Historical states sometimes contain populations whose political preferences, identities or constitutional aspirations cannot readily be reconciled within a single national framework. Where there is clear and sustained support, territorial separation may allow two or more populations to form distinct states and govern themselves. Such arrangements require principles for determining territorial boundaries, political consent, citizenship and what the status will be for minorities remaining on either side.



## Transitional concerns

Although it is reasonable that a nation might want to transition towards greater ancestral homogeneity there are important concerns with how those transitions are handled. Some notable concerns include:

* **Risk of statelessness** - Denaturalisation may leave an individual without any citizenship if no other state is legally obliged or willing to recognise that person as a citizen. To avoid this outcome, citizenship should not be revoked unless another secure nationality has already been obtained or is guaranteed as part of an enforceable arrangement.
* **Refusal by another state** - An ancestral connection does not, by itself, create a legal obligation for another state to grant citizenship or admit an individual for permanent residence. A proposed receiving state may refuse to accept people associated with it by ancestry, particularly when they were born and raised elsewhere and have never held its citizenship. The state pursuing denaturalisation therefore cannot assume that an ancestral country will accept the affected individuals. Such a refusal could create an impasse: the ancestral population seeking to redefine its political membership would be unable to implement its preferred policy without leaving individuals stateless. One possible response would be to negotiate agreements with alternative receiving states willing to grant secure residence and citizenship in exchange for financial assistance or other forms of compensation. Any such arrangement would require binding guarantees that citizenship is genuine and durable and that admission is secured before denaturalisation occurs. Achieving this will mean that no individual is left without a recognised nationality.
