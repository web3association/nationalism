# Discrimination, prejudice, racism and equal treatment

Understanding discrimination, prejudice and racism requires distinguishing their meanings and the contexts in which they are applied. The question considered here is whether some applications are fair and reasonable and where they become harmful or unjust.



## **Discrimination, prejudice and antagonism**

These terms can be broadly defined as follows:

* **Discrimination** - Treating categories of people differently, including on grounds such as race, ancestry, age, sex or disability.
* **Prejudice** - A preconceived opinion or feeling not based on reason or actual experience.
* **Antagonism** - Active hostility or opposition toward an individual or group.



## **Definitions of racism**

Racism has a wider range of definitions.

Cambridge Dictionary includes:

> _**“Policies, behaviours, rules, etc. that result in continued unfair advantage to some people and unfair or harmful treatment of others based on race.”**_

It also includes:

> _**“Harmful or unfair things that people say, do, or think based on the belief that their own race makes them more intelligent, good, moral, etc. than people of other races.”**_

Source: [https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/racism](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/racism**)

[Dictionary.com](http://dictionary.com) defines it as:

> _**“A belief or doctrine that inherent differences among the various human racial groups determine cultural or individual achievement, usually involving the idea that one's own race is superior and has the right to dominate others or that a particular racial group is inferior to the others.”**_

Source: [https://www.dictionary.com/browse/racism](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/racism**)

Merriam-Webster includes:

> _**“A belief that race is a fundamental determinant of human traits and capacities and that racial differences produce an inherent superiority of a particular race.”**_

It also includes:

> _**“Behaviour or attitudes that reflect and foster this belief: discrimination, prejudice, or violence against people because of their race.”**_

Source: [https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/racism](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/racism**)

Collins Dictionary defines it as:

> _**“The belief that people of some races are inferior to others, and the behaviour which is the result of this belief. Racism also refers to the aspects of a society which prevent people of some racial groups from having the same privileges and opportunities as people from other races.”**_

Source: [https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/racism](https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/racism**)



## **The complexity of the term racism**

Definitions of racism that focus on the idea that some groups are inherently superior to others are among the least useful. Measurable differences and outcomes exist between groups, so the claim that every group is exactly the same is not compelling. One group may be taller on average, another may have a higher average IQ score, and another may have a higher prevalence of a gene that makes lactose easier to digest. Some of these traits may be considered desirable. Even small differences can therefore mean that one group ranks above another on a particular measure.

These differences may arise from genetics, environmental conditions, cultural influences or a combination of these factors. There is nothing wrong with proposing and demonstrating that particular differences are not genetic in origin. However, that is distinct from claiming that no differences have a genetic basis and that all differences can be resolved through environmental or cultural changes.

Where differences exist, recognising them allows more effort to be directed towards understanding their effects and finding solutions to any resulting problems. Treating the identification of differences as inherently racist, and therefore wrong, is neither pragmatic nor consistent with the pursuit of truth. Whether a country welcomes these findings or not, it will face the consequences of its migration decisions if relevant differences are not properly understood.

More useful definitions of racism focus on discrimination, prejudice and antagonism directed towards people because of their race. These may appear in policies and rules or in people’s behaviour towards others.

The central question is whether discrimination or prejudice can ever be justified and, if so, whether justified instances should fall within the definition of racism.

For example, a group may discriminate to preserve its self-determination and govern itself as a distinct nation. If racism encompasses every race-based distinction of this kind, that action would be racist by definition. If racism refers only to unjust discrimination, however, the question becomes whether the particular distinction is justified.

Those excluded may consider a restriction unfair because they cannot migrate to a country that admits other groups. They may also find themselves excluded by several countries. Nevertheless, the argument presented here is that prospective migrants’ preferences do not provide a compelling reason to override the preferences of ancestral populations in their own countries.

Forcing a population to accept groups it does not want to live with can be understood as oppression. Applying the term “racism” broadly across these different and contested circumstances has therefore reduced some of its usefulness and precision.

A proposed narrower definition is:

> _**Unjust prejudice, discrimination or antagonism against a person or people on the basis of membership of a particular racial or ethnic group.**_

Rather than seeking widespread agreement on this definition, it may be more productive to examine which applications of discrimination or prejudice are just and which are unjust. Two proposed categories for this discussion are **preference discrimination** and **precautionary prejudice**.



## **Preference discrimination**

Preference discrimination describes distinctions arising from people’s preferences about association, identity and the kind of community they want to maintain.

Such preferences need not depend on a claim that every outsider is dangerous, inferior or incapable. A person or community may simply prefer an existing relationship or way of life.

People may want to preserve their ancestral identity, cultural traditions or inherited physical characteristics. These can include patterns of hair colour, eye colour, facial shape, bone structure and average height, while recognising that individuals vary within every population.

Preferences may also concern differences in behaviour, practices or social expectations that people regard as problematic or do not want to introduce into their community. Reasons people give for these preferences may include:

* Their experiences with members of a group were predominantly negative.
* Trusted people reported negative experiences.
* Reliable data identify differences in crime or other outcomes by age, ethnicity, religion or another characteristic.

Personal accounts and group-level statistics do not establish how every individual behaves. Nevertheless, they may influence people’s perceptions of compatibility, risk and whether they want to accept a particular association.

Preference discrimination can also arise without a claim of danger or misconduct. People may simply prefer the familiarity and continuity of their existing community rather than wish to expand or change it. Whether acting on that preference is justified depends on the context, particularly the distinction between personal association, national admission and the treatment of established residents.



**Individual preferences**

Individuals choose whom to date, marry, have children with and spend time with. These decisions may reflect attraction, cultural familiarity, shared ancestry or a desire to maintain particular family traditions. An individual might also avoid a group they consider statistically more likely to commit crime, such as by crossing the road.

Preventing such personal choices would force people into associations they do not want. Respect for others does not require making every personal relationship equally available to everyone.



**Private-group preferences**

Friends and private communities may organise around shared experiences, identities or preferences. They may value familiarity and continuity rather than seek unlimited expansion.

Their decision not to include someone does not necessarily express hostility. A group can respect outsiders while preferring to preserve its existing character. Preventing that choice would constitute oppression by those imposing unwanted association.



**National-population preferences**

The same reasoning extends to national populations. A population may prefer limited immigration, particular forms of migration or no additional discretionary settlement because it values its ancestral identity, culture or existing way of life.

The prospective migrants’ desire to enter, or the economic benefits they might provide, do not override that populations preferences.

However, there are differences between states and private groups. States exercise authority over people who did not individually choose the arrangement. Selecting future entrants is therefore different from disassociating from existing citizens or residents due to risks such as making someone stateless. Preventing this national choice would constitute a form of group oppression which might be imposed due to ideologies like liberalism. The distinct responsibilities of territorial government, compared with private association, are discussed in **Membership boundaries and freedom of association**.



## **Where discrimination becomes harmful**

Discrimination can be more harmful when it is used within a society where people have already been accepted.



**Public groups**

Public groups may organise sports, activities or causes. Where a group is publicly open to anyone in the nation-state, it makes sense not to discriminate. If several ancestral or racial groups have been accepted into the country, public groups should not exclude them from ordinary participation.



**Organisations**

Businesses, charities and other organisations are necessary for employment and access to goods and services. Discrimination in these settings can prevent people from living normal lives. It is therefore reasonable to prohibit it. People accepted into the country should be treated like others in professional and consumer environments.



## **Precautionary prejudice**

An individual or group may know little about another group’s language, behaviour or culture. In such circumstances, precautionary prejudice is proposed as a way to protect against harm or undesirable outcomes.

Where someone has no prior knowledge beyond the fact that another person belongs to a different group, they may make decisions intended to protect their safety.

It would not be fair to expect people to disregard their safety when they perceive a potential threat. Individuals and private groups may act on such precautions.

A national population may also want sufficient information and evidence of minimal risk before accepting migration from a group that it knows little about.

However, this does not make the same approach appropriate in public groups or organisations. In those settings, the individuals already live in the same country and should have been vetted before admission. Public groups and organisations should therefore give individuals the benefit of the doubt where they lack personal knowledge.



## **In-group and out-group preferences**

An in-group approach allows the existing population to select which groups may live with it. That might mean accepting no other groups or admitting only some. Critics may regard this as unfair to outsiders who lose opportunities to settle.

An out-group approach prioritises outsiders’ preferences over those of the established population. If the ancestral population does not want to live with certain groups, this approach forces it to accept an unwanted outcome.

Out-group preference may persist where an ideology such as liberalism imposes it or when external groups can compel acceptance. These can be respectively understood as an ideological form of internal oppression or as a form of externally imposed oppression.



## **Equal treatment within the country**

Anti-discrimination laws can protect people who have been accepted into a society. A majority may support admitting particular groups while a minority opposes them. Equal-treatment rules protect admitted residents from that minority’s discrimination.

Organisations and public groups should not discriminate against residents in ordinary professional, consumer or public participation settings. The proposed distinction is therefore:

* **Admission** - The nation determines the scale and conditions of entry and settlement.
* **Treatment after admission -** Accepted residents receive protection and fair access to ordinary social and economic life.



## **Changing national preferences**

An ancestral population may change its mind about whom it wants to live with. It should be able to identify problems, alter migration policy and seek the departure of individuals or groups it no longer wants to accommodate.

This is intended to protect both the rights of accepted migrants while they remain and the population’s authority over national membership. The detailed questions of visa revocation, citizenship deprivation and departure arrangements belong in **Existing citizenship, denaturalisation and security of residence**.



## **Proposed legal changes**

Individuals and private groups already exercise substantial freedom over personal association. Anti-discrimination laws commonly protect workplaces and public settings. These protections should remain because discrimination in organisations and public groups is harmful.

The main proposed change concerns national populations. They need a means of expressing preferences about who may live with them because these decisions can substantially affect culture and everyday life.

The criticism of existing liberal systems is that they do not consistently permit these preferences to determine national migration policy. This can produce internal in-group oppression by failing to account for the preferences of long-established ancestral populations.
