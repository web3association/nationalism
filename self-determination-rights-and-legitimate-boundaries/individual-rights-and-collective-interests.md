# Individual rights and collective interests

Political systems rarely satisfy all preferences and values equally. They involve trade-offs, including whether to prioritise individuals or collectives when their interests conflict. The comparisons below focus on national political systems, but they can also apply at regional and local levels. Different approaches may be adopted for different population groupings.

Individual-leaning and collective-leaning approaches should not be treated as complete opposites. Most political systems combine both. A state may protect individual rights while preserving a national language, funding cultural institutions, recognising indigenous land rights or promoting social solidarity.

The important question is not whether a system is purely individual or collective, but which takes priority when individual freedom conflicts with collective continuity.



## **Normative scope: universalism and particularism**

Normative scope concerns how far moral principles extend and to whom they apply. The distinction is not whether rights or duties exist, but where they come from and how broadly they reach.

**Universalism**, often associated with individual-leaning approaches, holds that some principles, values or standards apply to everyone.

It argues that what is right is not simply whatever a particular group believes. It emphasises common humanity, general principles, equal moral standing and standards that transcend culture or tribe. Its risks include over-abstraction and imposed uniformity.

**Particularism**, often associated with collective-leaning approaches, holds that moral and political judgement cannot be fully separated from history, culture, tradition, community, social roles and relationships.

It argues that what is right often depends on the context and community involved. It emphasises situated identity, inherited practices, special obligations and historical and cultural specificity. Its risks include excusing injustice and becoming exclusionary.



## **Social ontology: individualism and communitarianism**

Social ontology concerns the relationship between individuals, communities and social wholes.

**Individualism** treats individuals as the basic unit of moral and political concern.

**Communitarianism** holds that individuals are shaped by communities, traditions and social roles, and that these cannot be treated as secondary.

These are broad orientations rather than exact descriptions of every policy a political system adopts.



## **Political membership: civic and ancestral approaches**

Political membership concerns who belongs to the political community and receives citizenship, voting rights and representation rights. The distinction between civic and ancestral approaches concerns whether the nation is principally constituted through citizenship and shared political membership or through descent and heritage.

**Civic nationalism** defines the nation mainly through citizenship, institutions, laws and civic commitment. Anyone can belong regardless of ancestry if they subscribe to the state’s values.

It often emphasises equal individual citizenship, universal civic rights and non-ancestral belonging. Citizens can generally vote or become elected representatives, and government primarily represents all citizens.

Its risks include becoming too abstract, underestimating culture and historical identity, failing to preserve them, and failing to generate deep solidarity.

**Ancestral nationalism** defines the nation mainly through ancestry and heritage. The state has a responsibility to protect that particular group.

It emphasises collective identity, preference for the historic national group and preservation of historical identity and culture. In some arrangements, only members of the national ancestral group can vote or become elected representatives. Government primarily represents that group, although other residents may receive similar rights in other areas.

Its risks include exclusion of minorities and immigrants.



## **Why political membership matters**

Political power determines which representatives are elected, how government operates and whose interests it prioritises. Those who hold political power can influence the country’s direction and the laws and policies it adopts.

A nation can experiment with more individual-leaning or collective-leaning policies. An ancestral nationalist society could still adopt laws influenced by individualism or universalism.

Moving between more universalist and particularist policies will generally be easier and quicker than changing political membership.

Emigration, reproduction and death are difficult to influence or control. Because demographics can therefore be difficult to manage, membership decisions matter: they shape who holds political power as the population evolves.



## **The part–whole problem: atomism and holism**

The part–whole problem concerns whether a whole is simply the sum of its parts or has properties, powers or meanings that cannot be reduced to them.

**Social atomism**, associated here with individual-leaning approaches, sees society as a collection of self-sufficient individuals. The whole is the sum of its parts.

**Social holism**, associated here with collective-leaning approaches, sees society as an organic whole. Groups have a life or meaning beyond their individual members, and individuals may lose meaning or identity if the group structure fails.



## **Justice: procedures and outcomes**

Justice concerns how rights, duties, procedures and outcomes are assigned to regulate competing individual and collective claims.

Neither procedural nor substantive justice is inherently individualist or collectivist. Political systems often use both.

**Procedural justice**, often associated with individual-equality frameworks, focuses on the process. If the rules apply equally to every individual, without regard to race or creed, the outcome is considered fair.

**Substantive justice**, often associated with group-protective or outcome-focused frameworks, focuses on outcomes and actual social conditions.

It may argue that where a group is disappearing or becoming marginalised, the law should provide specific protections or rights to ensure its continued existence.



## **Legal rights: individual and collective models**

Legal rights allocate claims, powers, protections and duties to individuals, collectives or both.

**A predominantly individual-rights model** treats rights as belonging exclusively to individuals rather than ancestry groups, cultures or collective entities.

Laws are universal and impersonal, and everyone is equal before the law. Citizenship is often civic, based on political principles and residence.

Without group protections, its risks include weakened cohesion, inherited majorities, traditional identities or minority cultural continuity.

**A predominantly collective-rights model** recognises rights belonging at least partly to groups, whether ancestral, cultural, linguistic or religious. The state protects collective interests in identity, self-determination, cultural practices, language, land or institutions.

Individual rights remain, but group membership may shape or limit them. Citizenship or political status may depend partly or entirely on ancestry.

Its risks include entrenched hierarchy, exclusion and unequal treatment.



## **Legitimacy**

**Individual-leaning systems** generally ground legitimacy in individual rights and consent. They are legitimate insofar as they protect personal freedom, autonomy and equal legal standing.

**Collective-leaning systems** ground legitimacy in a shared identity, such as ancestry or culture. They are legitimate insofar as they maintain cohesion, solidarity, culture and group continuity.



## **Protection of historical populations and minorities**

**Individual approaches** commonly protect people through equal citizenship, freedom of speech, religion, association and culture and anti-discrimination rules.

They may impose constitutional limits on majority power, while allowing groups to protect themselves through voluntary association.

However, they may overlook the need for special recognition of historical populations or minorities, including their language, culture and political voice.

Group protection can depend on the majority’s willingness to support and enforce it. If demographic change produces a new majority, that majority may not share the historical majority’s attitudes toward protecting other groups.

**Collective approaches** commonly provide recognised group status, cultural rights, reserved representation or power-sharing.

They may protect the language, culture and traditions of historical populations and minorities.

Such arrangements can protect distinct communities more effectively, but they may also pressure members to conform to a dominant interpretation of group identity.

Collective guarantees can prevent minorities from being absorbed or dominated. In an ancestral nationalist system, political participation may be restricted to particular ancestral groups to protect their continuity, although other residents may experience this as exclusionary.



## **Social trust**

**Individual approaches** generate trust through rights, rules and institutional neutrality.

People trust predictable institutions when laws apply equally, contracts are enforced and rights are protected.

Trust can erode when inequality becomes excessive, institutions serve only the powerful, individualism weakens solidarity or fragmentation produces isolation.

Low-trust or diverse societies may need strong universal rules to prevent free-riding and conflict. Individual systems can scale trust through institutions rather than blood or culture.

**Collective approaches** generate trust through identity, belonging, common purpose and mutual obligation.

Trust grows when people feel part of the same community and institutions visibly promote the common good.

It can erode when dissenters or minorities are excluded, conformity is pressured or disputes about belonging weaken shared identity.

More homogeneous or communitarian high-trust societies may sustain collective arrangements with less formal enforcement. Such systems can generate intense in-group trust while risking out-group suspicion.



## **Exit and voice**

Exit concerns the ability to leave a community. Voice concerns the ability to dissent, participate and seek reform while remaining within it.

An exit-based approach considers group autonomy acceptable if members can leave.

A voice-based approach argues that this is insufficient. Leaving may mean losing family, identity, economic security or social existence. Justice therefore requires meaningful opportunities for internal dissent and reform, not merely departure.

**Individual approaches** usually place exit and voice at the centre because the individual is politically primary. Purely individualist arrangements may nevertheless tolerate fragmentation that creates friction between groups.

**Collective approaches** may make exit and voice more conditional because continuity and group authority matter more. Members could still retain meaningful rights, but purely collective systems risk suppressing dissent in the name of harmony.

Possible alternatives or opt-outs may include federalism or emigration.



## **Cultural continuity**

**Individual approaches** focus on freedom and equal rights. Distinct cultures can be harder to preserve where all citizens influence national culture.

Traditions may become voluntary and less binding. A group may preserve itself, but not by trapping or silencing members.

As demographics and preferences change, collective survival may depend increasingly on dominant groups. Distinct communities may struggle to reproduce their identity across generations.

**Collective approaches** focus on protecting a particular group. They can maintain norms, boundaries and inherited practices more effectively.

Traditions are less exposed to constant internal fragmentation. Reproduction, stability and political continuity can be prioritised, potentially providing more reliable protection for long-term group survival.



## **Neutrality**

**Individual approaches** may claim neutrality, but states usually reflect a dominant language, history, public culture or set of moral assumptions.

Larger population groups influence identity, culture and morality. Institutions may be neutral in operation while still changing as demographics change.

A thin identity based on shared values may also be insufficient to sustain cohesion through a crisis.

**Collective approaches** are less neutral by default. They explicitly organise politics around identity, the common good, collective duties or a moral purpose.



## **Resilience during crises**

**Individual approaches** draw resilience from flexibility, rights, checks on power, legal predictability, pluralism and opportunities for criticism and correction.

They can be adaptable but divided, particularly where crises require rapid unity, sacrifice, discipline or substantial restrictions.

**Collective approaches** draw resilience from solidarity, shared identity, mobilisation and willingness to bear burdens for the group.

They can struggle when identity breaks down, leaders lose legitimacy, minorities are blamed, dissent is suppressed or rigid unity prevents adaptation.



## **Group differences**

**Individual approaches** tend to treat people as equal individuals rather than representatives of groups. Differences remain tolerated but may become private, fluid and less politically functional.

**Collective approaches** recognise groups as political communities that may need protection, authority or distinct status. Differences become durable and politically recognised features of public life.



## **Demographic change**

**Individual approaches** generally regard demographic change as acceptable where individual rights, equal citizenship and fair institutions are preserved.

They ask whether individuals are treated fairly, rather than treating the preservation of every group as a primary political duty. Assimilation, transformation or disappearance of groups may therefore occur.

**Collective approaches** are more likely to treat demographic change as a political concern because it may threaten identity and continuity.

They ask whether a community can remain itself. Birth rates, migration, assimilation, integration and population balance can become central policy concerns, even where protecting continuity places pressure on some individual freedoms.



## **Adaptability**

**Individual approaches** usually adapt more easily to mixed, weakening or changing identities because individuals are not assigned permanently to fixed groups.

They can accommodate globalisation, migration, urban mixing and cultural pluralisation, but may lose shared purpose, trust and cohesion.

**Collective approaches** can struggle where stable group boundaries are essential, although they may adapt by redefining the collective at a broader or more flexible level.

They protect meaning and continuity but can become rigid when identities transform through intermixing or other changes.



## **When individual-leaning approaches may be preferred**

These approaches may be more suitable where:

* Society is deeply diverse and no shared culture can legitimately dominate.
* The principal danger is oppression by a majority, state, family, religion or community.
* Personal freedom, dissent and mobility are priorities.
* Identities are fluid, mixed or contested.
* Institutions need to manage pluralism without deep agreement on a common good.
* Peaceful coexistence between different moral worldviews is the objective.

They offer freedom and flexibility while accepting possible reductions in solidarity, common purpose and cultural continuity.



## **When collective-leaning approaches may be preferred**

These approaches may be more suitable where:

* A people, culture, language or nation faces a threat to continuity, or preservation is the preferred priority.
* Society has a strong shared identity that people broadly regard as legitimate.
* The main concern is disintegration, external domination or loss of continuity rather than personal oppression.
* Long-term coordination, solidarity and common purpose are especially important.

They offer continuity and cohesion while accepting risks of exclusion, conformity and reduced freedom for internal dissenters.



## **Combining approaches**

Political membership is one of the most important parts of a political system because it determines who influences national governance and change. A collective membership system such as ancestral nationalism does not prevent the adoption of individual-leaning policies elsewhere. Individual principles may protect rights, dissent, exit and equality. Collective principles may protect the identity, culture and continuity of distinct groups. In practice, political systems commonly combine both.
