# Membership boundaries and freedom of association

Boundaries, association, disassociation and exclusion help explain how groups preserve their character, function effectively and respond to changing circumstances.

**Boundaries** establish what a group is, what it exists to preserve and how membership works.

**Association and disassociation** concern how people enter, participate in and leave a group, and whether the group can end an association.

**Exclusion** means membership or participation is not automatically available to everyone. Admission depends on the agreement of those entitled to decide, the group’s purposes and any obligations governing that decision.

For many relationships, private groups, professional teams and organisations, non-membership is the starting point. Most people remain outside, and only a small number are invited or accepted. Some groups are not looking for additional members at all.

This is not necessarily a judgement that outsiders are bad or incapable. A group may already be satisfied with its membership, size, routines and purpose. Even an appealing proposal can change something its members do not want to change.

Financial gain, greater output or additional talent therefore does not automatically outweigh privacy, shared history, compatibility, founding principles or continuity.



## **Couples and households**

Imagine a couple living in a two-bedroom home. A sociable friend offers to rent the spare room, pay good rent and share household work. The couple declines. They value privacy, intimacy and having a home together more than the additional income or assistance. Their spare room is not an invitation, and the friend’s suitability does not create a vacancy they are obliged to fill.



**Boundaries**

The boundary protects a particular relationship and domestic environment. The couple can speak privately, establish routines and spend time together without accommodating another resident. Even an excellent housemate would change those conditions.

Their preference does not require them to dislike the friend. Enjoying someone’s company is different from wanting to share a household with them. A boundary would provide little protection if every beneficial offer created an obligation to reconsider it.



**Association and disassociation**

Each partner freely chooses the relationship and can end it. They may associate with other people as friends without making those people members of their household or romantic relationship. An outsider cannot join simply by wanting to participate or demonstrating that they would be a good partner or housemate.

The couple may be entirely happy as they are. They do not need to create an application process, a waiting period or a set of conditions through which someone can eventually become a partner. Ending an existing relationship can nevertheless create responsibilities involving property, tenancy or children. Disassociation does not erase those obligations.



**Exclusion**

A monogamous relationship excludes additional romantic partners by definition. A household can also remain closed to additional residents. Exclusion is not an accidental feature of these arrangements. It protects the intimacy, privacy and shared life for which they exist.

The couple can be selective about friendship and even more selective about cohabitation. They may decide that no additional resident, however agreeable, fits what they want.

Other relationship structures are possible, but they also depend on boundaries and consent. The relevant principle is that membership is not something an outsider can earn unilaterally by satisfying their own idea of reasonable conditions.



## **Private friendship groups**

Imagine friends who met at university and have held fortnightly games nights for years. They share memories, routines and jokes. They recently befriended someone they all like and invite that person to parties, but keep certain games nights for the original group. They are not looking to expand every part of their shared social life.



**Boundaries**

Their boundary protects a particular relationship and shared history. They may want to revisit familiar experiences without explaining every reference, changing the group’s dynamics or accommodating another person’s preferences.

Different occasions can have different boundaries. A large party may welcome newcomers while an annual reunion or regular games night remains restricted. The group can value new friendships without turning every established gathering into an open invitation.



**Association and disassociation**

Members can form new friendships, invite newcomers to some activities or gradually include them in the wider group. They can also withdraw, stop attending or end friendships. The group may stop inviting someone whose conduct repeatedly undermines trust or enjoyment.

These changes often happen through mutual willingness rather than formal membership procedures. A person may be friendly, reliable and enjoyable company without becoming part of every friendship circle. There need not be a reasonable pathway into a particular gathering if its participants want to keep it as it is.



**Exclusion**

Most people are excluded from a private friendship group’s activities by default. Its members do not need to assess everyone who might enjoy joining. Selectivity protects familiarity, trust, manageable group size and the character of particular occasions. Adding another person can change the experience even when that person has no objectionable qualities.

A newcomer can become a genuine member of the present-day group if the friendship develops. They cannot retroactively share the original members’ university experience, and becoming a friend does not automatically confer access to every private event. Exclusion can therefore preserve an existing relationship without preventing all future relationships.



## **Professional sports teams**

Imagine a successful professional football team with a settled squad, strong coordination and clearly defined roles. Several talented players would like to join. Some could improve particular aspects of performance, attract publicity or increase commercial revenue. The club nevertheless decides not to recruit them because it is satisfied with its squad and does not want to disrupt its balance. The existence of available talent does not mean the team needs more players.



**Boundaries**

The team’s boundary protects a specific competitive purpose. Its effectiveness depends not only on individual ability, but also on a workable squad size, complementary roles, tactical understanding, fitness and reliability, discipline, trust, cooperation and shared expectations.

A team is not simply a collection of the greatest possible number of talented individuals. An excellent player may still be unsuitable for its tactics, undermine its balance or duplicate a role already filled. Its boundary allows it to preserve the combination that makes the team function.



**Association and disassociation**

Players may seek trials or contracts, and the club can decide whether it wants to recruit. Neither side must agree merely because a relationship could offer some benefit. The club may recruit for a specific need, decline further recruitment or maintain a settled squad for an extended period.

Players can leave and contracts can end, subject to their terms and applicable rules. A team can also reconsider an association when performance, conduct or its requirements change. However, existing commitments matter. Selectivity does not allow the club to disregard contracts or fair procedures.



**Exclusion**

Exclusion is the normal starting point: almost everyone is outside the squad, including many capable athletes. The team may be extremely selective because admission affects the whole group. Ability is necessary for many roles, but it is not sufficient. Timing, fit, teamwork and an actual vacancy may matter just as much.

The club does not need to offer every interested player a route to membership. It can reject even reasonable proposals because it is already satisfied with its arrangement. Without exclusion, it could not preserve a manageable squad, maintain standards or coordinate its members effectively.



## **Organisations**

Imagine an organic farm founded around organic production. Skilled farmers offer to increase output, but only if it adopts synthetic fertilisers and methods inconsistent with its standards. The farm refuses. Higher profits do not outweigh its founding purpose. It may also decline additional workers who accept its standards if it has no need to expand.



**Boundaries**

Organisational boundaries protect missions, standards and ways of working. Organic production is part of the farm’s identity and its promise to customers, not merely an incidental practice. The founders may value environmental stewardship, particular methods or certification.

Abandoning those commitments changes the organisation rather than simply making it more efficient. Boundaries also protect the organisation’s ability to determine its size, structure and direction. Growth is not automatically preferable to maintaining an arrangement that already works.



**Association and disassociation**

People can apply, and the farm can assess whether their skills, approach and availability fit its needs. Applicants can accept or reject an offer. The organisation can also decide that it is not hiring. A capable applicant does not create an obligation to invent a role, expand the business or establish a future pathway into employment.

Employees can resign, and employers can end employment subject to contracts, employment law and fair procedures. Partnerships can also be declined or ended under agreed terms. Protecting a mission does not eliminate obligations concerning discrimination, notice, wages or other legal protections.



**Exclusion**

An interested person cannot declare themselves an employee, manager or director. Membership requires an authorised decision. Organisations exclude most people from internal roles by default because unrestricted access would undermine competence, coordination, accountability and confidential information.

Highly functioning organisations may be particularly selective. They need not accept every capable person, and they may prefer not to change their membership at all. Unlike participation in a historical event, organisational membership can ordinarily be acquired in full: someone who is hired becomes a genuine employee. But the possibility of joining does not mean every outsider must be offered an opportunity to do so.



## **Nation-states**

Imagine a prosperous country of ten million people with an ageing population and labour shortages. A million people abroad would like to move there, potentially filling vacancies and improving their lives.

After debate, the country chooses lower immigration. It accepts some economic costs because it places greater weight on housing, integration, institutional stability and cultural continuity.

The fact that migration could provide benefits does not, by itself, settle whether the country should admit more people. A population may prefer to address its difficulties through other arrangements rather than substantially change its membership.



**Why states differ from private groups**

The comparisons above illustrate why boundaries and selective admission matter. They do not make a state identical to a household, friendship group, sports team or business.

A state exercises coercive authority over a territory. Most citizens were born into it, and non-citizen residents also live under its laws. Its decisions affect people who did not individually choose the arrangement.

Private groups may decline additional association without offering a comprehensive public justification. State decisions carry greater responsibilities, including existing legal obligations and the rights of people already present.

The analogy therefore supports the importance of boundaries, not an unlimited authority to treat existing residents or citizens as unwanted applicants.



**National boundaries**

National boundaries determine jurisdiction, admission, residence and citizenship. They also help sustain the institutions through which a population governs itself. A population may value a common language, inherited traditions, public trust, institutional continuity, ancestral or cultural continuity and control over its political future.

Admission decisions affect those interests because newcomers participate in everyday life and may eventually acquire permanent residence or political membership.

A country does not have to treat economic growth, population growth or openness as overriding objectives. It can prefer a slower pace of change or decide that it does not want to expand its population through discretionary immigration.



**Selective admission as the starting point**

For admission purposes, a person outside the country does not become a member simply by wanting to join or demonstrating that their presence could be beneficial.

Entry, settlement and citizenship are distinct arrangements. Permission for one does not automatically establish entitlement to the others.

In this sense, non-membership is the starting point. Admission occurs through the country’s rules, permission or an applicable entitlement.

The argument made here is that a nation should not face an open-ended obligation to create settlement or citizenship pathways for every willing outsider. Subject to its existing obligations, it should be able to decide that it wants fewer new associations or no additional discretionary settlement.

A population may be satisfied with its existing community. It need not regard every shortage or economic opportunity as a reason to redefine that community.



**Why selectivity matters**

Selective admission allows a country to consider whether a proposed association is compatible with what it wants to preserve. Relevant questions may include:

* Is there a need or desire for additional settlement?
* Can housing and services accommodate it?
* What cultural expectations will apply?
* Is residence temporary or permanent?
* Will citizenship eventually be available?
* What external connections or influences may be relevant?
* How could the cumulative effect change public life?

The point is not that every newcomer will create problems. It is that beneficial individuals or arrangements can still contribute to changes the existing population does not want.

Selectivity can help a nation manage the pace and character of those changes. It does not guarantee that its culture or institutions will remain unchanged, but it preserves a means of influencing how external participation develops.



**National association and disassociation**

A state cannot know with certainty whether an incoming group will assimilate or whether an arrangement that initially works will remain acceptable as circumstances change. Differences can generate competing political preferences, and compromise may not always produce mutually acceptable outcomes.

Political communities need some ability to reconsider associations rather than treat every initial arrangement as beyond revision. However, reconsidering future admissions is different from withdrawing established residence or citizenship. Citizenship provides political rights and a secure national home, not merely a place on a voluntary membership list.

Allowing governments to discard citizens whenever preferences change would make everyone’s status insecure. Disassociation therefore requires meaningful justification and attention to existing commitments.

A distinct ancestral population may argue that reclaiming sovereignty and self-determination justifies changing a previous political association. Indigenous status or stronger historical ties may also be advanced as grounds for priority. These claims require separate consideration of the rights and circumstances of everyone affected. The detailed questions are addressed in **Existing citizenship, denaturalisation and security of residence**.



**National exclusion**

Exclusion is part of maintaining a meaningful admission boundary. Without it, a country could not decide the scale, duration or conditions of entry and settlement. A selective system does not need to offer every prospective migrant a reasonable route to permanent membership. It may have no suitable route for a particular applicant, or may not wish to create one. This does not remove applicable obligations, including refugee protections, or turn established citizens into outsiders.

Ancestral and civic membership must also be distinguished. No one can acquire another person’s ancestry or retroactively share their family history. A newcomer can nevertheless learn a language, participate in traditions and develop lasting attachments. Whether those connections provide a route to citizenship depends on the membership model the country adopts and its governing rules.



## **Why boundaries and exclusion are important**

Many successful relationships, friendship groups, professional sports teams and organisations are selective by design. They do not begin with an assumption that everyone should be admitted unless a serious objection can be proved. They begin with an existing purpose or relationship and decide whether any additional association would support it. Sometimes the answer is no because:

* The group is already complete.
* Its members are satisfied.
* Additional membership would change its character.
* There is no vacancy or practical need.
* The proposal conflicts with its purpose.
* The benefits do not outweigh the disruption.

Exclusion protects the boundary by making these decisions meaningful. A relationship anyone could join, a squad accepting every capable athlete or a business required to employ every suitable applicant would lose control over its intended form.

Crucially, being selective does not require being hostile. A group can respect outsiders while declining to include them. Nor must every closed boundary contain a future route to admission. Sometimes maintaining the existing arrangement is itself the preference being protected.

States have greater responsibilities than private groups. That changes how their decisions must be justified and implemented, but it does not eliminate the importance of selective admission and the ability to disassociate with certain groups when that is justified.



## **Scale and cumulative effects**

Small numbers of newcomers may barely register in everyday life. Each individual case can appear too minor to matter. However, admission can be cumulative, with lasting consequences. Where residence leads to settlement and citizenship, present decisions become part of the future population, culture and electorate.

A series of individually reasonable decisions can therefore produce a collective outcome that was never clearly considered. This supports deciding membership boundaries, admission levels and settlement terms before cumulative change creates demands to reverse them.

It also supports distinguishing temporary cooperation from permanent membership. A country can welcome visitors, trade, exchange or particular forms of work without treating every association as a pathway into the political community.



## **Preferences within the population**

People from the same ancestral group can have different preferences. Some want to live among groups with different backgrounds and cultures. Others prefer their own group to preserve a particular way of life. Neither preference needs to be suppressed.

A national majority favouring one arrangement does not automatically entitle it to prevent dissenters from forming private associations, clustering voluntarily or advocating local alternatives. Nor does it automatically entitle the majority to remove the minority.

Immigration policy, local variation, regional arrangements and voluntary association can accommodate some differences. A country therefore needs both control over external admission and ways to handle legitimate disagreement among those already within it.



## **Boundaries and liberalism**

A criticism of liberalism is that its emphasis on equality can overlook differences and their effects on groups. Differences in identity, culture and capabilities may affect cohesion and productivity. Giving those differences insufficient attention does not remove their consequences.

The argument here is that an electorate opposing large-scale immigration should be able to express that preference and pursue selective admission policies within its governing obligations. Prospective economic gains or outsiders’ desire to join should not automatically override the existing population’s interest in continuity. Otherwise, membership can become an open-ended duty to associate rather than a decision the political community meaningfully controls.

At the same time, groups can make mistakes, preferences can change and existing associations create responsibilities.

The objective is therefore not unrestricted exclusion. It is a balance in which communities can maintain meaningful boundaries and decline unwanted expansion while respecting established rights and avoiding unnecessary exclusion or statelessness.
