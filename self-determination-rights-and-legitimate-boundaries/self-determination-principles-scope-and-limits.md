# Self-determination: principles, scope and limits

Self-determination concerns a population’s political status and its ability to make collective decisions and control its future.

The underlying idea is that distinct groups should be able to govern themselves and be free from the control and power of other groups. Each group should be able to decide how it is governed.

Self-determination is recognised in international law and reflected in treaties and United Nations instruments recognising the right of peoples to determine their political status and pursue economic, social and cultural development. Its purpose is to protect distinct groups and their ability to govern themselves.



## **Why self-determination matters**

Groups may want to preserve and pass on:

* Culture and language.
* Heritage and historical memory.
* Institutions.
* A way of life.
* Continuity as a distinct ancestral population.

Every meaningful group has boundaries concerning who belongs, who participates, what it exists to protect and the rules or values that hold it together.

For nations and peoples, those boundaries may concern language, religion, legal traditions, historical memory, ancestry, political institutions or relationships to a homeland.

If another group gains control over a people’s political future, that people may lose the ability to protect these things. Schools, laws, public symbols, land use, migration rules, cultural institutions and political priorities may gradually be shaped by others.

Self-determination gives communities a means of establishing collective boundaries around what matters most to them.



## **Historical connections to a homeland**

Self-determination is particularly important where a group has a longstanding connection to a homeland.

If its culture, heritage and identity developed there over generations, political control over that place may be central to its survival.

For indigenous peoples, historic nations, national minorities and other long-rooted communities, self-determination can protect against absorption, domination or loss of continuity.

Groups ranging from small communities to nations should be able to govern themselves and make decisions about their futures.



## **Internal and external self-determination**

**Internal self-determination** concerns a people’s pursuit of political, economic, social and cultural development within an existing state.

**External self-determination** concerns a people’s international political status in relation to other states, particularly in colonial or occupied contexts.

The distinction concerns where the right is exercised and what it seeks to change.



## **Equal participation and collective continuity**

Internal self-determination is sometimes presented as a right protecting political participation, representation, equality and non-discrimination.

However, equal participation does not fully resolve questions of collective continuity.

If new groups receive voting rights, their relative population size may grow through immigration, emigration and differences in reproduction or mortality.

Their political and cultural influence may increase until they become dominant. Over the long term, they might replace the historical population.

From the historical population’s perspective, this can mean losing self-determination because another group’s interests increasingly shape national life.

If the historical population then wants to end that influence, separating the country into two states may not appear fair. The newer group entered through the historical population’s invitation, yet separation might give it territory and a state against the original population’s wishes.

Giving migrants citizenship and political participation therefore does not always produce desirable outcomes.

If adopted broadly, an approach in which migrants eventually receive citizenship could contribute to the replacement of ancestral populations and cultures, reducing global diversity even where the historical populations never wanted that result.

The demographic mechanisms underlying these concerns are discussed in **Demographic change: migration, births, deaths and intermarriage**.



## **Which groups should self-determination protect?**

The central questions are:

* Which group’s self-determination should take priority within a state?
* Which group’s continuity needs protection?
* What makes one claim stronger than another?

Historically rooted ancestral groups and distinct societies with long-developed cultural and political institutions are proposed here as the two most compelling groupings.



## **Protecting distinct ancestries**

Ancestry concerns descent and origin. People may share a similar genetic ancestry through lineage extending across many generations. Shared ancestry may include adaptations associated with visible differences such as height, facial features, bone structure and skin colour. Such populations may want to protect their continuity as distinct groups.

One of the most reliable ways to do so is to secure self-determination in the homeland from which they originated. Ancestrally based self-determination can help prevent external groups from negatively influencing continuity.

Self-government can allow the group to prioritise its members’ access to housing, food, education and public services, supporting livelihoods and continued existence. Protecting distinct ancestral backgrounds is presented here as a compelling way to preserve human diversity.



## **Protecting distinct societies**

A distinct society may develop its own culture and heritage over generations, alongside land development and infrastructure.

It may have been created by one or several ancestral groups, which may also have ancestral homelands elsewhere.

The groups that built such a society can benefit from self-determination as a means of protecting their way of life, heritage and culture.

They have the closest connection to what they developed and experience of how it was governed to create and sustain its prosperity.



## **Distinct ancestries and distinct societies**

Protecting an ancestry often means protecting both the people and the society they built in their homeland.

Protecting a society focuses more directly on its institutions and way of life, although this may also require protecting the self-determination of the ancestral population that built it.

The claim of an ancestral group in its own homeland is presented here as more compelling than the claim of a distinct society formed elsewhere. The ancestral group has nowhere else to go.

However, this priority creates difficult questions where colonisation has produced long-established settler societies.

Historical connections, the development of infrastructure, contributions across generations, reparations and the feasibility of different territorial arrangements all become relevant.

A more detailed Australia example and the proposed ways of handling these competing claims belong in **Grounds for territorial self-government**.



## **Proportionality and forms of self-government**

Self-determination can be exercised through:

* Protected local communities.
* Cultural institutions.
* Federalism.
* Devolution.
* Regional autonomy.
* Independent statehood.

Different communities may require different levels of authority. Preserving a language may require educational rights and cultural support. Protecting a broader way of life may support a case for local or regional government.

In some circumstances, a stronger claim to independence may arise. A legitimate interest in continuity does not automatically establish the need for a separate state if other arrangements are sufficient.

The question is what degree of authority addresses the community’s concerns while respecting others affected.



## **Self-determination and political membership**

Protecting a distinct group’s self-determination may mean not giving other people citizenship or political membership within the same system.

Giving other groups voting rights can create competing power relationships. If another group grows into a majority, the original group may lose the capacity to govern its own future.

For this reason, self-determination needs to be connected to the group the political system is intended to protect.

The two principal groupings proposed here are distinct ancestral populations and the groups that built distinct societies.

Applied to these communities, self-determination can provide a more reliable means of preserving continuity over the long term.
