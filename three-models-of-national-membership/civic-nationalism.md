# Civic nationalism

Civic nationalism defines the nation through citizenship, political values, laws and institutions. Belonging comes from commitment to the state and its principles rather than ancestry or cultural background.

A civic nation is generally built around constitutional government, equal citizenship, rule of law, individual rights and participation in public life. People from different backgrounds can belong if they accept and support its common political framework.



## **Principal objective**

The goal is to build and maintain a unified political community based on:

* Shared citizenship.
* Equal rights.
* Loyalty to common laws.
* Loyalty to common institutions.

Anyone from any ancestral background or culture may become a member if they accept the country’s laws and civic values. Civic nationalism seeks unity by emphasising what citizens share politically rather than what distinguishes them culturally or ancestrally.



## **Inclusive membership**

Civic nationalism is inclusive in principle. People from different ancestral, racial, linguistic or religious backgrounds can become members by accepting the shared political framework.

It is therefore more open to immigrants, outsiders and minority groups than nationalism based principally on ancestry. However, an inclusive basis of membership does not necessarily require unrestricted immigration or easy naturalisation. Citizenship may remain highly conditional.



## **Flexible national identity**

Because membership is based on citizenship and political values, national identity and culture can change over time. New groups may influence public values, cultural life and understandings of national identity. Civic identity is also commonly combined with expectations involving language, history and public culture.



## **Citizenship**

Citizenship may be granted to people who accept the state’s laws, institutions and political values. Eligible people may include:

* People born in the country.
* Immigrants who naturalise.
* Long-term residents meeting legal requirements.

Membership is acquired through commitment, participation and legal recognition rather than inherited identity alone. Applicants may need to obey the law, understand government, support constitutional institutions and adopt shared political principles. Once citizenship is acquired, citizens normally enjoy equal status regardless of origin.



## **Voting**

Voting power is given equally to citizens who meet the applicable eligibility requirements. Their political status does not depend on ancestry or cultural background. The underlying principle is that citizens are equal members of the political community.



## **Representation and public office**

Representation is based on citizenship rather than ancestry. Citizens may become representatives if they meet requirements such as age, residence or electoral eligibility. Representatives derive legitimacy from the support of citizens, not membership of a particular ancestral group.



## **Summary of advantages and disadvantages**

The following summarises the advantages and disadvantages identified in this analysis. Detailed comparisons appear in the **Comparative evaluation and competing arguments** section.



**Advantages**

* **Adaptability in mixed societies** - Provides a shared political identity where no single ancestral or cultural identity dominates.
* **Simple membership principles** - Citizenship can be based on laws, institutions and civic responsibilities without requiring ancestry verification or a comprehensive assessment of cultural assimilation.
* **Comparatively inexpensive administration** - Standardised language and civic-knowledge tests can make naturalisation relatively predictable and cost-effective.
* **Global talent attraction** - Broadly inclusive membership and a path to equal citizenship can attract global talent seeking lasting belonging.
* **Migrant security and retention** - Secure residence and citizenship can encourage migrants to stay and invest, but that can also mean retaining those with lower economic contributions or limited integration.



**Disadvantages and risks**

* **Limited protection for ancestral continuity** - Open membership can reduce the historical population’s demographic share and over the long-term this could contribute to the loss of distinct populations and global diversity.
* **Cultural change and parallel societies** - Equal legal membership does not guarantee cultural assimilation. Communities may remain socially separate or gradually reshape the historical culture.
* **Demographic change and self-determination** - Once newcomers become citizens and voters, the historical population may find it harder to retain political control or reverse earlier membership decisions.
* **Unequal effects between countries** - States receiving substantial migration may experience greater changes to identity and culture than states receiving little migration.
* **Inherited-characteristic concerns** - Dysgenic effects on average population behaviours and intelligence could become a problem due to factors such as migration and intermixing.
* **Government incentives and strategic naturalisation** - Governments may reshape the electorate through admission policy, while applicants may seek citizenship mainly for passports, benefits, security or political influence.
* **Foreign influence and crisis resilience** - Transnational loyalties or weak cohesion may become more consequential during economic, diplomatic or security crises.
* **Weaker emotional attachment** - A nation defined mainly through institutions and political values may generate less attachment than one grounded in shared culture or ancestry.



## **Questions for evaluation**

The model raises questions about:

* How civic commitment can be assessed.
* Whether citizenship tests reliably establish integration.
* How institutions and shared public culture are sustained.
* How equal citizenship relates to historical-group continuity.
* Whether civic identity generates sufficient emotional attachment and solidarity.

The testing questions are considered in **Naturalisation and civic or cultural citizenship tests**. The wider trade-offs belong in **Comparative evaluation and competing arguments**.
