# Cultural nationalism

Cultural nationalism centres the nation on shared culture. This may include language, traditions, religion, customs, heritage, historical memory and social norms. It emphasises a common way of life rather than ancestry alone. People may belong if they adopt or participate in the dominant culture, even where they do not share the majority population’s descent.



## **Principal objective**

The goal is to preserve or strengthen the nation’s distinct culture. Cultural nationalists commonly regard shared culture as important for cohesion and historical continuity. They may therefore support:

* National-language protection.
* Preservation of historical sites.
* Traditional customs.
* Cultural education.
* Heritage institutions.
* Shared symbols and historical memory.



## **Conditional inclusion**

Cultural nationalism is conditionally inclusive. People from different ancestral, racial, linguistic or religious backgrounds may become members if they adopt or participate in the national culture.

This commonly involves learning the language, accepting public customs, identifying with national traditions and supporting the political framework. It is more open than ancestral nationalism because belonging may be acquired through assimilation or integration. It is less open than civic nationalism because more substantial cultural adaptation is expected.



## **Different levels of cultural expectation**

Requirements vary. A relatively open version may require language learning and respect for constitutional principles. A more demanding version may expect newcomers to abandon much of their previous cultural identity. A central question is how much adaptation is required and how much private cultural difference remains protected.



## **Partially flexible identity**

Cultural nationalism values continuity. New members are generally expected to fit into the established framework rather than redefine it radically. Nevertheless, newcomers may gradually influence culture, and the existing population’s culture can also change. The model therefore provides a partially flexible identity rather than a completely fixed one.



## **Citizenship**

Citizenship may be available to people who adopt the national language, traditions and culture. Eligibility may depend on:

* Language proficiency.
* Knowledge of history and customs.
* Participation in social life.
* Acceptance of central institutions.
* Evidence of assimilation or integration.



## **Voting**

Once accepted as citizens, people generally receive equal political rights. Cultural nationalism does not necessarily require ancestry-based distinctions in voting. However, it may attach importance to continuing cultural participation and national identification.



## **Representation and public office**

Citizens may become representatives regardless of ancestry. Political leaders may be expected to support and promote the national language, traditions and cultural institutions. They may be judged partly on how well they defend the nation’s cultural identity.



## **Summary of advantages and disadvantages**

The following summarises the advantages and disadvantages identified in this analysis. Detailed comparisons appear in the **Comparative evaluation and competing arguments** section.



**Advantages**

* **Stronger cultural preservation** - Explicit attention to language, traditions, historical memory and shared practices offers greater protection for inherited culture than civic membership alone.
* **Cohesion and solidarity** - Where assimilation succeeds, shared culture can strengthen trust, belonging, mutual obligation and willingness to contribute to common institutions.
* **Moderately manageable citizenship administration** - Cultural assessments require more judgement than civic tests but are presented as generally less demanding than ancestry-verification systems.
* **Global talent attraction** - Membership through cultural integration rather than ancestry can attract global talent, although assimilation requirements may deter some.
* **Migrant security and retention** - Secure membership can encourage migrants to integrate and settle, but that can also mean retaining those with lower economic contributions or limited integration.



**Disadvantages and risks**

* **No guarantee of ancestral continuity** - People from different backgrounds can become members, so the historical population’s demographic position can change even where cultural adaptation succeeds.
* **Incomplete cultural preservation** - Assimilation is not necessarily complete or one-directional; newcomers can reshape the culture they join, especially at higher migration levels.
* **Difficult assessment and enforcement** - Knowledge tests do not prove genuine assimilation. Monitoring beliefs and everyday behaviour can become subjective, intrusive or administratively burdensome.
* **Cultural rigidity and gatekeeping** - Preservation can become resistance to beneficial change or a dispute over who defines authentic national culture.
* **Demographic change and political control** - New citizens gain influence over future cultural standards, migration policy and national direction, potentially weakening the historical population’s self-determination.
* **Unequal international effects and diversity loss** - Countries receiving larger inflows bear greater integration pressures, while historical cultures and identities may still diminish over time.
* **Inherited-characteristic concerns** - Dysgenic effects on average population behaviours and intelligence could become a problem due to factors such as migration and intermixing.
* **Political manipulation, foreign ties and crises** - Governments may alter cultural standards for political purposes, while assimilation may not eliminate external loyalties or tensions exposed during crises.



## **Questions for evaluation**

The model raises questions about:

* What constitutes the national culture.
* Who determines the required cultural standards.
* Whether assimilation can be measured reliably.
* Whether knowledge, participation and genuine identification can be distinguished.
* How culture can be preserved without excessive surveillance or coercion.
* Whether cultural membership preserves the historical ancestral population.

The testing questions are considered in **Naturalisation and civic or cultural citizenship tests**. The broader trade-offs belong in **Comparative evaluation and competing arguments**.
