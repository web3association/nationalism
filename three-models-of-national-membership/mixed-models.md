# Mixed models

A nation-state does not have to adopt a wholly civic, cultural or ancestral approach. It can combine different membership principles, cultural expectations and territorial arrangements. Political membership, immigration levels, residence rights and cultural policies are related but separate choices.



## **Combining membership and immigration policies**

One country may understand itself primarily as an ancestral and cultural community, grant citizenship mainly by descent and allow little discretionary immigration. Another may define membership civically, offer naturalisation and accept moderate or high immigration. Other combinations include:

* Low immigration with equal civic membership for those admitted.
* Significant temporary migration with restrictive naturalisation.
* Civic citizenship with demanding language and cultural expectations.
* Descent-based membership alongside residence opportunities for outsiders.

There is no single immigration level or national-identity model that every country is morally required to adopt. Different populations may prioritise continuity, openness, pluralism, economic development, mobility or a particular pace of change.



## **Individual rights within collective membership**

Collective political membership does not require every policy to be collective-leaning. An ancestral nationalist society could still use universal laws, protect personal freedoms and adopt individual-leaning policies. Likewise, a civic system could recognise collective interests in language, cultural institutions, indigenous land or minority protection. The important question is how these principles interact when they conflict.



## **Ancestral arrangements within civic states**

A civic nationalist state could allow forms of ancestral or cultural self-organisation at local and regional levels. Possibilities include:

* Local communities.
* Protected communities.
* Autonomous regions.
* Federal arrangements.
* Other recognised forms of self-government.

These could allow historical groups to protect identity and culture without requiring the entire state to adopt ancestral membership. A group may pursue self-determination at several levels rather than rely on one national solution. This could be especially important where the historical population believes national politics no longer protects its preferred way of life.



## **Civic arrangements within ancestral states**

An ancestral nationalist state could allow more civic approaches within particular territories or institutions. Examples include:

* Local communities with broader resident participation.
* Special economic zones.
* Charter cities.
* Special administrative regions.
* Autonomous or federal regions choosing more open arrangements.

Such areas could attract global talent, test institutions or support new developments. External participants might receive political roles within a particular project without gaining equivalent authority throughout the national system. The historical population could retain control over broader settlement or membership questions while allowing more open local participation.



## **New societies and historical nation-states**

Civic nationalism may be particularly useful where new societies are being created. A new state may not have a deeply rooted historical identity or culture to preserve in the same way as an older nation. Its founding population may already have ancestral homelands elsewhere and may accept a more fluid identity from the beginning.

From this perspective, ancestral nationalism is more compelling for preserving historical populations and cultures, while civic and cultural approaches may be more suitable for new societies or experiments.

The proposed combination is to protect existing historical continuity while creating room for innovation and new ways of living together.



## **Preferences can differ within the same ancestry**

Members of an ancestral population need not agree on one model. Some may prefer openness and cultural mixing. Others may place more value on continuity and rootedness.

A single national arrangement may therefore fail to accommodate the full range of preferences. Local and regional autonomy can provide room for different approaches within a wider political framework.

The aim is not necessarily for one model to replace every other model, but for communities to compare arrangements and choose those that suit their priorities.



## **Public authority and voluntary association**

Private association differs from territorial government. The more an arrangement affects existing residents, essential services or political rights, the stronger the need for public accountability and protections against unreasonable exclusion.

Local autonomy also requires ways for members to challenge decisions and seek reform, not only the option to leave. Different arrangements therefore need clear boundaries between private membership rules and public governing powers.



## **Territorial options and practical implementation**

Mixed models can operate through several territorial forms, but these should not be treated as interchangeable. Local communities, economic zones, charter cities, autonomous regions, federal units, freely associated states and independent states provide different powers. Their legal basis, available resources and relationship with wider government also differ.
