# Civic, cultural and ancestral nationalism: an overview

Three distinct forms of nationalism are used throughout this analysis: civic nationalism, cultural nationalism and ancestral nationalism. Each defines the nation differently and prioritises a different form of continuity.



## **Civic nationalism**

Civic nationalism defines the nation through shared citizenship, laws, political institutions, civic values and participation in a common public life. It prioritises civic-state continuity, seeking to preserve:

* Sovereignty.
* Institutions.
* The legal order.
* Civic unity.
* Equal citizenship.

Belonging to the nation comes from a person’s commitment to the state and its principles rather than their ancestral or cultural background.

A civic nation is usually built around constitutional government, equal citizenship, rule of law, individual rights and participation in public life. People from different backgrounds can belong to the same civic nation if they accept and support its shared political framework.

Civic nationalism is inclusive in principle. Its national identity and culture can be flexible because people from different backgrounds may join the political community and influence its public life.



## **Cultural nationalism**

Cultural nationalism defines the nation through a shared culture, including language, customs, traditions, beliefs, social norms, historical memory and a way of life.

It prioritises cultural continuity, seeking to preserve and transmit the nation’s culture across generations. Education, assimilation and common cultural practices can support that objective.

The nation is understood as a cultural community. People may be considered part of it if they adopt or participate in its dominant culture, even when they do not share the majority population’s ancestry.

Cultural nationalism is conditionally inclusive. Outsiders may gain belonging through cultural assimilation or integration, but cultural adaptation is expected.

Its national identity is partially flexible. New members are generally expected to fit into the existing cultural framework rather than radically redefine it, although they may still gradually influence the culture.



## **Ancestral nationalism**

Ancestral nationalism defines the nation through continuity with a distinct historical people, including shared ancestry and lineage.

It prioritises people continuity, seeking to preserve the long-established ancestral population associated with the nation. This is often understood as the basis for preserving its culture and political inheritance as well.

Membership is treated as something a person is born into rather than something they can fully acquire through legal citizenship, cultural adoption or political loyalty.

Ancestral nationalism is exclusive in principle. People without the required ancestry may be allowed to live and work in the country, but they may not be recognised as full members of the nation. It places a strong emphasis on continuity, protecting the interests, identity and continued existence of the ancestral group.



## **Multiracial and multicultural societies**

Nationalism does not necessarily require a nation-state to be ancestrally homogeneous or monocultural. Each nation-state may adopt different rules for membership, residence and cultural expectations.

**Civic nationalism** can be multiracial and multicultural in principle because membership is based on citizenship and shared political institutions rather than ancestry. It may nevertheless require integration into a common civic culture.

**Cultural nationalism** can be multiracial if people of different ancestries adopt the national culture. It is often not multicultural because it commonly gives priority to one national culture.

**Ancestral nationalism** may tolerate cultural or racial diversity within the nation-state, but it defines full national membership through descent from the historic national population. Non-ancestral groups may be accepted as residents without being regarded as full members of the nation.



## **Related but distinct policy choices**

Immigration levels, citizenship rules, national identity and integration policies should be considered as separate dimensions rather than points on a single political spectrum.

A country can have low immigration while making citizenship accessible to the relatively small number of migrants it admits. Another can rely heavily on temporary migrant workers while rarely granting citizenship. A third can combine high permanent immigration with a demanding naturalisation process.

The three forms of nationalism provide different answers to who belongs to the nation. They do not, by themselves, settle every question about who may enter, how long someone may reside or what rights residents receive.
