# Related forms of nationalism and terminology

Civic, cultural and ancestral nationalism are not the only forms of nationalism.

Civic nationalism is one of the main forms associated with prioritising civic-state continuity.

Cultural nationalism, together with related or overlapping variants such as religious and linguistic nationalism, is most closely associated with cultural continuity.

Ancestral nationalism is one of several forms associated with the continuity of a distinct people. Other variants include nativist nationalism, racial nationalism, blood nationalism and blood-and-soil nationalism.

These people-continuity approaches define membership differently and allow different levels of inclusiveness.



## **Ethno-nationalism and ethnic nationalism**

The word _ethnos_ is derived from Greek and refers to a people understood as a cultural-historical community. In modern discussion, it is often used interchangeably with _ethnic_, although the two are not always identical in meaning.

_Ethnos_ generally refers to a community bound together by shared identity, culture, language, history, traditions and often a sense of common ancestry. It expresses the idea of peoplehood: a distinct cultural group that may be understood separately from a political nation-state.

In ancient Greek usage, however, _ethnos_ did not always refer to a strictly defined cultural group. Its meanings were broader and could include a “band”, “tribe”, “caste”, “crowd” or simply a number of people accustomed to living together.

The term _ethnic_ is also broad and has been defined in several ways:

* “Related or belonging to a group of people who can be seen as distinct because they have a shared culture, tradition, language, history, etc.” - [**Cambridge Dictionary**](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/ethnic)
* “Of or relating to large groups of people classed according to common racial, national, tribal, religious, linguistic, or cultural origin or background” - [**Merriam-Webster Dictionary**](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/ethnic)
* “Means connected with or related to different racial or cultural groups of people” - [**Collins Dictionary**](https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/ethnic)

Both _ethnos_ and _ethnic_ tend to combine ancestry and cultural aspects into a single concept. Depending on their interpretation, they may also allow someone outside the group to join the nation through sufficient cultural assimilation.

Because culture and ancestry are distinct aspects of group identity, it can be more useful to treat them separately rather than merge them under these broader labels.

For this reason, **cultural nationalism** and **ancestral nationalism** are used here as distinct terms. Separating them makes it clearer whether national membership is defined through assimilation into a culture or descent from a historic population.

A cultural nationalist is primarily concerned with preserving the nation’s culture, traditions and way of life. An ancestral nationalist is primarily concerned with preserving the continuity of its ancestral group.

An ancestral nationalist may also care deeply about cultural preservation, but ancestral nationalism is the more explicit term when descent principally defines the nation.



## **Nativist nationalism**

Nativist nationalism prioritises the interests and political influence of people considered native to a country over immigrants, outsiders or newer arrivals.

It is concerned with protecting an established national community from rapid external change, especially demographic, cultural or political change caused by immigration.

Nativism varies significantly in how it defines membership and who counts as native.



**Territorial forms**

In a territorial sense, someone born and raised in a country may be described as native even if their ancestors came from elsewhere.

Under _jus soli_, a person acquires citizenship through birth within the state’s territory. Jus soli is not inherently nativist; it is a territorial citizenship rule commonly associated with civic membership.

It may nevertheless operate within a mildly nativist system if native-born citizens are distinguished from foreign-born immigrants or receive some preference.

Whether the locally born children of immigrants count as native depends on how the particular movement defines the term.



**Multigenerational forms**

A stricter form may require a person’s family to have lived in the country for several generations.

For example, a movement might recognise native status only where a family has lived in the country for fifty or one hundred years, or for three or more generations.

This can help preserve the ancestral population because it makes full national membership harder to acquire. However, it can also create a pathway through which newer groups eventually become the dominant part of the population.



**Descent-based forms**

The strictest form overlaps more clearly with ancestral nationalism. Native status is tied to descent from the founding or long-established peoples of the nation, rather than birth or several generations of residence alone.

Nativist nationalism is therefore a flexible and broad category. It can describe approaches intended to limit or slow demographic change while allowing some population change over time.

Where the aim is specifically to preserve historical demographic continuity, narrower forms of people-based nationalism are likely to be more suitable.



## **Racial nationalism**

Racial nationalism defines national belonging through membership in a racial group. Instead of defining the nation through citizenship, culture, language or descent from a specific ancestral population, it uses a broader racial category.

Compared with blood nationalism, racial nationalism can be more inclusive within its chosen category. A racial nationalist who defines the nation in terms of whiteness might include English, French, German, Dutch or Scandinavian people within the same grouping.

In that sense, racial nationalism can include multiple ethnic or ancestral groups that would remain distinct under narrower forms of blood or ancestral nationalism.

However, this broad categorisation is less precise if the goal is to preserve a particular historical people. A racial category can include many distinct peoples with different languages, cultures, histories and ancestral identities.

Racial nationalism is therefore better understood as a form of broad racial-group continuity rather than an effective solution for preserving a distinct national ancestral group. It may preserve a wider biological or racial category without specifically preserving each distinct people within it. If the focus is a particular historic nation or founding population, ancestral nationalism or forms of blood nationalism would be more suitable.



## **Blood nationalism**

Blood nationalism defines national membership primarily through shared blood and genealogical or biological descent. A person belongs because they are understood to share a common biological background with the nation’s people. A nation may contain multiple related bloodlines, but membership remains inherited and rooted in biological descent.

Ancestral nationalism overlaps with blood nationalism because both are descent-based. However, ancestral nationalism can be applied more broadly. It may focus on descent from the founding or long-established peoples of a nation even when those peoples did not share a single biological bloodline. If several distinct groups participated in a nation’s formation, their descendants could all be included.

Blood nationalism creates a narrower membership boundary when it requires a stronger degree of common biological descent. Ancestral nationalism may be more pragmatic where individuals or minority groups have had ancestral ties to a nation for centuries without sharing the majority’s primary bloodline. Such groups may be deeply integrated into the historical population and have no meaningful connection to another nation over an extended period. This permits a broader understanding of inherited national belonging than strict blood-based nationalism.

If the main priority is descent from the founding or long-established peoples, **ancestral nationalism** is usually the more suitable term. If the priority is preserving a distinct biological group defined by common blood, **blood nationalism** is more precise.



## **Blood-and-soil nationalism**

Blood-and-soil nationalism combines inherited descent with a strong connection to a particular homeland. The “blood” element refers to shared blood and biological descent. The “soil” element refers to rootedness in a specific land, territory or ancestral homeland.

Blood nationalism can be geographically portable. A person may be considered part of the nation because of descent even if they live abroad or were raised elsewhere.

Blood-and-soil nationalism is stricter because it also stresses rootedness in the homeland itself. A blood-and-soil nationalist may argue that full national belonging requires both the correct descent and a formative connection to the ancestral land, its culture and its historical community. This can make it more restrictive toward both immigrants without the relevant ancestry and diaspora members who have the ancestry but lack a lived connection to the homeland.

Ancestral nationalism does not necessarily require an organic bond between bloodline and land in the same way. Its primary concern is continuity with the nation’s founding or long-established ancestral population, although it can also place a similar importance on territorial and cultural rootedness.

Blood-and-soil nationalism begins with bloodline and adds land-rootedness. Ancestral nationalism begins with continuity with the historic people and may incorporate land, culture and upbringing as additional factors. This makes ancestral nationalism the broader and more flexible form that may apply additional factors on top, while blood-and-soil nationalism is the stricter and more exclusionary approach.



## **Why this analysis uses ancestral nationalism**

This analysis uses ancestral nationalism because it describes the aim of preserving a distinct historical people without requiring the stricter biological boundaries of blood nationalism. It can be used to recognise multiple founding populations and long-settled minorities, making it more flexible when addressing mixed ancestry which will be especially important during any initial transitions over to ancestral nationalist solutions. Ancestral nationalism can be seen as a pragmatic solution to help with ensuring existing citizens don’t face the risk of statelessness. It is therefore used as the principal people-continuity model for comparison with civic and cultural nationalism. Other forms of nationalism that prioritise the preservation of a distinct people may still be preferred or adopted in the future.
