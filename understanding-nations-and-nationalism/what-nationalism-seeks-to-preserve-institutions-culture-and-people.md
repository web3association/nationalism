# What nationalism seeks to preserve: institutions, culture and people

What nationalism seeks to preserve depends on how the nation is defined. Different forms of nationalism prioritise different kinds of continuity. These can be grouped into civic-state continuity, cultural continuity and people continuity.



## **Civic-state continuity**

Civic-state continuity prioritises preserving:

* State sovereignty.
* Shared institutions.
* Laws and citizenship.
* Civic values.
* Constitutional order.
* Systems of governance.
* Political identity.

In this approach, the nation’s continuity is principally understood through the persistence of its political institutions and shared civic life.



## **Cultural continuity**

Cultural continuity prioritises preserving:

* Language.
* Customs and traditions.
* Beliefs.
* Historical memory.
* Social norms.
* Public culture.
* A shared way of life.

This approach is concerned with whether the nation’s culture continues to be practised and passed between generations.



## **People continuity**

People continuity prioritises preserving the nation as a distinct historical people. This includes:

* Its long-established ancestral population.
* The descendants of that population.
* Its inherited group identity.

This approach is concerned not only with whether institutions or cultural practices continue, but also with whether the historical population associated with them continues as a distinct group.



## **Combining these priorities**

These priorities can be combined. Some nationalists argue that preserving the continuity of a distinct people is also the best way to preserve that people’s culture and civic-state. In this view, demographic continuity supports cultural and civic-state continuity. Others place greater emphasis on cultural continuity. They argue that a nation can endure even if its population changes, provided newcomers adopt its language, customs, values and way of life.

Civic nationalists may instead see civic-state continuity as the core requirement. From this perspective, the nation-state persists as long as its laws, institutions, citizenship and shared political values remain intact.

In this sense, civic-state continuity can be seen as the most minimal form of preservation, cultural continuity as a broader form, and people continuity as the most encompassing. People continuity aims to preserve not only the civic-state and culture, but also the historical population associated with them.



## **The limits of preservation**

Certain nationalist systems may be designed to promote particular forms of civic, cultural or demographic continuity, but no political system can prevent all forms of change. Even a highly homogeneous and self-governing population may substantially alter its:

* Institutions.
* Political values.
* Language.
* Religion.
* Cultural traditions.
* Way of life.

Migration, emigration and intermarriage may also change its demographic composition.

Culture is inherently one of the most difficult things to preserve because it commonly evolves with changing preferences, environments and technology.

Civic-states can be easier to preserve when their institutions function as intended and their political values are easy to adopt and agree with.

The level of preservation of a nation’s distinct population can vary more drastically across different forms of nationalism.



## **Choosing what matters most**

Each nation needs to decide which aspects of its identity it wants to preserve. Those priorities help determine which form of nationalism, or combination of forms, is most suitable.

A political system may prioritise institutional continuity, cultural continuity, people continuity or a combination of all three. The important distinction is between what the system seeks to preserve and how reliably its membership rules and institutions can achieve that outcome.
